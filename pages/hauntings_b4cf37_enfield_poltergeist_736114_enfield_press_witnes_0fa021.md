## Introduction

Doug Bence and Graham Morris occupy an unusual place in the [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) Poltergeist debate because they were neither family members nor paranormal investigators when they first entered the story. They arrived as journalists from the *Daily Mirror*, responding to what initially appeared to be a local human-interest story. Their accounts became influential because they describe apparently spontaneous disturbances witnessed before the case had acquired its later mythology. At the same time, both men's recollections illustrate why press testimony is difficult to evaluate: they reported striking events, yet neither became an uncritical advocate for every later claim associated with the case. Their evidence therefore sits at the intersection of eyewitness testimony, journalism and sceptical scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. ## Why Were the Journalists Called to the House?

The reporters were not investigating a famous haunting. In early September 1977, neighbours and family members were already reporting unexplained noises, [moving objects]({{ 'moving-objects/' | relative_url }}) and disturbances inside the Hodgson home. After unsuccessful attempts to obtain help from official channels, a neighbour contacted the *Daily Mirror* in the hope that publicity might bring assistance or attention to the family's situation. Reporter Doug Bence and photographer Graham Morris were dispatched to the house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Douglas Bence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. News of the World, 2 April...Read more...</span></span></span>

This timing matters. Their visit occurred during the early phase of the case, before the prolonged investigations by Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair became the dominant narrative. Supporters of the Enfield case often point to Bence and Morris as relatively independent witnesses because they arrived as outsiders with a newspaper assignment rather than a pre-existing commitment to paranormal explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. According to later recollections by Bence, the journalists initially saw little that seemed extraordinary. The situation changed when family members gathered in a room and activity allegedly began around them. ## What Did They Report Seeing?

The most famous element of the journalists' testimony concerns flying Lego bricks and other small objects. Multiple later accounts, including Bence's own recollections and summaries in [psychical-research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}) sources, state that objects appeared to move through rooms without an obvious visible cause. Photographer Graham Morris reported being struck on the forehead by a Lego piece travelling with enough force to leave a noticeable mark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Mirror]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. Morris consistently described the event as more than a simple tossed toy. In later interviews, he recalled standing in the kitchen while members of the family were visible and said he did not observe anyone throwing the objects that struck him. He described Lego bricks and marbles moving around the room while he watched.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horrifiedmagazine.co.uk/horrified-stagin/the-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horrifiedmagazine.co.uk">[Horrified Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horrifiedmagazine.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horrified MagazineThe Enfield Poltergeist30 Aug 2023 — The Mirror sent reporter Douglas Bence and photographer Graham Morris. ## Why Do Believers Consider Their Testimony Important?

For supporters of the Enfield case, the value of Bence and Morris lies less in what they concluded than in when they witnessed it. Their observations occurred before the case became internationally famous and before extensive paranormal-[investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) teams became involved. This reduces, though does not eliminate, concerns that they were influenced by years of publicity or elaborate legend-building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. Another frequently cited point is that the journalists had professional incentives to identify ordinary explanations. Reporters are trained to look for sources, causes and inconsistencies. Believers argue that Morris's account of being struck by a Lego brick while observing the room carries weight precisely because he was there to document events, not to validate paranormal claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horrifiedmagazine.co.uk/horrified-stagin/the-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horrifiedmagazine.co.uk">[Horrified Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horrifiedmagazine.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horrified MagazineThe Enfield Poltergeist30 Aug 2023 — The Mirror sent reporter Douglas Bence and photographer Graham Morris. ## How Does Press Involvement Complicate Reliability?

The same facts that make the journalists interesting witnesses also create methodological problems.

First, neither Bence nor Morris was conducting controlled observation. They were reporters in a crowded domestic environment. Their role was to gather information and photographs, not to secure a laboratory-style record of events. As a result, their testimony depends heavily on memory and personal interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. Second, newspaper reporting inevitably introduced publicity into the case. Once national coverage began, the Hodgson family received visitors, investigators and growing public attention. Critics have long argued that publicity could encourage exaggeration, performance or selective reporting. Even some psychical researchers who examined Enfield later expressed concern that parts of the case may have been staged for visitors and journalists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anita_Gregory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anita Gregory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anita Gregory</span></span></span>

Third, Bence himself never adopted an unquestioning position. In later [reflections]({{ 'reflections/' | relative_url }}) he emphasised that unusual events occurred but rejected the idea that every famous image or claim should be accepted at face value. He has stated that happenings at Green Street were real and puzzling, while also cautioning against more dramatic retellings that expanded beyond what he personally witnessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.douglasbence.org.uk/journalism/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: douglasbence.org.uk">[Douglas Bence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">douglasbence.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Douglas BenceEnfield PoltergeistGraham was hit by a Lego brick and his word has never been doubted. ## What Do Their Claims Mean for the Evidence Debate?

The testimony of Doug Bence and Graham Morris remains significant because it occupies a middle ground rarely found in haunting cases. Their accounts are stronger than anonymous rumours or second-hand stories, but weaker than controlled scientific evidence.

Supporters of the Enfield Poltergeist point to the journalists as independent [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }}) who encountered apparent object movements before the case became a cultural phenomenon. Sceptics counter that even sincere reporters can misinterpret fast-moving events in a chaotic environment, particularly when children, darkness, surprise and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) are involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Triablogue]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26. What makes their testimony enduring is not that it settles the case, but that it resists easy dismissal. Morris's account of being struck by a Lego brick and Bence's corroborating recollections provide one of the better-known examples of apparently spontaneous activity witnessed by outsiders. Yet those same accounts remain dependent on human observation rather than decisive physical proof. In the broader Enfield evidence debate, the press witnesses therefore function less as a final answer and more as a reminder of why the case remains contested nearly half a century later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Douglas Bence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Daily Mirror, 10 September (1977). 26.
