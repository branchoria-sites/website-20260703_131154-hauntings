## Introduction

Many of the most convincing “ghost figures” in haunting photographs are created by a simple photographic [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) rather than by anything paranormal. In dim locations such as old houses, castles, churches, cemeteries and allegedly haunted buildings, cameras often struggle to gather enough light. To compensate, they keep the shutter open longer. If a person moves during that exposure, the camera records them across multiple positions instead of freezing them in one place. The result can be a stretched, faded or semi-transparent figure that looks remarkably like an apparition. Photography specialists have long recognised this as motion blur or long-exposure ghosting, and the same effect is routinely used by photographers who deliberately create ghost-like images for artistic purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canon.ge/get-inspired/tips-and-techniques/capturing-motion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canon.ge">[Canon Georgia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canon.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">iaAll you need to know about motion photographyJune 5, 2019 — Slow shutter speeds and long exposures enable you to create motion blur...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2019</span></span></span>

## How Low Light Leads to Slow Shutter Speeds

A camera records an image while its shutter remains open. In bright daylight this may happen so quickly that movement is effectively frozen. In dark environments, however, the camera often needs a longer exposure to collect sufficient light. The longer the shutter remains open, the more opportunity there is for movement to be recorded as blur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canon.ge/get-inspired/tips-and-techniques/capturing-motion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canon.ge">[Canon Georgia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canon.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">iaAll you need to know about motion photographyJune 5, 2019 — Slow shutter speeds and long exposures enable you to create motion blur...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2019</span></span></span>

This is particularly common in haunting photographs because many are taken:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* At night or in poorly lit interiors.
* Without professional lighting equipment.
* In locations where flash is restricted or ineffective.
* Using automatic camera settings that prioritise brightness over motion freezing.

</div>

Under these conditions, a person walking through a corridor, climbing stairs or turning their head may not appear as a sharply defined individual. Instead, their image becomes smeared across the sensor during the exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canon.ge/get-inspired/tips-and-techniques/capturing-motion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canon.ge">[Canon Georgia+2PastPaperHero]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canon.ge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">iaAll you need to know about motion photographyJune 5, 2019 — Slow shutter speeds and long exposures enable you to create motion blur...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2019</span></span></span>

Even slight camera movement can contribute. A photographer holding a camera in a dark room may unintentionally shift position while the shutter is open, creating additional streaking and distortion that can make ordinary subjects appear strangely elongated or detached from their surroundings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_stabilization" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Image stabilization</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Image stabilization</span></span></span>

## Why Moving People Can Look Transparent

The most striking “ghost” photographs often involve people who appear partially visible, translucent or only partly formed. This effect has a straightforward explanation rooted in how exposure records time.

If a person remains still for only part of a long exposure and then moves away, the stationary background continues to be recorded after the person has left the frame. The background effectively accumulates more exposure than the moving subject. As a result, the subject appears faint or transparent while the surroundings remain solid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theschoolofphotography.com/tutorials/ghost-photography-tutorial" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theschoolofphotography.com">[theschoolofphotography.com+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theschoolofphotography.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghost photography tutorial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Create Ghost Effects In-Camera25 Oct 2025 — Photography of ghosts is a long-exposure photography technique that records movement over tim...</span></span></span>

Photographers intentionally exploit this effect when creating artistic “ghost” images. Long-exposure tutorials routinely demonstrate how a person can be made to appear spectral simply by standing in a scene briefly and then moving away before the exposure finishes. The resulting figure looks remarkably similar to many alleged [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}). A related effect occurs when flash is used together with a slow shutter speed. The flash briefly freezes a subject, producing a sharp image, while the remainder of the exposure records movement before or after the flash. This can create a doubled appearance consisting of a solid figure accompanied by a blurred, transparent echo. To someone unfamiliar with photographic techniques, the result can look like a spirit separating from a body or an apparition standing beside a living person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.documentyourdaytoday.com/week-36-slow-shutter-speed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documentyourdaytoday.com">[Document Your Day to Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documentyourdaytoday.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">This ghosting happens with slow shutter speed photos using flash too,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document Your Day to DayPhotography Challenge - Week 36 - Slow Shutter SpeedSeptember 2, 2022 — If someone moves during a slow shutter sp...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 2, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why Apparitions Often Look Incomplete

Motion blur rarely produces a complete transparent human figure. Instead, it tends to create exactly the kinds of anomalies frequently reported in ghost photographs:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A torso without clear legs.
* A pale face with an indistinct body.
* A figure fading into a wall or doorway.
* Multiple overlapping outlines.
* A person appearing only from the waist up.
* Streaks that vaguely resemble robes or flowing clothing.

</div>

These odd shapes occur because different parts of the body move at different speeds during an exposure. Arms swing, heads turn and legs move independently. The camera records all of these positions together, creating a composite image that never existed as a single real-world moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_blur_%28media%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Motion blur (media</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Motion blur (media</span></span></span>

This can be especially misleading in historic buildings where viewers already expect unusual experiences. A blurred visitor on a staircase may be interpreted as a Victorian ghost, while the same blur in a shopping centre would likely be recognised as a photographic artefact.

## Clues That Separate Blur from a Figure

Investigators can often distinguish motion blur from a genuinely unexplained image by examining several visual clues.

### Soft Edges and Directional Smearing

Motion blur usually follows a direction. A moving subject may appear stretched horizontally, vertically or diagonally across the image. Apparitions attributed to blur often have fuzzy edges rather than clear boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_blur_%28media%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Motion blur (media</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Motion blur (media</span></span></span>

### Consistency with Human Movement

Many ghost-like photographs show figures positioned exactly where a living person could have been walking moments earlier. When the apparent figure follows a natural path through the scene, ordinary movement becomes a strong explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-exposure_photography" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long-exposure photography</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long-exposure photography</span></span></span>

### Exposure and Metadata

Modern image files frequently contain exposure information. A shutter speed of one second, two seconds or longer immediately increases the likelihood that movement contributed to the image. Investigators who review metadata can often identify whether the camera settings were suitable for producing blur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shutter_speed" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shutter speed</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shutter speed</span></span></span>

### Reproducibility

Perhaps the strongest clue is that the effect can be recreated easily. Photographers routinely generate transparent figures using nothing more than a tripod, a slow shutter speed and a moving subject. If an alleged apparition can be reproduced under controlled conditions, that weakens the claim that the original image requires a paranormal explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theschoolofphotography.com/tutorials/ghost-photography-tutorial" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theschoolofphotography.com">[theschoolofphotography.com+2Digital Photography School]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theschoolofphotography.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghost photography tutorial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Create Ghost Effects In-Camera25 Oct 2025 — Photography of ghosts is a long-exposure photography technique that records movement over tim...</span></span></span>

## Why These Images Remain Convincing

Motion-blurred figures are persuasive because they resemble cultural expectations of ghosts. Popular imagery often portrays spirits as pale, translucent and only partly visible. Long-exposure photography naturally produces those same characteristics. The camera is not recording an invisible entity; it is compressing movement across time into a single frame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-exposure_photography" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long-exposure photography</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long-exposure photography</span></span></span>

This overlap between photographic artefacts and popular ghost imagery helps explain why transparent apparitions continue to appear in haunting photographs.
