## Introduction

Ghost stories are often woven into [local folklore]({{ 'local-folklore/' | relative_url }}), family memory and the atmosphere of [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}). Yet when a haunting is accompanied by headaches, dizziness, confusion, unusual fatigue or a sense of dread, one of the first questions should be whether the building itself is making people ill. Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning has repeatedly been identified as a cause of experiences that witnesses initially interpreted as paranormal. Because the gas is colourless, odourless and difficult to detect without proper equipment, people may attribute strange sensations, voices, apparitions or feelings of presence to ghosts when they are actually experiencing the neurological effects of poisoning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC+2CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics12 Jan 2026 — The most common symptoms of CO poisoning are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomac...</span></span></span>

This does not mean every haunting claim has a medical explanation, nor does it erase the cultural importance of ghost stories. It does mean that [safety checks]({{ 'safety-checks/' | relative_url }}) should come before supernatural conclusions, especially in older houses with fuel-burning appliances, chimneys or poor ventilation.

## Why Old Furnaces and Poor Ventilation Matter

Many haunting legends are attached to old houses, and older buildings can contain conditions that increase carbon monoxide risk. Furnaces, boilers, [water heaters]({{ 'water-heater/' | relative_url }}), fireplaces, wood-burning stoves and other fuel-burning appliances can produce CO when combustion is incomplete. If vents are blocked, chimneys deteriorate, flues fail or ventilation is poor, the gas can accumulate indoors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/15663-carbon-monoxide-poisoning" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: my.clevelandclinic.org">[Cleveland Clinic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">my.clevelandclinic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">15663 carbon monoxide poisoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cleveland ClinicCarbon Monoxide Poisoning Symptoms20 Jul 2023 — Carbon monoxide poisoning occurs when you breathe in carbon monoxide fume...</span></span></span>

This overlap is significant because the very characteristics that make a building feel haunted—age, creaking infrastructure, disuse, enclosed rooms and neglected maintenance—can also create environmental hazards. A house may gain a reputation for strange experiences while the real underlying problem remains undetected.

Public health agencies consistently emphasise that CO cannot be identified by smell or sight. People often discover exposure only after symptoms develop or a detector sounds an alarm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carbon monoxide poisoningSymptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning · a headache · dizziness · feeling sick or being sick · feeling weak · tir...</span></span></span>

In folklore-rich settings, this invisibility can encourage supernatural interpretations. Residents may hear unexplained noises, feel watched, wake during the night in a confused state or experience unusual anxiety. Without knowledge of an environmental hazard, those experiences may naturally be fitted into existing ghost narratives.

## The Cases That Shaped the Carbon Monoxide Theory

One of the most frequently cited examples comes from a 1921 medical report by ophthalmologist W. H. Wilmer. Members of a family living in an old house reported frightening experiences, including [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}) and other disturbances. [Investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) eventually revealed carbon monoxide exposure, providing a physiological explanation for the experiences. The case became one of the classic examples used in later discussions of hauntings and environmental causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002939421908250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eyeby WH Wilmer · 1921 · Cited by 29 — It gives an account of the ocular symptoms of such...</span></span></span>

Modern accounts continue to appear. Reviews of environmental explanations for hauntings have documented incidents in which residents reported ghostly encounters before investigators discovered improperly installed heaters or other sources of carbon monoxide. One reported case involved a woman who believed she had seen a ghost, only for investigators to identify dangerous CO levels from a faulty water-heating installation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7304295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">carbon monoxide poisoning in...Read more...</span></span></span>

These examples are memorable not because they prove that all hauntings are caused by gas exposure, but because they demonstrate how readily poisoning symptoms can be mistaken for paranormal events.

## How to Separate Safety Checks from Folklore

A practical investigation does not require rejecting local traditions or dismissing witnesses. Instead, it begins by asking whether any environmental factor could be contributing to the reported experiences.
