## Introduction

One of the most common features of a sensed presence in a supposedly haunted place is not the feeling that *something* is there, but the conviction that *someone* is there. People often describe an unseen observer standing behind them, watching from a doorway, or waiting just beyond sight. The experience feels social: the presence seems aware, attentive, and directed toward the witness.

Research on [felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }}) suggests that this “being watched” sensation may arise when the brain's systems for detecting other minds become active under uncertain conditions. Rather than requiring a visible figure or a voice, the mind can infer agency from incomplete information. In dim, unfamiliar, emotionally charged settings, the brain may treat ambiguous cues as signs that another conscious being is nearby. This [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) helps explain why empty rooms can sometimes feel intensely occupied, even when no clear sensory evidence is present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36990104/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 33 — Felt presence offers a sublime opport...</span></span></span>

## What Makes a Presence Feel Social?

A cold draught, strange smell, or unexplained sound does not automatically create the impression of a ghost. What makes a sensed presence distinctive is the feeling that another intelligence is involved.

Researchers who study felt presence note that people frequently report an unseen being that appears to possess awareness, intention, or attention. The experience is often described in interpersonal terms: “someone was behind me”, “something was watching me”, or “I felt another person in the room”. The key feature is not merely perception but social perception—the impression of another mind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36990104/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 33 — Felt presence offers a sublime opport...</span></span></span>

This social quality explains why a haunted location can feel threatening even without dramatic phenomena. A witness may hear no voice and see no figure, yet still react as though another person has entered the space. The emotional response resembles a reaction to human presence rather than to an unusual environmental event.

Some researchers have argued that felt presence should be understood partly as a form of social cognition. The same mental systems that help people track the intentions, attention, and awareness of others may sometimes generate a perception of agency when evidence is incomplete or ambiguous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dreamscience.ca/en/documents/publications/_2007_Nielsen_Reprint_CC_16_975-983_felt-presence-commentary.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dreamscience.ca">[Dreamscience]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dreamscience.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Felt presence: Paranoid delusion or hallucinatory social</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Felt presence: Paranoid delusion or hallucinatory social...November 15, 2007 — by T Nielsen · 2007 · Cited by 65 — Rather th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 15, 2007</span></span></span>

## Agency Without Sight or Sound

Humans are remarkably sensitive to signs of agency. We routinely infer the presence of other minds from tiny cues: a movement in peripheral vision, a sound in another room, a shift in attention, or a pattern that seems purposeful.

Psychologists have long studied this tendency through theories of agency detection. The basic idea is that people often interpret uncertain stimuli as signs of intentional actors rather than random events. From an evolutionary perspective, falsely assuming that an agent is present may sometimes be less costly than failing to notice a real person, predator, or threat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/32525276/2153599X.2017.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.uva.nl">[Pure]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.uva.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DARE (Digital Academic Repository)Today — It has been hypothesized that humans have evolved a hypersensitivity to detect intentio...</span></span></span>

Laboratory studies show how easily agency can be perceived. People frequently detect faces in random patterns, infer intention from simple moving shapes, or report the presence of agents in ambiguous virtual environments. In one virtual-reality study, most participants reported perceiving an agent at least once when none was actually present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328253042_Fear_the_unseen_supernatural_belief_and_agency_detection_in_virtual_reality" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gatesupernatural belief and agency detection in virtual reality</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">supernatural belief and agency detection in virtual realityJanuary 1, 2020 — 14 Apr 2022 — Results indicated that the VR task...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2020</span></span></span>

Importantly, the brain does not need a complete image of a person to activate social processing. A suggestion of movement, a faint sound, or a sense that attention is directed toward oneself can be enough to create the feeling that another being occupies the space.

In haunted settings, where lighting is poor and expectations are often heightened, these agency-detection processes may have more opportunity to operate. Small uncertainties can become organised into the perception of an unseen observer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36990104/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 33 — Felt presence offers a sublime opport...</span></span></span>

## When Threat Detection Fills the Gap

The feeling of being watched often carries an emotional charge. People do not merely sense a presence; they sense a presence that is monitoring them.

This may involve the interaction of agency detection with threat-monitoring systems. Human attention is highly tuned to signs that another individual may be observing, approaching, or evaluating us. Being watched has important social consequences, so the brain devotes substantial resources to detecting it.

Evidence from cognitive neuroscience shows that even real surveillance alters perception. Experiments have found that people who know they are being monitored become faster at detecting faces and social cues, suggesting that watchfulness can change how the brain processes information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae039/7920510" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicBig brother: the effects of surveillance on fundamental aspects...by K Seymour · 2024 · Cited by 10 — we show that being wat...</span></span></span>

In a haunted environment, uncertainty may encourage a similar state of heightened vigilance. A person alone in a dark corridor may repeatedly scan for signs of another individual. When no clear explanation emerges, the mind can still generate a social interpretation: someone is there, just unseen.

The result is a powerful asymmetry. The witness feels observed without seeing an observer. That imbalance often creates anxiety because the imagined agent appears to possess informational advantage. It knows where the witness is; the witness does not know where it is.

## Why “Being Watched” Feels So Convincing

A striking aspect of felt presence reports is the certainty they often carry. Witnesses frequently insist that they *knew* somebody was there despite lacking evidence.

One reason may be that social awareness systems normally operate below conscious reasoning. In everyday life, people constantly track gaze direction, body position, proximity, and social attention without deliberate analysis. When these systems generate an output—“someone is nearby”—the conclusion can arrive as an intuition rather than a logical deduction.

Neuroscientific research into presence hallucinations and felt presence experiences suggests that the brain can sometimes construct a representation of another nearby person even when sensory information is incomplete. The resulting experience feels perceptual rather than imagined. People do not merely think that someone may be present; they experience another presence as a fact of the environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brainfacts.org/thinking-sensing-and-behaving/thinking-and-awareness/2024/presence-hallucination-research-shows-how-our-brains-conjure-ghostly-illusions-103024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brainfacts.org">[BrainFacts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brainfacts.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Presence Hallucination Research Shows How Our Brains...30 Oct 2024 — Presence hallucinations are the strange sensation that so...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why many haunted-place reports focus on certainty rather than evidence. The conviction originates from internal social-processing mechanisms that ordinarily serve useful functions in daily life.

## The Presence That Watches

Not all felt presences are hostile. Studies and collected reports show that some are experienced as protective companions, guides, deceased relatives, or neutral [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }}).
