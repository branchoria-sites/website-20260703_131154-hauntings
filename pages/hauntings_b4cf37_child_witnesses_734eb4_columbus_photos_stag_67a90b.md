## Introduction

The Columbus poltergeist case became famous in 1984 largely because of a set of striking photographs. At the centre was 14-year-old Tina Resch of Columbus, Ohio, around whom a series of alleged poltergeist disturbances were reported. The images, particularly a photograph appearing to show a telephone flying through the air in front of her, seemed to provide something rare in haunting cases: visual evidence of an object in motion at the exact moment of an alleged paranormal event. Yet the same photographs that made the case famous also became the focus of some of its strongest criticism. Investigators, sceptics, journalists and later commentators disagreed sharply over what the images actually showed and whether they documented unexplained phenomena or carefully timed trickery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

The Columbus case remains a useful example within child-centred poltergeist reports because it demonstrates how persuasive dramatic imagery can be while still leaving crucial questions unanswered about who moved an object, when it began moving, and what happened immediately before the camera shutter was pressed.

## Why the Photographs Made Tina Resch Famous

The photographs were taken by Columbus Dispatch photojournalist Fred Shannon during the intense media attention surrounding the case. The most widely circulated image appears to show a telephone handset and cord suspended in front of Tina while she sits in a chair. The photograph was distributed widely and became one of the defining visual symbols of modern [poltergeist claims]({{ 'moving-objects/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

What made the image so compelling was its apparent simplicity. Unlike witness testimony, it seemed objective. Readers did not need to rely on descriptions of knocks, sounds or fleeting observations. They could look directly at a picture and see an object apparently in mid-flight.

For many [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }}), the photograph appeared to answer the central question of the case. If a telephone was visibly airborne, perhaps the reports of flying objects were genuine. The image helped transform a local Ohio story into an internationally discussed paranormal case and attracted the attention of parapsychologist William Roll, who later investigated the family and argued that unusual events had occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/tina-resch-columbus-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Tina Resch (Columbus Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaTina Resch (Columbus Poltergeist) - Psi EncyclopediaJul 9, 2019 — A violent burst of apparent poltergeist activity in an...</span></span></span>

However, photographs freeze only an instant. They can show where an object is, but not necessarily how it got there.

## What Sceptics Argued About Staging and Weak Evidence

Critics focused on exactly that limitation. James Randi and other sceptical investigators argued that the famous images did not actually demonstrate spontaneous object movement. Instead, they contended that the photographs merely captured objects after they had already been thrown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

According to sceptical accounts, an important detail was often overlooked: witnesses frequently reported difficulty catching any object beginning to move. One criticism held that objects seemed to move only when observers were distracted or looking elsewhere. The camera could therefore record an object already in flight without establishing whether an unseen force or an ordinary human action had started the movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

Randi's review of photographic sequences and unpublished images led him to conclude that the evidence pointed towards deliberate staging rather than paranormal activity. He argued that other photographs showed signs of ordinary physical manipulation and that the celebrated telephone image looked consistent with an object that had simply been tossed. He characterised the overall evidential record as largely anecdotal and insufficient to support extraordinary claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

Further scepticism emerged when television footage reportedly captured Tina deliberately knocking over a lamp and then reacting as though a paranormal event had occurred. Although supporters argued that one staged incident did not necessarily invalidate every report, critics viewed the footage as strong evidence that at least some phenomena were manufactured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

The staging argument did not depend on proving that every incident was fraudulent. Rather, sceptics maintained that once trickery was documented, the burden of proof on the remaining claims became much higher.

## How Still Images Differ from Continuous Observation

The Columbus photographs illustrate a broader evidential problem in [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) and poltergeist reports. A still photograph records a fraction of a second. It cannot reveal what happened immediately before or after the image was captured.

In practical terms, a photograph of a flying telephone cannot by itself distinguish between several possibilities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* An object thrown by a person.
* An object accidentally knocked loose.
* An object manipulated off-camera.
* A genuinely unexplained movement.

</div>

All four scenarios can produce a visually dramatic image.

Continuous observation, by contrast, allows investigators to examine sequence, timing and causation. A video recording showing an object at rest, beginning to move without contact and continuing under controlled observation would provide a different kind of evidence from a single frame showing only the middle of an event.

This distinction became central to debate over the Columbus case. Believers often pointed to the photographs as unusually vivid documentation. Critics replied that the images recorded effects rather than causes. The photographs showed that objects were airborne at a particular moment; they did not conclusively show why they were airborne.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

## What the Columbus Photos Ultimately Demonstrate

The enduring significance of the Columbus photographs lies less in what they prove than in what they reveal about evidence in hauntings involving children. The images were powerful because they appeared to provide direct visual confirmation of extraordinary events centred on a teenager. Yet decades of debate have shown that visual drama and evidential strength are not the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Resch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tina Resch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tina Resch</span></span></span>

Supporters of the case continue to regard the photographs as part of a larger pattern of unusual activity investigated by William Roll and others. Sceptics continue to argue that the images are better explained by deliberate manipulation and that subsequent evidence of trickery undermines their credibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/tina-resch-columbus-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Tina Resch (Columbus Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaTina Resch (Columbus Poltergeist) - Psi EncyclopediaJul 9, 2019 — A violent burst of apparent poltergeist activity in an...</span></span></span>
