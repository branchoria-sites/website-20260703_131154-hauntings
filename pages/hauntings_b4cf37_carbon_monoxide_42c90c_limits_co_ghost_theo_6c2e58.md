## Introduction

Carbon monoxide has earned its place in discussions of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) because it can produce confusion, dread, hallucinations, unusual sensations and other experiences that people may interpret as supernatural. Some famous “haunted house” cases were eventually traced to faulty furnaces or other sources of exposure. Yet treating carbon monoxide as the explanation for every ghost report is as simplistic as assuming every unexplained experience is paranormal. Good [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) requires matching explanations to evidence, not applying a single answer to every case.

The most responsible approach is to treat carbon monoxide as an urgent safety check. If symptoms and environmental clues point towards possible poisoning, the building should be assessed immediately. If those clues are absent, however, other psychological, physiological and environmental factors may fit the reported experiences better. The challenge is not choosing between “ghosts” and “carbon monoxide”, but understanding which explanation best matches the circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7304295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wilmer W. ## When the Explanation Fits

Carbon monoxide is most convincing when haunting reports are accompanied by signs that toxic exposure could be occurring. The classic pattern is not merely seeing a strange figure or hearing a noise. Instead, it involves a combination of unusual perceptions and physical symptoms affecting one or more occupants of a building.

The best-known example remains the case described by ophthalmologist William H. Wilmer in 1921. A family living in a deteriorating house reported phantom [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), mysterious figures, oppressive feelings and other apparently supernatural events. Investigation eventually identified carbon monoxide exposure from a faulty furnace as the cause. Once the problem was corrected, the haunting experiences ceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002939421908250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eyeby WH Wilmer · 1921 · Cited by 29 — In 1921 Wilmer suggested a direct toxic effect on...</span></span></span>

## When Other Causes Fit Better

The existence of genuine carbon monoxide cases does not mean every haunting narrative shares the same pattern. Many reports lack the medical and environmental indicators expected in poisoning cases.

### Sleep Paralysis and the Sense of Presence

One of the most studied sources of ghost-like experiences is sleep paralysis. During these episodes, a person becomes partly awake while elements of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep persist. The result can include an inability to move, intense fear, visual and auditory hallucinations, [chest pressure]({{ 'chest-pressure/' | relative_url }}) and the powerful feeling that another being is present in the room.

Researchers have repeatedly found that the “sensed presence” commonly reported during sleep paralysis closely resembles many traditional ghost encounters. People often describe shadowy figures, intruders at the bedside or an overwhelming awareness that someone else is nearby, despite no external person being present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574867/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensed presence as a correlate of sleep paralysis distress...by E Solomonova · 2008 · Cited by 78 — Isolated sleep paralysis (ISP...</span></span></span>

Importantly, these experiences occur in otherwise safe environments and do not require toxic exposure. When haunting reports are concentrated around sleep, bedrooms, awakening episodes or recurring night-time encounters affecting a single individual, sleep-related explanations may fit better than carbon monoxide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cashp.columbian.gwu.edu/close-encounter-or-just-half-sleep-how-scientific-inquiry-can-inform-paranormal-experiences" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cashp.columbian.gwu.edu">[Paleobiology Center+2Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cashp.columbian.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paleobiology Center Close Encounter or Just Half Sleep?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How Scientific Inquiry...Hallucinations also occur often during sleep paralysis, and might come from parts of the brain still producing...</span></span></span>

### Suggestion, Expectation and Belief

Human perception is influenced by context. If people believe a house is haunted, ambiguous sounds, shadows or sensations can become interpreted through that [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}). This does not imply dishonesty; it reflects how the brain constructs meaning from incomplete information.

Research examining paranormal beliefs has found links between reports of paranormal experiences and factors such as sleep disturbances, nightmares, hypnagogic hallucinations and related perceptual experiences. These findings suggest that some haunting reports emerge from interactions between belief, perception and ordinary cognitive processes rather than from poisoning or supernatural causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36632033/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The associations between paranormal beliefs and sleep...by B Rauf · 2023 · Cited by 12 — This study aims to fill a gap in the lite...</span></span></span>

### Grief and Emotional Stress

Many haunting reports occur after bereavement. People may sense a deceased relative's presence, hear a familiar voice or interpret ordinary events as meaningful signs. Such experiences are well documented in grief research and often occur without signs of mental illness or environmental hazards.

In these situations, carbon monoxide usually lacks explanatory power because the experiences are tied to specific emotional circumstances rather than shared environmental exposure. The timing, meaning and personal significance of the experience may provide more useful clues than the physical structure of the building.

## How to Avoid Lazy Debunking

The popularity of the carbon monoxide explanation has created a new problem: it can become a reflexive dismissal rather than an evidence-based assessment.

A careful investigation asks whether the specific facts support the theory. Simply hearing that someone saw an apparition does not justify assuming carbon monoxide exposure. Likewise, the absence of an obvious natural explanation does not justify concluding that a ghost was present.

A better approach follows a sequence of questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Is there a safety risk?** Check for carbon monoxide exposure and other environmental hazards first.
2. **What is the pattern?** Determine whether experiences occur during sleep, periods of illness, grief or heightened stress.
3. **Who experienced it?** Shared experiences affecting multiple occupants may point towards environmental factors, while isolated experiences may suggest different explanations.
4. **What independent evidence exists?** Distinguish between subjective perceptions and verifiable events.
5. **Does the explanation fit all major details?** Prefer explanations that account for the full pattern rather than a single striking feature.

</div>

This approach avoids two common errors: assuming every haunting is paranormal and assuming every haunting is carbon monoxide poisoning. Both mistakes replace investigation with preconceived conclusions.

## Why the Limits Matter

The real lesson of the carbon monoxide theory is not that ghost stories are solved once and for all. Rather, it demonstrates that apparently supernatural experiences can sometimes emerge from ordinary causes that are medically significant and potentially dangerous. The theory is valuable precisely because it identifies a specific [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) with clear warning signs.

Its limitation is equally important. Carbon monoxide explains some hauntings, not all of them. Sleep paralysis, suggestion, grief, building acoustics and other well-documented factors can generate experiences that look remarkably similar on the surface.
