## Introduction

For alleged electronic voice phenomena (EVP), the key question is not whether one person can hear words in a recording, but whether multiple independent listeners hear the same words without being told what to expect. Blind review methods were developed to address exactly this problem. By removing labels, transcripts, ghost stories and investigator interpretations before playback, researchers and investigators can test whether an apparent voice communicates a consistent message or whether listeners are supplying their own meanings to an ambiguous sound. Studies of auditory perception and EVP-like recordings repeatedly show that expectations can influence whether people hear a voice at all and what they think it says. When listeners are blinded, agreement often drops sharply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — When a voice was detected...</span></span></span>

Within haunting investigations, blind review does not prove that a voice is paranormal. Instead, it provides a fairer and more rigorous way to evaluate whether an EVP claim survives the removal of suggestion.

## Why Labels and Backstories Should Be Hidden First

Many EVP clips are presented alongside a narrative. Investigators may explain that the recording came from a haunted prison, that a spirit was asked a question moments earlier, or that the sound supposedly says a specific phrase. These details can unintentionally guide perception.

Psychological research on auditory [pareidolia]({{ 'pareidolia/' | relative_url }})—the tendency to perceive meaningful speech in ambiguous sounds—has demonstrated that context affects what people report hearing. In a widely cited study by Michael Nees and Charlotte Phillips, participants who were primed to think about paranormal EVP were more likely to report hearing voices in ambiguous recordings than participants given a neutral explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — When a voice was detected...</span></span></span>

This matters because many famous EVP examples are first encountered after listeners have already been told what the recording allegedly contains. Once a phrase is suggested, it can become difficult to hear alternatives. The effect is similar to visual illusions in which a hidden image becomes obvious after someone points it out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_perception" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Speech perception</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Speech perception</span></span></span>

A proper blind review therefore removes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Claimed transcripts.
* Information about the haunted location.
* Details about who recorded the clip.
* Information about questions supposedly asked before the sound occurred.
* Any indication that the clip is considered paranormal.

</div>

The listener receives only the audio.

## How to Compare Listener Transcripts

The strongest blind-review approach resembles procedures used in perception research and forensic listening tests. Rather than asking, “Do you hear a ghost?”, investigators ask participants to write down exactly what they hear.

A basic protocol includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Present the audio without explanation.
2. Allow repeated listening under identical conditions.
3. Require each listener to write a transcript independently.
4. Prevent discussion between listeners.
5. Collect responses before revealing any proposed interpretation.

</div>

The resulting transcripts can then be compared. If an alleged EVP truly contains a clear phrase, independent listeners should produce similar wording. If responses vary widely, the recording may be too ambiguous to support strong claims.

Researchers examining purported [EVP recordings]({{ 'evp-recordings/' | relative_url }}) have found that although participants sometimes report hearing voices, agreement about the content of those voices is often low. In the Nees and Phillips study, listeners who detected speech frequently disagreed about the actual message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — When a voice was detected...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important. Hearing “a voice” is not the same as hearing “the same sentence”.

### Open Responses Versus Multiple Choice

One common weakness in EVP testing is the use of leading options.

For example, imagine an investigator plays a clip and asks listeners whether it says:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* “Get out.”
* “Leave now.”
* “Go away.”
* “Help me.”

</div>

Even if the sound is unclear, many people will choose one of the offered phrases.

A more rigorous method uses open-ended transcription first. Listeners write whatever they believe they hear without prompts. Only after these responses are collected might researchers compare them with proposed interpretations.

Open-ended methods are generally harder on an EVP claim, but they better measure genuine agreement.

## What Agreement Levels Can Tell Investigators

Blind review turns a subjective impression into something that can be measured.

Several outcomes are possible.

**High agreement.**

If most independent listeners write nearly identical transcripts, the recording likely contains recognisable speech. This does not establish a paranormal source, but it does show that the words are objectively detectable.

**Moderate agreement.**

Listeners may agree on certain words while differing on others. This suggests that part of the signal is intelligible and part remains uncertain.

**Low agreement.**

If transcripts vary dramatically, the clip may be functioning more like an auditory inkblot test than a clear recording. Different listeners are extracting different patterns from the same sound. Research on EVP perception has repeatedly highlighted this possibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — When a voice was detected...</span></span></span>

For investigators, low agreement is often more informative than the original claim. It indicates that confidence in a particular interpretation should be reduced.

## A Practical Example of Blind Evaluation

Consider a recording that investigators claim contains the phrase “I died here”.

In a non-blind setting, new listeners are told the phrase before playback. Many will subsequently report hearing exactly those words.

In a blind review, twenty listeners receive only the audio file.

Their transcripts might produce results such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* 3 hear “I died here”.
* 4 hear “right here”.
* 5 hear “I’m right here”.
* 4 hear unintelligible speech.
* 4 hear no voice at all.

</div>

Under these conditions, the proposed phrase no longer appears uniquely supported. The recording may still contain a speech-like sound, but the evidence that it specifically says “I died here” is weak.

This example illustrates why blind review focuses on listener convergence rather than investigator conviction.

## Common Pitfalls That Undermine Blind Testing

Even investigators who attempt blind review can accidentally introduce cues.

Frequent problems include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Naming audio files with suggestive titles.
* Revealing the haunted location before testing.
* Playing clips alongside dramatic photographs or video.
* Providing partial transcripts.
* Allowing listeners to discuss interpretations before submitting responses.
* Replaying only selected segments rather than the full recording context.

</div>

Each of these factors can influence perception and inflate apparent agreement.

Another issue is selective reporting. If investigators test many clips but publish only the few that produce favourable responses, the results can appear stronger than they really are. Good practice requires recording both successful and unsuccessful trials.

## Why Blind Review Matters in Haunting Investigations

Blind review is one of the simplest governance measures available to anyone evaluating EVP claims. It does not require specialised equipment, advanced audio software or a particular belief about ghosts. It merely separates the recording from the story surrounding it.

Because EVP evidence often depends on interpretation, the central question is whether the interpretation survives independent examination. Research on auditory pareidolia, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) effects and EVP perception suggests that listeners can be strongly influenced by context and often disagree about the content of ambiguous recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — When a voice was detected...</span></span></span>
