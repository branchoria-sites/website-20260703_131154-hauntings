## Introduction

In the study of grief-related [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), few pieces of research have had as much influence as W. Dewi Rees’s 1971 paper *The Hallucinations of Widowhood*. At a time when seeing, hearing, or sensing a deceased spouse was often assumed to be a sign of mental instability, Rees reported something unexpected: such experiences were remarkably common among ordinary bereaved people and were usually experienced as comforting rather than pathological. His findings helped shift discussion away from the idea that [grief visions]({{ 'grief-visions/' | relative_url }}) were rare abnormalities and towards the view that they can be a normal part of bereavement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5096884/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hallucinations of Widowhoodby WD Rees · 1971 · Cited by 655 — 227 widows and 66 widowers were interviewed to determine the exte...</span></span></span>

For the broader topic of hauntings, the significance of Rees’s work lies in its focus on experiences that many people would casually describe as ghost encounters. Rather than investigating supernatural claims, Rees examined how widows and widowers experienced the continuing presence of a loved one after death. The result was one of the most frequently cited studies in bereavement research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/phenomenology-and-impact-of-hallucinations-concerning-the-deceased/1AA7D2F7BB52C491462566785D752E02" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">widow or widower. ## What Rees Asked Widows and Widowers

Rees was a general practitioner working in rural mid-Wales. While studying widowhood and adjustment to loss, he interviewed 227 widows and 66 widowers, representing a large proportion of the widowed population in a defined local area. The sample was unusually comprehensive for its time, covering more than 80% of all widowed residents and over 94% of those well enough to participate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5096884/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hallucinations of Widowhoodby WD Rees · 1971 · Cited by 655 — 227 widows and 66 widowers were interviewed to determine the exte...</span></span></span>

The most striking result was that almost half of those interviewed reported some form of hallucination or illusion involving their deceased spouse. Men and women reported such experiences at broadly similar rates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5096884/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hallucinations of Widowhoodby WD Rees · 1971 · Cited by 655 — 227 widows and 66 widowers were interviewed to determine the exte...</span></span></span>

Rees also found that many of these experiences lasted far longer than outsiders might expect. They were most common during the first decade after bereavement, but some continued for years. ## Why the Findings Challenged Stigma

The importance of the study was not simply that these experiences occurred. The more controversial finding was that they did not appear to fit the stereotype of mental illness.

Rees reported no clear relationship between the experiences and known depressive illness. Social isolation also failed to explain why some widowed people sensed their spouse while others did not. Instead, the strongest associations pointed in a different direction: experiences were more common among people who had enjoyed long, happy marriages and who had shared children with their spouse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incidence of hallucination increased with length of marriage and ...Read more</span></span></span>

This pattern suggested that the experiences reflected the depth and persistence of attachment rather than psychiatric breakdown. The dead spouse remained psychologically present because the relationship had been deeply woven into everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incidence of hallucination increased with length of marriage and ...Read more</span></span></span>

Perhaps the most influential sentence in the paper was Rees’s conclusion that these hallucinations should be regarded as “normal and helpful accompaniments of widowhood.” That statement challenged decades of assumptions that unusual sensory experiences after bereavement were inherently pathological.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incidence of hallucination increased with length of marriage and ...Read more</span></span></span>

The study also highlighted the role of stigma. Many participants had concealed their experiences, sometimes for years, because they feared being judged or considered irrational. Rees revealed that what seemed private and unusual was, in fact, widely shared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incidence of hallucination increased with length of marriage and ...Read more</span></span></span>

## What the Experiences Actually Looked Like

One reason Rees’s work remains influential is that it documented a spectrum of experiences rather than a single dramatic phenomenon.

Some reports resembled what people imagine when they hear the word “ghost”: a clear visual appearance of the deceased spouse. Others were far subtler. A widow might hear familiar [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), feel someone sit on the bed, or suddenly know with certainty that her husband was standing nearby. In many cases the experience felt completely real at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The incidence of hallucination increased with length of marriage and ...Read more</span></span></span>

Modern researchers often distinguish between full sensory experiences and “sense of presence” experiences. Rees’s interviews captured both forms, showing that bereavement encounters exist on a continuum from faint impressions to vivid perceptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/166741/7/1363461520962887.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research Onlinebereavement perception or hallucination of the deceasedby P Sabucedo · 2023 · Cited by 40 — During and after th...</span></span></span>

Importantly, most participants did not interpret the experiences as frightening. Many described them as reassuring, comforting, or emotionally sustaining during a difficult period of adjustment. ## How Later Research Broadened the Picture

Subsequent studies repeatedly found results that echoed Rees’s original observations.

A study of widowed nursing-home residents in the United States reported that 61% of widows experienced hallucinations of their deceased spouse. Most considered the experiences beneficial, nearly half continued to experience them, and more than half had never previously discussed them with anyone. ## Why the Rees Study Still Matters

More than fifty years after publication, Rees’s study remains a landmark because it altered the framework through which [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }})-related hauntings are understood.

Before Rees, reports of seeing or hearing a deceased spouse could easily be dismissed as signs of disorder. After Rees, researchers increasingly treated them as experiences requiring explanation rather than automatic pathologisation. The question shifted from “Why is this person hallucinating?” to “How common are these experiences, what do they mean to the bereaved, and how do they fit into the process of continuing a bond with someone who has died?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/phenomenology-and-impact-of-hallucinations-concerning-the-deceased/1AA7D2F7BB52C491462566785D752E02" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">widow or widower.
