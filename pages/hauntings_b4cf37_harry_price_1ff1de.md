## Introduction

Harry Price mattered because he did more than investigate [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}): he helped decide which hauntings became famous, what counted as evidence, and how later readers would imagine the case. At Borley Rectory in Essex, his most celebrated haunting, Price turned scattered local reports, newspaper curiosity, séances, witness statements, vigils, photographs, fragments of excavation, and theatrical publicity into the enduring claim that Borley was “the most haunted house in England”. The result is not a simple triumph of [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) or a simple exposure of fraud. It is a cautionary case in how a charismatic investigator can enlarge a haunting while also weakening the evidential record through publicity, selective reporting, weak controls and personal myth-making. Price’s legacy is therefore double-edged: he helped push ghost hunting towards organised methods and instruments, but Borley also shows how easily a haunting can become inseparable from the investigator who promoted it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/ghost-hunting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia+2Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Ghost Hunting – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Ghost Hunting – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

## Why Harry Price changed the shape of ghost hunting

Harry Price was not just a believer with a notebook. He came to [psychical research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}) through conjuring, performance, collecting, journalism and organised experimentation. The University of London archive records his early interest in archaeology and collecting, his 1922 exposure of the spirit photographer William Hope, the foundation of the National Laboratory of Psychical Research in 1923, and his later attempt to connect psychical research with the University of London through the University of London Council for Psychical Investigation in 1934.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.libraries.london.ac.uk/Details/archive/110007211" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.libraries.london.ac.uk">[University of London Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.libraries.london.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Details &#124; Harry Price archive...</span></span></span>

That mixture mattered. A haunting investigator with knowledge of conjuring is better placed to spot trickery than a purely credulous observer. Price’s exposure of William Hope shows the useful side of that skill. Hope’s spirit photographs had powerful spiritualist support, and Price arranged specially marked photographic plates to test whether Hope was using the plates supplied for the sitting or swapping in prepared plates carrying alleged spirit “extras”. The Science Museum Group Journal describes the case as carefully calculated, with the hidden plate markings designed to reveal substitution after processing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk/article/photographic-plates-and-spirit-fakes-remembering-harry-prices-investigation-of-william-hopes-spirit-photography-at-its-centenary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk">[journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencemuseum.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Yet the same qualities that made Price effective also made him risky. He understood theatre, publicity and narrative. The Science Museum Group Journal notes that Price had trained as a performer and storyteller in his youth, and later became a popular media raconteur of occult subjects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk/article/photographic-plates-and-spirit-fakes-remembering-harry-prices-investigation-of-william-hopes-spirit-photography-at-its-centenary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk">[journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencemuseum.ac.uk.</span></span></span> In hauntings, that ability could turn a messy set of reports into a gripping public story. It could also encourage the public to remember the dramatic version before the careful one.

Price’s collecting also shaped his authority. Senate House Library describes the Harry Price Library of Magical Literature as a collection of nearly 13,000 books, pamphlets and periodical titles, with strengths in magic, conjuring, psychical phenomena and related subjects. Price deposited the library at Senate House in 1936 and bequeathed it to the University of London in 1948.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.london.ac.uk/about/services/senate-house-library/collections/printed-special-collections/harry-price-library-magical-literature" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: london.ac.uk">[University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">london.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on london.ac.uk.</span></span></span> This gave him a scholarly aura: he was not merely visiting haunted houses, but building an archive, a laboratory culture and a public identity around psychical research.

## How much did Harry Price shape Borley?

[Borley]({{ 'borley/' | relative_url }}) Rectory already had stories attached to it before Price arrived. Reports included a spectral nun, strange figures, bells, whispers and other disturbances. But Borley became a national haunting through a chain of media and investigative choices in which Price was central. According to the Psi Encyclopedia account, the Smiths, then living at the rectory, asked the *Daily Mirror* to put them in touch with someone who might help; the newspaper contacted Price, then director of the National Laboratory of Psychical Research, and he visited on 12 June 1929 with his secretary Lucy Kaye and a newspaper reporter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

That origin matters because the case entered Price’s hands through the press, not through a quiet scientific referral. From the start, Borley was a haunting, an investigation and a media event. Price interviewed multiple residents, local witnesses and later figures connected with the rectory, but the first visit also produced dramatic reported phenomena: a broken glass roof, a smashed red glass candlestick, thrown pebbles and séance raps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

Price’s role became even more decisive in the late 1930s. From May 1937 to 1938, he rented Borley Rectory and appealed in *The Times* for helpers, creating a team of 48 “official [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }})”, including an army colonel, a doctor and the engineer Sidney Glanville. This sounds methodical, and in some ways it was more structured than casual ghost watching. But the same account notes a serious weakness: Price provided guidance, yet no official log of events was kept.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

That gap is central to the Borley problem. Price gave the haunting a framework, vocabulary and publicity machine, but the record he left was not strong enough to settle the case. In a haunting, where noises, sightings and object movements can be fleeting, the difference between a contemporaneous log and a later narrative is enormous. Price helped create the evidence record, but that record was filtered through his books, choices and reputation.

His publications fixed the public image. *The Most Haunted House in England*, listed by the Internet Archive as Price’s 1941 book on “Ten Years’ Investigation of Borley Rectory”, gave the case its most enduring label.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/mosthauntedhouse0000harr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> His earlier *Confessions of a Ghost-Hunter*, published in 1936, also helped establish Price as a public-facing investigator rather than a purely private researcher.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.218596" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Borley’s fame therefore cannot be separated from Price’s authorship: he did not merely report the legend; he branded it.

## What people report

The reports connected with Borley are varied, which is one reason the case has remained so hard to summarise. It is not only a “ghost nun” story, though that image became the most memorable. The Psi Encyclopedia lists [early reports]({{ 'early-report/' | relative_url }}) of a spectral nun seen by the Bull sisters in 1900 and later alleged appearances to other witnesses, as well as claims of apparitions linked to deceased individuals. During the Smiths’ tenancy in 1928, reported incidents included servants’ bells ringing by themselves, shadowy figures and whispered words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

During the Foyster period from 1930 to 1935, the claims became more poltergeist-like. Price later claimed that at least 2,000 inexplicable phenomena occurred, including footsteps, whispers, bells, raps, knocks, scents of perfume and incense, thrown objects, locked and unlocked doors, shattered windows, overturned furniture, spontaneous fires, wall writing, writing on paper and Marianne Foyster allegedly being thrown from her bed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

Those reports are important because they create different evidential problems:

* **Apparitions** could involve misperception, expectation, low light, folklore, memory or deliberate invention.
* **Noises** could come from animals, building movement, acoustics, outside activity or human pranksters.
* **Thrown objects and broken items** require tighter controls, because they may involve concealed human action, accident or later exaggeration.
* **Wall writing and séance communications** are especially vulnerable to suggestion, theatrical framing and the investigator’s expectations.
* **Physical traces**, such as the jaw bone and objects excavated under Price’s supervision in 1943, appear stronger at first glance, but their origin and dating were later disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

Price’s treatment of these reports was not uniformly credulous. He sometimes cast doubt on Marianne Foyster’s role, suggesting that she might have been responsible for phenomena witnessed on one occasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span> This is one reason the case resists a simple “Price believed everything” reading. He could be sceptical, and his background in conjuring gave him tools for detecting deception. The more difficult question is whether he applied those tools consistently when the haunting itself had become central to his own reputation.

## Natural explanations to check

Borley is useful for readers of hauntings because it shows what should be checked before treating a haunting as unexplained. The later Society for Psychical Research investigation, published after Price’s death by Eric Dingwall, Mollie Goldney and Trevor Hall, argued that the case could be reduced by normal explanations and deception. The Psi Encyclopedia summary says the SPR investigators found little reason to trust Price’s veracity or the genuineness of the phenomena. They suggested that apparitions could be illusions or hallucinations; sounds could come from rodents, outside activity or unknown movement in the rectory; incense smells were not surprising near a church; physical phenomena could involve coincidence or accidental movement; and other events could reflect mistaken memory, distorted recall or outright deception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The strongest lesson is not that every Borley report is automatically false. It is that haunting evidence must be sorted by type and by control. A single unexplained sound in an old rectory is weak evidence. A pattern of sounds heard by independent witnesses is more interesting, but still vulnerable to acoustics, animals and expectation. Object movement is stronger only if access is controlled, observers are independent, timing is recorded and normal handling is excluded. Séance communications are weakest when they supply dramatic backstory without documentary support.

Several natural or non-paranormal checks stand out in Borley:

**The building itself.** A large old rectory, later damaged by fire and eventually demolished, offered many opportunities for noises, draughts, shifting materials and misleading acoustics. The SPR critique specifically pointed towards rodents, outside activity and unknown movement within the rectory as possible sources of sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

**The people present.** The Foyster period placed many reports around Marianne Foyster, and later accounts included accusations and counter-accusations of fraud. Marianne reportedly told later interviewers that Price knew the diary of occurrences was fictitious and that Lionel Foyster had faked some phenomena to observe reactions, while also maintaining that not every incident was caused by them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span> That leaves a messy human record rather than a clean paranormal one.

**The record-keeping.** Price’s 1937–38 observer scheme sounds impressive, but the lack of an official log undercuts it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span> A haunting investigation without a reliable contemporaneous log is vulnerable to selection, [memory drift]({{ 'memory-drift/' | relative_url }}), duplicated stories and later narrative shaping.

**The investigator’s incentives.** Price had public standing, books, broadcasts and a reputation invested in psychical research. The University of London archive records his BBC broadcast from a haunted manor house in Meopham in 1936, his books, public experiments and continuing work across psychical subjects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.libraries.london.ac.uk/Details/archive/110007211" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.libraries.london.ac.uk">[University of London Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.libraries.london.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Details &#124; Harry Price archive...</span></span></span> None of this proves fraud, but it does mean publicity was part of the environment in which evidence was gathered.

## The controversy after Price

Price died in 1948, and the argument over Borley intensified after his death. The key critical moment was the SPR’s posthumous investigation by Dingwall, Goldney and Hall, published in 1956. Their report accused Price’s Borley account of distortion and concluded that the evidence for haunting and poltergeist activity diminished under analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The criticism did not end the case. Robert Hastings later published an SPR report seeking to vindicate Price, re-examining allegations including Charles Sutton’s testimony, the excavated materials, the “flying brick” incident, Foyster writings, graffiti and claims of manipulation. Hastings argued that attacks on Price’s personal integrity were largely unfounded and suggested that the authors of the critical Borley report had their own agenda against him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

This is why Borley remains a controversy rather than a closed file. The sceptical case is strong on weak controls, possible fraud, memory problems and normal explanations. The defensive case is strong enough to warn against turning criticism of Price into a certainty that every accuser was reliable or every report worthless. In haunting studies, that middle position is often the most honest one: Borley is a poor case for proving ghosts, but an excellent case for studying how evidence, personality, publicity and dispute interact.

Price’s wider career reinforces the same tension. His exposure of William Hope shows a capable investigator using technical controls to test a famous medium.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk/article/photographic-plates-and-spirit-fakes-remembering-harry-prices-investigation-of-william-hopes-spirit-photography-at-its-centenary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk">[journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.sciencemuseum.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencemuseum.ac.uk.</span></span></span> His role at Borley shows how the investigator himself can become part of the phenomenon. Once the public story depends on a named ghost hunter, the haunting is no longer just a set of reported events in a place. It becomes a story about trust in the interpreter.

## Why the story persists

Borley persists because it has almost every ingredient a haunting needs: an old clerical house, a spectral nun, poltergeist disturbances, wall writing, séances, ruins, disputed relics, dramatic photographs, accusations of hoaxing, a famous investigator and a long afterlife in books, television and online discussion. After the rectory was damaged by fire in 1939 and demolished in 1944, the story did not end. Later activity was said to shift towards the church opposite, and the site’s notoriety drew visitors, media attention and further investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The case also persists because Price made it legible. “The most haunted house in England” is a powerful phrase. It gives readers a simple handle for a complicated case. Without Price, Borley might have remained one local haunting among many. With Price, it became a benchmark against which later British hauntings could be compared.

There is also a deeper reason. Borley lets different readers find different stories. Believers can point to the volume of testimony, the range of witnesses, the long duration of reports and the difficulty of explaining every incident neatly. Sceptics can point to weak controls, sensational journalism, possible human fakery, disputed artefacts and the posthumous SPR critique. Historians of ghost hunting can study Price as a bridge between Victorian psychical research, interwar media culture and modern haunted-location investigation.

That is Price’s real importance to hauntings. He did not simply ask whether a ghost was present. He demonstrated how a haunting becomes public knowledge: through interviews, instruments, newspapers, books, institutional labels, dramatic names, archives, counter-reports and reputation battles. Borley’s value today is less as proof of the supernatural than as a warning about evidence under pressure. A haunting may begin with [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) in a corridor, but its fame often depends on who arrives to explain them.
