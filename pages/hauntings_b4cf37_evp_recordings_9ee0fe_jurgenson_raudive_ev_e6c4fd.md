## Introduction

The modern idea of electronic voice phenomena (EVP) began not with digital ghost-hunting gadgets but with reel-to-reel tape recorders in the late 1950s and 1960s. Two figures dominate this origin story: Swedish artist and film-maker Friedrich Jürgenson, who claimed to discover mysterious voices while recording birdsong, and Latvian psychologist Konstantin Raudive, who transformed those early experiences into a large-scale research programme. Together they helped establish the belief that tape recorders could capture messages from the dead, creating one of the most influential strands of modern haunting culture. Their work remains central to debates about whether EVP represents spirit communication, radio interference, technological artefacts, or the human tendency to find meaningful speech in ambiguous sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/jurgenson-friedrich" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopedia.com">[Encyclopedia.com+2EVL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopedia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jurgenson friedrich</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jürgenson, Friedrich (1903-1987)In July 1959 Jürgenson recorded the song of a Swedish finch on his tape recorder and on playback heard wh...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1959</span></span></span>

## Jürgenson's Birdsong Recordings

The most frequently cited origin point for EVP occurred in 1959. Friedrich Jürgenson, already known as a painter, singer and documentary film-maker, was using a portable tape recorder to capture birdsong in rural Sweden. When he played back the recording, he reported hearing unexpected human voices mixed with the bird calls. According to later accounts, some of the voices appeared to comment on the birds being recorded, while subsequent recordings seemed even more personal. Jürgenson came to believe that one voice belonged to his deceased mother, addressing him by a family nickname.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/education/2004/aug/05/research.highereducation1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2Encyclopedia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Blasts from the past &#124; Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianBlasts from the past &#124; ResearchAugust 5, 2004 — 4 Aug 2004 — Papal portrait artist and one-time opera singer Friedrich Jurgen...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 5, 2004</span></span></span>

Initially, Jürgenson suspected a technical explanation such as accidental radio reception. Tape equipment of the period was often vulnerable to stray broadcasts and electrical interference. However, he concluded that ordinary radio signals could not explain everything he was hearing. Rather than abandoning the recordings, he began making many more of them, gradually shifting from recording nature sounds to actively seeking the mysterious voices themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.evl.uic.edu/documents/liquidon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: evl.uic.edu">[EVL+2Ash International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">evl.uic.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1959, Swedish film producer and artist. His experiences were publicised through books and media appearances during the early 1960s. One of the most influential was *Voices from Space*, in which he argued that modern recording technology had opened a new channel of communication. At first he sometimes interpreted the voices as originating from unknown intelligences or cosmic sources; later he increasingly identified them as communications from deceased individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_J%C3%BCrgenson" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friedrich Jürgenson</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friedrich Jürgenson</span></span></span>

What made Jürgenson's claims culturally significant was not merely the voices themselves but the technology involved. Traditional spirit communication had relied on mediums, séances and trance states. Jürgenson presented a machine as the intermediary. A tape recorder appeared objective, repeatable and modern, giving paranormal claims a technological frame that felt distinct from nineteenth-century spiritualism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387249567_Totenfunk_Investigating_the_Techniques_and_Theologies_of_the_Electronic_Voice_Phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “Totenfunk”: Investigating the Techniques and...16 Dec 2024 — In 1963, the artist Friedrich Jürgenson announced to the...</span></span></span>

## How Raudive Turned a Curiosity into a Movement

If Jürgenson discovered EVP, Konstantin Raudive popularised it.

Raudive was a Latvian psychologist and academic who encountered Jürgenson's work in the mid-1960s. Fascinated by the recordings, he sought out Jürgenson and began participating in experiments. Their early sessions reportedly produced little that seemed remarkable, but Raudive later claimed that careful repeated listening revealed voices embedded within the recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstant%C4%ABns_Raudive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Konstantīns Raudive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Konstantīns Raudive</span></span></span>

Unlike Jürgenson, who approached the phenomenon largely as an individual explorer, Raudive attempted to systematise it. He spent years collecting recordings and claimed to have accumulated tens of thousands—and eventually more than 100,000—examples of alleged spirit voices. He described these voices as unusually brief, often consisting of only a word or short phrase. Many were said to combine multiple languages or exhibit compressed, telegraphic speech patterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

Raudive experimented with several recording methods:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Recording silence through a microphone.
* Recording radio static from untuned frequencies.
* Using electronic circuits intended to minimise ordinary broadcasts.

</div>

He argued that similar voice-like messages appeared across these different techniques, which he regarded as evidence against simple radio interference explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstant%C4%ABns_Raudive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Konstantīns Raudive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Konstantīns Raudive</span></span></span>

The publication of *Breakthrough*—originally released in German and later translated into English in 1971—brought EVP to a much larger audience. The book included descriptions of experiments, interpretations of recordings and examples that readers could hear on accompanying audio releases. ## The Claimed Voice Archives

A major reason Raudive became such an influential figure was the sheer scale of his archive. He believed that repeated recording sessions revealed consistent patterns rather than isolated anomalies. Some recordings were made in controlled environments, including radio-frequency-shielded settings intended to reduce contamination from conventional broadcasts. Raudive maintained that identifiable words still emerged under these conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

Supporters viewed this accumulation of recordings as evidence that a genuine phenomenon existed. Critics reached a different conclusion. Because the voices were often faint and ambiguous, listeners frequently disagreed about what was being said. Raudive commonly supplied interpretations or transcripts, raising the possibility that [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) influenced perception. This criticism would become one of the central debates surrounding EVP for decades to come.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

Even among believers, many of the recordings remained cryptic. Messages were often fragmentary rather than conversational. Instead of sustained dialogue, listeners typically reported isolated names, warnings, greetings or brief statements. This pattern became a defining characteristic of EVP lore and continues to influence how modern investigators interpret recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>
