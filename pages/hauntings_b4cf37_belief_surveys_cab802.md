## Introduction

Surveys suggest that ghost belief is not a fringe curiosity: it remains common enough to keep haunted places socially credible, commercially useful and personally meaningful. Those figures do not prove that any place is haunted. They do show why haunting stories travel so well. A rumour about [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) in a castle corridor, a shadow in a hotel room or voices in an old house lands in a public culture where many people are already open to the possibility.

## What people report

Haunted-place surveys usually reveal two overlapping things: belief in ghosts as a general idea, and reports of experiences tied to a particular location. The second category is especially important for [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), because a haunting is normally understood as repeated strangeness associated with a house, workplace, hotel, castle, road, ruin or other site.

The most common reports are not usually dramatic [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}) in white sheets. They are ordinary-sounding disturbances that become eerie because of context: a noise when no one else is present, a sudden change in atmosphere, a shadow seen at the edge of vision, a voice, a smell, a feeling of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }}). YouGov’s 2025 US poll found that the most common paranormal experiences among 13 options were feeling a presence or unknown energy, reported by 35%; smelling an unexplained odour, 32%; hearing unexplained sound or music, 31%; hearing a voice when no one was there, 26%; and feeling an unexplained temperature change, 26%.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span>

A 2025 Realtor.com survey, focused directly on haunted homes, found a similar pattern among people who said they had lived in a home they considered haunted: 67% reported strange sounds such as footsteps, music or banging; 61% reported shadowy figures or apparitions; 58% reported objects moving on their own; 51% reported feelings of touch or [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}); 51% reported disembodied voices; and 48% reported [flickering lights]({{ 'ghost-lights/' | relative_url }}) or appliances switching on or off.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mediaroom.realtor.com/2025-10-28-Bat%2C-Cat-or-Ghost-in-the-Attic-86-of-Americans-Believe-Haunted-Houses-Are-Possible-Realtor-com-R-Survey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mediaroom.realtor.com">[Media &#124; Move, Inc.]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mediaroom.realtor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on realtor.com.</span></span></span>

These figures matter because they make hauntings easier to understand as a category of experience. The typical haunted-place story is built from ambiguous sensory details, not from laboratory-grade evidence. A creaking pipe, a draught, a half-seen figure, a sleep-state experience or a misremembered sound can become more meaningful when it happens in a place already described as haunted.

The strongest surveys also show that people do not always interpret strange experiences in the same way. In YouGov’s 2025 US poll, among Americans who said they had experienced something paranormal, 33% thought a scientific explanation they did not know about was more likely, while 35% thought the experience was more likely otherworldly or supernatural.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span> That split is crucial: the same kind of event can be treated as a mystery, a ghost, a brain-state, a building fault or an unresolved personal memory, depending on the witness and the setting.

## How many people believe in ghosts?

Public belief varies by country, wording and survey design, but recent figures repeatedly show a large minority rather than a tiny fringe. In July 2025, YouGov found that 12% of Britons said ghosts definitely exist and 26% said they probably exist, making 38% in total; 56% said ghosts probably or definitely do not exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/52619-ghosts-magic-do-britons-believe-in-the-supernatural" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span></span></span>

The National Folklore Survey for England, released in 2025, similarly found that just over one in three respondents believed in ghosts or spirits of deceased people. It also found that more than 60% of respondents knew a ghost story or legend connected to their home region, whether or not they personally believed in ghosts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk/news/first-findings-from-the-nfs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk">[National Folklore Survey for England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk.</span></span></span> This distinction is important: haunted places do not need universal belief to survive. They need enough believers, enough curious sceptics and enough local storytelling to keep the association alive.

US evidence points in the same direction. YouGov reported in October 2025 that 38% of Americans believed ghosts exist, with women more likely than men to say so.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span> Gallup’s 2025 paranormal-belief analysis found that 34% of Americans were generally open to paranormal concepts, believing in at least three of the concepts tested, while the remaining 66% were generally sceptical. Among the open group, ghosts had especially high agreement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/692738/paranormal-phenomena-met-skepticism.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paranormal phenomena met skepticism.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Phenomena Met With Skepticism in U.S...</span></span></span>

Gallup reported in 2005 that belief in haunted houses stood at 37%, while belief that spirits of dead people can come back in certain places or situations stood at 32%.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/16915/three-four-americans-believe-paranormal.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">three four americans believe paranormal.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">three four americans believe paranormal.aspx</span></span></span> The precise numbers shift, but the broad picture is stable: ghost belief remains common enough that a “haunted” label can still shape how people interpret a place.

## Why haunted places feel plausible

A haunted place usually has three things working together: a suggestive setting, a story attached to it and ambiguous experiences that can be fitted into that story. [Old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}) are especially good at this. They produce noises, shadows, smells and temperature changes; they often have uneven lighting, unfamiliar acoustics and hidden maintenance systems; and they frequently come with histories of death, imprisonment, illness, violence, wealth, poverty or religious change.

Public heritage bodies and tourism researchers often treat ghost stories as part of how people connect with the past, not simply as claims to be debunked. English Heritage has described many sites as carrying supernatural stories, and its staff survey once ranked Bolsover Castle as its spookiest site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/about/search-news/pr-spooky-bolsover/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pr spooky bolsover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pr spooky bolsover</span></span></span> Goldsmiths researchers studying ghost tours argued that ghost walks can expose visitors to city history in an entertaining form, attracting believers and non-believers because the ghostly frame makes death, violence and taboo subjects easier to discuss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gold.ac.uk/news/spectral-cities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gold.ac.uk">[Goldsmiths, University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gold.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gold.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This is why public belief surveys and haunted places belong together. A person does not have to be a committed believer to enjoy a ghost walk, visit a haunted castle or repeat a local legend. But if a sizeable minority already believes ghosts may exist, the story has a ready audience. If many more people are undecided or enjoy the atmosphere, the story has an even wider route of transmission.

Haunted homes show the same mechanism in a more personal setting. Realtor.com’s 2025 survey found that 86% of Americans believed it was possible that houses can be haunted, while 12% said they had lived in a haunted home themselves and 7% knew someone who had.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mediaroom.realtor.com/2025-10-28-Bat%2C-Cat-or-Ghost-in-the-Attic-86-of-Americans-Believe-Haunted-Houses-Are-Possible-Realtor-com-R-Survey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mediaroom.realtor.com">[Media &#124; Move, Inc.]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mediaroom.realtor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on realtor.com.</span></span></span> That unusually high “possible” figure should not be read as firm belief in ghosts. It does, however, show how many people are willing to leave the door open.

## Natural explanations to check

A careful approach to haunted places starts by taking reports seriously without assuming the cause is supernatural. The most useful question is not “Was it a ghost?” but “What could produce this experience in this place?”

Environmental explanations are often the first to check. Low-frequency sound, known as infrasound, is below the normal range of human hearing but can be produced by traffic, ventilation, machinery, pipes and other building systems. Frontiers reported in 2026 that experimental exposure to infrasound was linked with increased irritability and raised cortisol, suggesting one possible reason some old buildings or basements feel unsettling even when nothing obvious can be heard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/news/2026/04/27/vibrating-pipes-restless-spirits-increased-stress-irritability-infrasound-exposure-paranormal-experiences-frontiers-behavioral-neuroscience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on frontiersin.org.</span></span></span> This does not explain every haunting claim, but it is relevant to reports of unease, dread or a charged atmosphere.

Carbon monoxide is a more urgent practical concern. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion as common symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning, and notes that it can be fatal, especially when people are asleep or impaired.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span> In a home with repeated “haunting” reports, especially headaches, nausea, confusion, visual disturbances or symptoms affecting several occupants, safety checks should come before paranormal interpretation.

Sleep states are another major source of ghost-like experiences. Sleep paralysis occurs in the transition between sleep and waking, when a person may be unable to move or speak while experiencing vivid sensory impressions and a strong sense of presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Sleep Paralysis: phenomenology, neurophysiology and treatment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Sleep Paralysis: phenomenology, neurophysiology and treatment</span></span></span> That does not make the experience imaginary in the casual sense; it can feel intensely real. But it does mean that a frightening figure at the bedside, pressure on the body or an unseen intruder in the room may have a known sleep-related explanation.

Building conditions also matter. Loose floorboards, heating systems, animals in roof spaces, expanding pipes, draughts, faulty wiring, reflective glass, passing headlights and unfamiliar acoustics can all create events that feel targeted or intelligent. The more emotionally charged the setting, the easier it is to connect separate incidents into a single story.

## What surveys can and cannot prove

Belief surveys are useful evidence about culture, not proof of ghosts. They tell us how many people say they believe, what kinds of experiences people report, which experiences cluster together and how social attitudes change over time. They cannot establish that a particular apparition, voice or haunted room was caused by a dead person.

Survey wording is especially important. “Do ghosts exist?”, “Can houses be haunted?”, “Have you ever had a paranormal experience?” and “Have you ever lived in a house you thought was haunted?” are not the same question. A person may reject the idea that ghosts definitely exist but still say a haunted house is possible. Another may have had a vivid experience but prefer an unknown scientific explanation. YouGov’s 2025 US findings show this complexity clearly: 60% of Americans said they had experienced at least one paranormal event, but among those experiencers, opinion was almost evenly split between unknown scientific and supernatural interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span>

Commercial surveys also need caution. Real-estate polls about haunted houses can reveal social attitudes towards stigma, disclosure and buying behaviour, but they are often timed around Halloween and framed for public interest. Realtor.com’s findings are still useful because they show how “haunted” reputations affect imagined housing choices: 33% said they would buy a home rumoured to be haunted if the price was right, another 14% said it would not affect their decision, and only 15% said they would never move into a home with a haunted history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mediaroom.realtor.com/2025-10-28-Bat%2C-Cat-or-Ghost-in-the-Attic-86-of-Americans-Believe-Haunted-Houses-Are-Possible-Realtor-com-R-Survey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mediaroom.realtor.com">[Media &#124; Move, Inc.]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mediaroom.realtor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on realtor.com.</span></span></span>

The best reading is balanced: surveys show that haunted-place belief is socially real even when the haunting itself is unproven. A rumour can affect tourism, local identity, house-buying decisions and personal memory without becoming evidence of a supernatural cause.

## Why the story persists

Haunted-place stories persist because they sit in a rare middle ground. They are specific enough to feel local — this staircase, this room, this inn, this castle, this road — but flexible enough to survive scepticism. A believer may treat the story as evidence of spirits. A sceptic may treat it as folklore, theatre, psychology or history. A tourist may not care either way if the story makes the place memorable.

The National Folklore Survey’s finding that more than 60% of respondents knew a ghost story or legend from their home region is especially revealing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk/news/first-findings-from-the-nfs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk">[National Folklore Survey for England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk.</span></span></span> It suggests that ghost stories function as local cultural knowledge, not merely private belief. Knowing the story of a haunted bridge, pub or ruin can be part of knowing the place.

Haunted places also offer a way to talk about death indirectly. Ghost tours, heritage stories and local legends often turn executions, epidemics, accidents, murders, wars and family tragedies into narratives people can approach at a safe distance. Goldsmiths researchers found that ghost tours can make tragic history entertaining without removing its connection to real past events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gold.ac.uk/news/spectral-cities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gold.ac.uk">[Goldsmiths, University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gold.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gold.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That helps explain why non-believers still participate: the ghost is not only a claim about the afterlife, but a device for making history vivid.

Finally, surveys show that belief is not isolated from experience. People who think they have lived in a haunted house are more likely to report other paranormal experiences, and people open to paranormal ideas often endorse several of them rather than just one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/692738/paranormal-phenomena-met-skepticism.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.gallup.com">[Gallup.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.gallup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paranormal phenomena met skepticism.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Phenomena Met With Skepticism in U.S...</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span> This does not mean they are wrong about every experience. It means belief, attention, memory and interpretation tend to reinforce one another.

## The clearest takeaway

Public belief surveys do not settle whether ghosts exist, but they explain why hauntings remain culturally powerful. A substantial minority in Britain, England and the United States says ghosts may exist; many more people report ambiguous experiences such as presences, sounds, voices, odours or temperature changes; and local ghost stories are widely known even among people who are not firm believers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/52619-ghosts-magic-do-britons-believe-in-the-supernatural" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk/news/first-findings-from-the-nfs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk">[National Folklore Survey for England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalfolkloresurvey.co.uk.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/53258-most-americans-say-they-have-experienced-at-least-one-paranormal-event" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Most Americans say they have experienced at least one paranormal event</span></span></span>

The sensible approach to a haunted place is therefore double-layered. Check natural explanations first: safety hazards, sleep states, sound, light, building faults and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}). But also recognise the social fact: haunted reputations endure because people keep finding them meaningful. They turn buildings into story-bearing places, and surveys show there is still a large public ready to listen.
