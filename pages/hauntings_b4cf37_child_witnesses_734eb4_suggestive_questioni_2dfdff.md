## Introduction

[Child witnesses]({{ 'child-witnesses/' | relative_url }}) are often at the centre of poltergeist reports, but the reliability of what they describe depends not only on what they experienced but also on how adults questioned them afterwards. Research on child testimony consistently shows that children can provide accurate and useful accounts when interviewed carefully. However, repeated questioning, leading prompts and strong expectations can alter how events are remembered and reported. In a haunting or poltergeist setting, where fear, uncertainty and attention are already high, these risks become especially important. The key issue is not whether a child is honest. It is whether the process of repeatedly discussing unusual events gradually changes the story itself. Studies of child memory, forensic interviewing and witness testimony suggest that it can.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2180422/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Structured forensic interview protocols improve the quality and...by ME Lamb · 2007 · Cited by 1164 — Use of the structured NICHD Pro...</span></span></span>

## Can Repeated Questions Change a Haunting Story?

The short answer is yes. Repeated questioning can change a child's account, especially when the questions imply that a different answer is expected.

Researchers have found that when children are asked the same question several times, many assume their first answer must have been wrong or incomplete. Instead of simply repeating what they originally said, they may revise, elaborate or reinterpret their memory. Experimental studies show that accuracy can decline when specific questions are repeated, particularly when those questions are difficult, unanswerable or suggestive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022096505001827" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2University of Southampton Web Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The effect of question repetition within interviews on young...by S Krähenbühl · 2006 · Cited by 60 — This study investigat...</span></span></span>

In a poltergeist case, this [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) can be subtle. A child who initially reports hearing a loud knock may later be asked:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* “Did it sound like it came from the wall?”
* “Was it the ghost again?”
* “Are you sure you didn't see something move?”
* “What happened after the knocking started?”

</div>

None of these questions necessarily introduces deliberate deception. Yet each one provides additional structure that may influence how the event is remembered. Over time, the child's account can become more detailed and dramatic even if the original experience was brief and ambiguous. Research on children's suggestibility has repeatedly shown that interviewer expectations can affect the information children provide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1133074015000124" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Children&#x27;s suggestibility research: Things to know before...by AC Hritz · 2015 · Cited by 109 — We review unexpected findin...</span></span></span>

## What Careful Child Interviewing Tries to Protect

Modern forensic interviewing methods were developed largely because researchers recognised how easily poor questioning can contaminate testimony.

The best-known example is the NICHD (National Institute of Child Health and Human Development) interview protocol. Rather than asking children to confirm specific claims, interviewers are trained to encourage free narrative accounts through open-ended prompts such as “Tell me everything that happened” or “What happened next?”. Multiple studies show that structured approaches produce more reliable information and reduce the use of suggestive questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2180422/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[Griffith Research Repository+3PMC+3NICHD Protocol]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Structured forensic interview protocols improve the quality and...by ME Lamb · 2007 · Cited by 1164 — Use of the structured NICHD Pro...</span></span></span>

The goal is not simply to avoid false reports. It is also to avoid accidentally reshaping genuine memories. Good interview practice attempts to protect several things:

* The child's original wording.
* The distinction between observation and interpretation.
* The separation of memory from later discussion.
* Resistance to interviewer expectations.
* Confidence based on actual recollection rather than social [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ojjdp.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh176/files/pubs/248749.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojjdp.ojp.gov">[PMC+3OJJDP+3NAAG]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojjdp.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Child Forensic Interviewing: Best Practicesby C Newlin · 2015 · Cited by 181 — The literature clearly explains the dangers of repeat...</span></span></span>

These safeguards matter because memory is reconstructive. People do not replay experiences like recordings; they rebuild them from stored fragments, later information and current expectations. Children are particularly sensitive to these influences, especially in emotionally charged situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/7e659cb7-0114-4f1f-9d39-845228f9022d/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge University Repository+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bridge University RepositoryChildren as Witnesses: Remembering, Reporting, and...by DA Brown · 2025 · Cited by 2 — In this review, we...</span></span></span>

## Why Poltergeist Homes Create Poor Interview Conditions

Poltergeist reports often arise in exactly the sort of environment that psychologists regard as difficult for obtaining uncontaminated testimony.

Unlike a controlled interview, a reported haunting usually involves multiple conversations occurring simultaneously. Family members discuss events with one another. Neighbours ask questions. Journalists seek details. Paranormal investigators request descriptions. Friends, teachers and relatives may all become interested.

A child may end up recounting the same incident dozens of times.

Each [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}) creates opportunities for subtle feedback. Adults may react with surprise to some details and indifference to others. They may unconsciously reward dramatic descriptions by paying more attention. They may suggest interpretations before the child has formed one independently.

Research on repeated interviews shows that repetition itself is not always harmful. Under supportive and carefully structured conditions, multiple interviews can sometimes help children provide fuller accounts without reducing accuracy. The danger arises when repetition is combined with interviewer bias, misleading information or pressure to produce a particular kind of answer. Poltergeist cases frequently contain exactly those risk factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* High emotional arousal.
* Strong prior beliefs about supernatural causes.
* Multiple interviewers.
* Long time periods between discussions.
* Community attention and media interest.
* Ongoing expectations that new phenomena will occur.

</div>

From a witness-reliability perspective, these are poor conditions for preserving a clean first account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225765/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The use and impact of repeated questions in diagnostic child...by E Macleod · 2021 · Cited by 3 — Younger children are particularly s...</span></span></span>

## How Retelling Can Blur Memory, Fear and Expectation

One reason haunting narratives often grow over time is that memory and interpretation can become intertwined.

Imagine a child hears an unexplained bang at night. Initially the memory may contain only a few elements: a sound, darkness and fear. Later conversations introduce new ideas: perhaps the noise came from a ghost, perhaps objects moved beforehand, perhaps strange events happened elsewhere in the house.

As these discussions accumulate, the child may no longer clearly distinguish between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What was directly experienced.
* What was inferred afterwards.
* What others suggested.
* What became part of the family's shared story.

</div>

Research on suggestibility shows that children can sometimes incorporate misleading information into later accounts, particularly when authority figures repeatedly revisit the same topic. False details need not be entirely invented; they may emerge through a gradual blending of memory and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1133074015000124" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[Civic Research Institute+3ScienceDirect+3PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Children&#x27;s suggestibility research: Things to know before...by AC Hritz · 2015 · Cited by 109 — We review unexpected findin...</span></span></span>

Fear can intensify this process. Anxiety naturally increases attention to possible threats and encourages people to search for explanations. In a household where unusual events are already being interpreted as paranormal, ambiguous experiences may increasingly be remembered within that framework.

The result is not necessarily dishonesty. A child can sincerely believe a later version of a story that differs from the earliest account. This distinction is crucial because credibility and accuracy are not the same thing. ## A Poltergeist Example: The Problem of Escalating Narratives

The famous [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) case illustrates why these concerns matter. The alleged disturbances became heavily associated with two children, Janet and Margaret Hodgson. Over many months they were interviewed by family members, neighbours, journalists and paranormal investigators while remaining at the centre of intense public attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

Supporters of the case argue that the volume of witnesses and recordings makes it difficult to dismiss entirely. Critics point out that some incidents were admitted to be fabricated and that many observations occurred under conditions vulnerable to expectation and interpretation. Even investigators who believed much of the case was genuine sometimes expressed doubts about portions of the children's accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

The significance of Enfield for this topic is methodological rather than paranormal. It demonstrates how a child-centred haunting case can evolve through months of questioning, retelling and interpretation. Once a child becomes the focal witness, the haunting narrative and the interview process become difficult to separate.

## The Main Lesson for Evaluating Child Poltergeist Testimony

Research on child witnesses does not support the simple claim that children are unreliable. In fact, children often provide valuable testimony when questioned appropriately. The more important lesson is that the method of questioning matters enormously. Structured, open-ended interviewing helps preserve what a child actually remembers, whereas repeated, leading or expectation-driven questioning can gradually reshape an account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2180422/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[Griffith Research Repository+3PMC+3OJJDP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Structured forensic interview protocols improve the quality and...by ME Lamb · 2007 · Cited by 1164 — Use of the structured NICHD Pro...</span></span></span>
