## Introduction

Claims of electronic voice phenomena (EVP) often depend on the idea that a faint sound hidden in static or background noise is a genuine disembodied voice. One of the most important ways researchers have tested this claim is by removing the haunted location entirely and asking people to listen to recordings that contain only random noise. If listeners still report hearing words, whispers or messages, then at least some EVP experiences can be explained without invoking ghosts.

These experiments do not attempt to prove that all EVP reports are mistaken. Instead, they examine a simpler question: how often do people perceive speech when no speech is actually present? The results have been influential because they show that voice-like experiences can emerge from ordinary psychological and perceptual processes, especially when listeners expect to hear something meaningful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/predictors-of-hearing-electronic-voice-phenomena-in-random-noise-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">predictors of hearing electronic voice phenomena in random noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dersfield Research PortalSchizotypy, fantasy proneness, and paranormal beliefs1 Mar 2020 — This study used a modified White Christmas...</span></span></span>

## Can Random Noise Sound Like Ghost Voices?

Researchers studying EVP have adapted laboratory techniques originally designed to investigate false perceptions. Participants are typically told about EVP, [ghost voices]({{ 'ghost-voices/' | relative_url }}) or hidden messages and are then asked to listen carefully to recordings containing white noise, pink noise or other ambiguous sounds.

The logic is straightforward. If a listener reports hearing words in a recording that contains no speech, the report cannot be evidence of an external voice. Instead, it demonstrates the brain's tendency to organise ambiguous sensory information into familiar patterns. This process is commonly called auditory [pareidolia]({{ 'pareidolia/' | relative_url }}): the perception of meaningful speech within unclear or random sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 52 — We compared perceptions fo...</span></span></span>

In the context of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), these experiments provide a useful control condition. Rather than asking whether a specific haunted building contains spirits, they ask whether the listening process itself can generate convincing voice experiences.

### What Random Noise Listening Tasks Test

Random-noise EVP studies are designed to measure several related effects:

* **Speech detection bias**: whether people report hearing voices where none exist.
* **[Expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) effects**: whether prior information about ghosts or EVP increases reports.
* **Confidence levels**: how certain listeners feel about what they heard.
* **Individual differences**: whether belief systems or personality traits influence perception.

Many studies use deliberately ambiguous audio because real-world [EVP recordings]({{ 'evp-recordings/' | relative_url }}) are often similarly unclear. Researchers are therefore testing the exact situation that ghost hunters frequently encounter: trying to identify speech in noisy, low-quality recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 52 — We compared perceptions fo...</span></span></span>

## The 36 Per Cent EVP Report Finding

One of the most frequently cited findings comes from a study that used a modified version of the well-known "White Christmas" false-perception paradigm. Participants first learned about EVP and then listened to an audio track consisting only of combined white and pink noise. They were instructed to indicate whenever they believed they heard an EVP message.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/predictors-of-hearing-electronic-voice-phenomena-in-random-noise-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">predictors of hearing electronic voice phenomena in random noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dersfield Research PortalSchizotypy, fantasy proneness, and paranormal beliefs1 Mar 2020 — This study used a modified White Christmas...</span></span></span>

The result was striking: 39 of 107 participants—about 36 per cent—reported hearing EVP despite the absence of any actual voice embedded in the recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/predictors-of-hearing-electronic-voice-phenomena-in-random-noise-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">predictors of hearing electronic voice phenomena in random noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dersfield Research PortalSchizotypy, fantasy proneness, and paranormal beliefs1 Mar 2020 — This study used a modified White Christmas...</span></span></span>

For haunting research, the importance of this finding lies less in the exact percentage than in what it demonstrates. More than one-third of listeners experienced what felt like a voice event when exposed only to noise. The study also found links between these reports and factors such as paranormal belief and related psychological traits, suggesting that expectation and interpretation play a substantial role in perceived EVP experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/predictors-of-hearing-electronic-voice-phenomena-in-random-noise-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">predictors of hearing electronic voice phenomena in random noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dersfield Research PortalSchizotypy, fantasy proneness, and paranormal beliefs1 Mar 2020 — This study used a modified White Christmas...</span></span></span>

The finding helps explain why ghost investigators sometimes disagree about recordings. If listeners can perceive voices in pure noise under laboratory conditions, then disagreement over ambiguous EVP clips from allegedly haunted locations becomes easier to understand.

## Why Listeners Hear Messages That Are Not There

The human auditory system evolved to detect speech quickly and efficiently. Missing an important voice can carry social costs, so perception tends to favour finding potential speech patterns even when the signal is weak.

This tendency becomes especially powerful when three conditions occur together:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Ambiguous sound** – static, hiss, interference or environmental noise.
2. **Expectation** – the listener believes a voice may be present.
3. **Pattern completion** – the brain fills gaps in incomplete information.

</div>

Studies of auditory pareidolia have repeatedly shown that contextual cues influence what people hear. When listeners are told that a recording may contain a paranormal voice, they become more likely to report hearing one. Experimental work comparing EVP recordings, degraded speech and noise has found that suggestion and framing can significantly alter voice perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2michaelanees.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 52 — We compared perceptions fo...</span></span></span>

A familiar example occurs when someone is shown a subtitle before hearing a noisy recording. Once a phrase has been suggested, many listeners suddenly hear the same words. The effect can feel compelling even though the underlying audio has not changed.

## What the Result Does and Does Not Prove

The random-noise experiments are sometimes misunderstood by both sceptics and believers.

What they **do show** is that apparently meaningful voices can be reported under conditions where no actual speech exists. They demonstrate that false-positive voice detections are common enough to be expected and that expectation can influence perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/predictors-of-hearing-electronic-voice-phenomena-in-random-noise-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">predictors of hearing electronic voice phenomena in random noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dersfield Research PortalSchizotypy, fantasy proneness, and paranormal beliefs1 Mar 2020 — This study used a modified White Christmas...</span></span></span>

What they **do not show** is that every EVP recording is automatically explained. A laboratory demonstration of false voice perception does not analyse every individual case. ## Why Noise Experiments Matter for EVP Claims

Random-noise EVP studies are valuable because they isolate the listening process itself. Haunted buildings, ghost stories and emotional expectations are removed, leaving only the interaction between sound and perception.

The central lesson is not that listeners are dishonest or irrational. Participants in these studies generally report what they sincerely believe they heard. The evidence instead suggests that hearing a voice is not always reliable proof that a voice was present. In a field where many recordings consist of brief, unclear sounds buried in static, that distinction is crucial.
