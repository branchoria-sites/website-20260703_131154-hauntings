## Introduction

When believers and sceptics stand in the same allegedly haunted room, they do not always have completely different experiences. Research suggests that both groups can notice unusual sounds, cold patches, feelings of unease, fleeting visual impressions or the sense that something is “not quite right”. The major difference often emerges at the next stage: how those experiences are interpreted. Believers are generally more likely to describe an anomaly as evidence of a ghost, while sceptics are more likely to search for environmental, psychological or ordinary explanations. This distinction is important because some of the strongest [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) research has found that unusual experiences and paranormal interpretations are not the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prior to visiting...Read more...</span></span></span>

## What Hampton Court Found About Belief

One of the most frequently cited investigations of haunted locations took place at the supposedly haunted areas of [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace in England. Researchers led by Richard Wiseman compared people who already believed in ghosts with those who did not. Participants walked through the same locations and recorded any unusual experiences.

The results revealed a clear belief gap. Believers reported significantly more anomalous experiences than disbelievers and were more likely to attribute those experiences to ghosts. The difference remained notable even though both groups moved through the same environment. The study also examined suggestion by varying what participants were told beforehand, finding that expectations interacted with existing beliefs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prior to visiting...Read more...</span></span></span>

What makes the Hampton Court findings particularly useful is that they separate two questions that are often merged in popular ghost stories:

1. Did something unusual happen?
2. What caused it?

The research suggested that believers were more likely to answer “yes” to both questions. Sceptics, however, were less likely to report anomalies and much less likely to interpret them as paranormal even when they did notice something unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Prior to visiting...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Noticing an Anomaly Versus Explaining It

A common assumption is that sceptics simply experience nothing strange. The evidence does not support such a simple division.

Studies of hauntings and anomalous experiences repeatedly show that many reports begin with ambiguous events: a noise, a shadow, a sensation of presence, dizziness, chills or an unexplained feeling of apprehension. Such experiences can occur in [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}), unfamiliar environments and emotionally charged settings regardless of a person's worldview.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216229/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCCase Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by J Houran · 2022 · Cited by 16 — To clarify, poltergeist disturbances are clusters of anomalous subjective (S) experiences (e.g., ap...</span></span></span>

The key difference is often interpretive.

* A believer may hear unexplained [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) and consider a supernatural source plausible.

* A sceptic may hear the same sound and assume building movement, echoes or another visitor.
* A believer may experience a sudden chill and connect it to a reported haunting.
* A sceptic may think of airflow, temperature variation or heightened attention.

In this sense, belief acts less like a switch that creates experiences from nothing and more like a framework for assigning meaning to uncertain events. Researchers in anomalistic psychology—the study of unusual experiences without assuming paranormal causes—have long argued that interpretation is a crucial stage in haunting reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/paranormal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association+2Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Psychological AssociationSpeaking of Psychology: Ghosts, Ouija boards, and ESPHe studies the psychology of paranormal beliefs an...</span></span></span>

## Why Scepticism Does Not Erase Strange Feelings

Scepticism does not make a person immune to unsettling sensations. Several strands of research indicate that people can experience feelings of presence, disorientation, anxiety or odd bodily sensations without believing in ghosts.

Experiments that attempted to create “haunted” environments in controlled conditions found that participants sometimes reported unusual experiences regardless of the manipulated environmental factors. Reports included dizziness, sensed presences and feelings of detachment. Importantly, the strongest predictor of reporting such experiences was often the participant's prior paranormal belief rather than the physical manipulation itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010945208001299" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An attempt to build a “haunted” room by manipulating...by CC French · 2009 · Cited by 85 — Furthermore, Persinger (1985) cl...</span></span></span>

This does not mean sceptics never report anything unusual. Rather, it suggests that the same sensations may be filtered differently. A sceptic might describe feeling uneasy, while a believer might describe the identical feeling as evidence of an unseen entity.

Research on paranormal belief has also found that believers tend to show a greater tendency toward false-positive detections of agency or meaningful patterns in ambiguous situations. In practical terms, when presented with uncertain sensory information, believers may be somewhat more likely to perceive a presence or intentional source behind it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9067702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal beliefs and cognitive function: A systematic review...by CE Dean · 2022 · Cited by 67 — In the 11 studies exploring cognit...</span></span></span>

## The Shared Experience Hidden Beneath the Disagreement

One reason debates about hauntings often become polarised is that believers and sceptics may focus on different parts of the same event.

The believer may emphasise the emotional impact:

> “I felt a presence in the room.”

The sceptic may emphasise the uncertainty:

> “I felt something strange, but I do not know what caused it.”

From a research perspective, both statements can describe the same underlying experience. The disagreement concerns explanation rather than necessarily the raw sensation itself. This distinction appears repeatedly in studies of hauntings, sensed presences and other anomalous experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216229/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Psychology Today]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCCase Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by J Houran · 2022 · Cited by 16 — To clarify, poltergeist disturbances are clusters of anomalous subjective (S) experiences (e.g., ap...</span></span></span>

That helps explain why investigations of reputedly haunted locations often record unusual experiences from a range of visitors while finding substantial variation in paranormal interpretations. ## What the Belief Gap Suggests About Hauntings

The most important lesson from studies comparing believers and sceptics is that researchers increasingly treat experience and explanation as separate questions. People can share an environment, notice similar anomalies and still reach very different conclusions about what happened.

Hampton Court Palace remains a prominent example. Believers and sceptics walked through the same allegedly haunted spaces, yet believers reported more anomalies and were more likely to attribute them to ghosts. The findings do not prove or disprove hauntings.
