## Introduction

Many people who sense the presence of a deceased loved one find the experience comforting, reassuring, or emotionally meaningful. Research consistently shows that bereavement-related experiences such as hearing a familiar voice, feeling someone nearby, or briefly seeing the deceased are common and are not, by themselves, evidence of mental illness. However, not every experience remains comforting. For some people, what initially feels like a welcome connection gradually becomes frightening, confusing, overwhelming, or disruptive to everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13576275.2026.2690491" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineSensory experiences of the deceased in bereavementby P Sabucedo · 2026 — Current studies estimate that between 50%...</span></span></span>

When grief-related experiences produce persistent fear, interfere with daily functioning, intensify emotional suffering, or contribute to social withdrawal, they may require support regardless of how the individual interprets them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32533614/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2BPS Psychology Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A survey on psychotherapy practice with the ambivalent-to...by P Sabucedo · 2021 · Cited by 32 — When experiences of presence go a...</span></span></span>

## Signs an Experience Is Becoming Distressing

A bereavement experience can become troubling in several different ways. The shift is often gradual rather than dramatic.

One common pattern is increasing fear. A person may initially feel comforted by sensing a deceased spouse's presence but later become frightened if the experiences seem unpredictable, occur at night, or are interpreted as signs that something is wrong. Anxiety can become attached to specific rooms, objects, anniversaries, or times of day. The emotional tone changes from connection to apprehension.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The phenomenology and impact of hallucinations concerning...by E Elsaesser · 2021 · Cited by 55 — People who have suffered the loss o...</span></span></span>

Another warning sign is growing disruption of daily life. The experience itself may not be the problem; the difficulty arises when someone becomes unable to sleep, avoids parts of the home, struggles to work, withdraws from relationships, or feels compelled to organise life around the experiences. Mental health organisations that distinguish normal grief from prolonged grief consistently emphasise impairment in everyday functioning as a key concern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychiatry.org">[American Psychiatric Association+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychiatry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Psychiatric AssociationProlonged Grief DisorderAn individual with prolonged grief disorder (PGD) may experience intense longing...</span></span></span>

Other indicators that support may be useful include:

## When Grief and Fear Start Reinforcing Each Other

A troubling cycle can develop when grief and fear begin feeding one another.

The bereaved person may experience something unusual—a voice, a presence, a vivid encounter on waking—and become alarmed. That alarm increases vigilance. Heightened vigilance can make ordinary sensations, memories, and environmental cues feel more significant. The person then becomes increasingly focused on detecting signs of the deceased, which can intensify emotional reactions and reinforce fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The phenomenology and impact of hallucinations concerning...by E Elsaesser · 2021 · Cited by 55 — People who have suffered the loss o...</span></span></span>

Researchers who study distressing “sensed presence” experiences have found that suffering often arises not only from the perception itself but from uncertainty about what it means. Is it a comforting continuation of a bond? A sign of psychological deterioration? A supernatural warning? A symptom of unresolved grief? Ambiguity can become emotionally exhausting, especially when no safe space exists to discuss it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32533614/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2BPS Psychology Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A survey on psychotherapy practice with the ambivalent-to...by P Sabucedo · 2021 · Cited by 32 — When experiences of presence go a...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty may be particularly strong after sudden, traumatic, violent, or highly unexpected deaths. In such situations, grief, shock, and attempts to make sense of the loss can intertwine, making unusual experiences more emotionally charged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bridgestorecovery.com/complicated-grief/signs-complicated-grief-disorder/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bridgestorecovery.com">[Bridges to Recovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bridgestorecovery.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bridges to RecoveryWhat Are the Signs of Complicated Grief Disorder?Experiencing more than one death within a short period of time · Bein...</span></span></span>

## How Silence and Stigma Can Worsen Fear

One reason frightening grief experiences can become more disruptive is that many people are reluctant to talk about them.

Bereaved individuals often worry that describing a sensed presence, voice, or apparition will lead others to assume they are mentally ill. Historically, terms such as “hallucination” have carried strong pathological connotations, even though bereavement-related experiences frequently occur in otherwise healthy people. This fear of judgement can encourage secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/grief-hallucinations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bps.org.uk">[British Psychological Society+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bps.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">grief hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Psychological SocietyGrief hallucinations &#124; BPS23 Mar 2026 — Grief hallucinations are a good example: there is absolutely no link...</span></span></span>

Silence can have several consequences:

* The person receives no reassurance that such experiences are relatively common in bereavement.
* Distressing interpretations go unchallenged.
* Anxiety becomes private and self-reinforcing.
* The individual may become increasingly isolated during a period when support is most needed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/mental-health/mental-illnesses-and-mental-health-problems/bereavement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcpsych.ac.uk">[www.rcpsych.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcpsych.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">is a distressing but common experience. ## Supportive Responses That Do Not Dismiss the Experience

A common mistake is to force a choice between two extremes: either declaring the experience supernatural or insisting that it is “just in your head”.

Many grief specialists favour a more practical approach. The immediate question is whether the experience is helping, harming, or confusing the bereaved person. Supportive responses focus on understanding the person's emotional reaction rather than arguing over the ultimate nature of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The phenomenology and impact of hallucinations concerning...by E Elsaesser · 2021 · Cited by 55 — People who have suffered the loss o...</span></span></span>

Helpful responses often include:

* Listening without ridicule or sensationalism.
* Acknowledging that bereavement-related presence experiences are common.
* Exploring how the experience affects daily life.
* Distinguishing comfort, uncertainty, and fear rather than treating all experiences alike.
* Addressing sleep problems, anxiety, trauma symptoms, or social isolation when present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/mental-health/mental-illnesses-and-mental-health-problems/bereavement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcpsych.ac.uk">[www.rcpsych.ac.uk+2HA 香港医院管理局]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcpsych.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">is a distressing but common experience. ## When Professional Help Becomes Important

Most grief-related presence experiences do not require psychiatric treatment. Nevertheless, there are situations where professional support is appropriate.

Help may be especially valuable when grief remains intensely disruptive over long periods, when the person cannot re-engage with life, or when experiences become part of a broader pattern of severe anxiety, depression, trauma reactions, or prolonged grief disorder. Clinical definitions of prolonged grief emphasise persistent distress and significant impairment in work, relationships, or daily functioning rather than unusual experiences alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychiatry.org">[American Psychiatric Association+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychiatry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Psychiatric AssociationProlonged Grief DisorderAn individual with prolonged grief disorder (PGD) may experience intense longing...</span></span></span>

These situations fall outside the typical range of bereavement experiences and require immediate professional attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/hallucinations-hearing-voices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hallucinations and hearing voices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">February 3, 2021 — Immediate action required: Call 999 or go to A&amp;E now if: · want to harm yourself or someone else · hear voices telling...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 3, 2021</span></span></span>
