## Introduction

A cold spot is one of the most commonly reported signs of a haunting. Yet the moment when a patch of cool air becomes a “ghostly presence” often depends on what people have been told before they enter the room. In locations with a reputation for [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), visitors frequently arrive expecting unusual sensations. When they then encounter a sudden chill—a common feature of [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }})—the experience can feel personally meaningful and even supernatural. Research on haunted locations suggests that suggestion and prior belief can influence both what people notice and how they interpret it. The cold air is real; the question is how it is labelled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242695933_An_investigation_into_the_alleged_haunting_of_Hampton_Court_Palace_Psychological_variables_and_magnetic_fields" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Hertfordshire Research Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) An investigation into the alleged haunting of</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) An investigation into the alleged haunting of ...December 1, 2002 — Hampton Court Palace is reputed to be one of the mo...</span></span></span>

## Why cold air feels meaningful in haunted rooms

Cold spots are especially powerful because temperature is experienced directly through the body. Unlike a strange sound that might be questioned or a shadow that might be reinterpreted, a chill feels immediate and personal. People often describe it as though something has moved through the room or stood beside them.

When visitors have already heard stories about spirits appearing as cold patches, the sensation acquires a ready-made explanation. The temperature change is no longer just a physical event; it becomes evidence within a narrative. Psychologists studying allegedly haunted environments have repeatedly found that expectations can shape reports of unusual experiences. People told that a location is haunted tend to report more anomalous sensations than those given a neutral explanation for the same environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistic_psychology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalistic psychology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anomalistic psychology</span></span></span>

This does not require deception or imagination. Human perception works by combining sensory input with expectations. When the meaning of a sensation is uncertain, context helps determine the interpretation. In a reputedly haunted room, a chill may be interpreted as a presence. In an office, the same sensation might be blamed on air conditioning.

A famous example comes from research conducted at [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace. Participants walked through an allegedly haunted area after receiving different information about unusual activity there. Reports of anomalous experiences were influenced by both belief in ghosts and the suggestions participants received beforehand. The study did not show that cold spots were imaginary; rather, it demonstrated that expectations affected how experiences were reported and understood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/242695933_An_investigation_into_the_alleged_haunting_of_Hampton_Court_Palace_Psychological_variables_and_magnetic_fields" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) An investigation into the alleged haunting of</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) An investigation into the alleged haunting of ...December 1, 2002 — Hampton Court Palace is reputed to be one of the mo...</span></span></span>

## Draughts, stonework and heating patterns

One reason cold spots feature so prominently in haunting stories is that old buildings genuinely produce local temperature variations.

Historic structures often contain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Poorly sealed doors and windows.
* Hidden ventilation paths.
* Thick stone walls that remain cooler than surrounding air.
* Uneven heating systems.
* Stairwells, corridors and alcoves that channel airflow.

</div>

These features can create sharply defined pockets of cooler air. ## How expectation changes the label

The crucial [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) is not the creation of the cold spot itself but the creation of its meaning.

Consider two visitors entering the same room:

* One is told that witnesses often feel a ghost pass through a particular corner.
* The other is told that the building has known ventilation problems.

If both encounter a cool patch of air, they experience the same physical stimulus. What differs is the interpretation.

Psychological research on hauntings frequently points to this process of context-dependent interpretation. Expectations direct attention towards certain sensations and provide a framework for understanding them. Once a person has been primed to expect paranormal activity, ordinary environmental features can become evidence supporting that [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistic_psychology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalistic psychology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anomalistic psychology</span></span></span>

Importantly, suggestion appears to work best when the stimulus is ambiguous. A dramatic mechanical draught from an open window is easy to identify. A subtle chill in a dim corridor is much harder to explain immediately. Ambiguous experiences leave room for competing interpretations, and suggestion helps determine which interpretation feels most convincing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalistic_psychology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anomalistic psychology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anomalistic psychology</span></span></span>

Research has also shown that belief matters. People who already accept the possibility of ghosts are generally more likely to interpret unusual sensations as paranormal. ## Why cold spots remain persuasive

Cold spots occupy a unique place in haunting reports because they sit at the boundary between objective and subjective experience. A temperature difference can be physically real and measurable, yet its significance remains open to interpretation.

This combination makes cold spots unusually persuasive. Witnesses are not necessarily mistaken about what they felt. The debate centres on what the sensation means. In reputedly haunted locations, stories about ghosts provide one interpretation. Building design, airflow, thermal properties and expectation provide another. The sensation itself may be genuine in either case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hpdarch.com/haunted-house-or-outdated-design/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hpdarch.com">[hpd architecture + interiors+2Sealed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hpdarch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hpd architecture + interiors Ghosts or Just Outdated Design?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Haunted House Myths...October 28, 2025 — Fluttering Curtains and Cold Drafts.
