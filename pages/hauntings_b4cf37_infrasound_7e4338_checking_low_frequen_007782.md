## Introduction

When a room feels oppressive, unsettling, or strangely “haunted”, checking for low-frequency sound is often more useful than immediately assuming a paranormal cause. Research on infrasound and low-frequency noise suggests that some environments can produce bodily sensations—unease, [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}), irritability, dizziness, heightened alertness, or a vague sense of presence—that people later interpret as supernatural. However, finding low-frequency sound is not the same as proving it caused the experience. A careful [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) asks a more demanding question: does a specific sound source, at a measurable level, in a particular place and time, actually match the reported sensations?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.wind-watch.org/leventhall-defra-lowfreqnoise.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.wind-watch.org">[Wind Watch+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.wind-watch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">leventhall defra lowfreqnoise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wind WatchLow Frequency Noise Report 2003May 7, 2003 — Low frequency noise causes extreme distress to a number of people who are sensitiv...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2003</span></span></span>

The goal is not to “debunk” every haunting claim. It is to separate a plausible environmental trigger from a vague explanation. Good noise checking relies on observation, comparison, and restraint rather than on dramatic conclusions.

## What to Record Before Measuring Anything

Before switching on instruments, document the experience itself. Many investigations fail because measurements are taken without a clear description of what is being explained.

Record:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The exact location where the sensation occurs.
* The time of day and weather conditions.
* Whether the feeling appears suddenly or builds gradually.
* Whether it happens only in one part of the building.
* Whether machinery, ventilation, traffic, or industrial activity is operating at the time.
* Whether different people report similar sensations independently.

</div>

A useful test is to map the experience room by room. If people consistently report discomfort in one corner, corridor, cellar, or stairwell, that provides a specific target for investigation. If reports shift from visit to visit, the connection to a fixed acoustic source becomes less clear.

It is also important to record prior expectations. A person entering a location already described as haunted may interpret ordinary discomfort differently from someone entering without any background information. ## Why a Measurement Alone Is Not Enough

Finding a low-frequency signal does not automatically explain a haunting report.

Low-frequency sound is common. Traffic, weather, mechanical systems, and distant industrial activity can all generate it. Infrasound exists in many places where nobody reports ghosts. Acoustician Geoff Leventhall has repeatedly cautioned that low-frequency noise should be studied carefully and in context rather than treated as a universal explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.wind-watch.org/leventhall-defra-lowfreqnoise.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.wind-watch.org">[Wind Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.wind-watch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">leventhall defra lowfreqnoise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wind WatchLow Frequency Noise Report 2003May 7, 2003 — Low frequency noise causes extreme distress to a number of people who are sensitiv...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2003</span></span></span>

A stronger case requires several links:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* A measurable sound source exists.
* The source operates when experiences occur.
* The sound is strongest where reports cluster.
* Removing or reducing the source changes the experience.
* Alternative explanations become less convincing.

</div>

Without those links, the investigation has identified a possible contributor rather than a demonstrated cause.

## Matching Timing and Location

The most persuasive acoustic explanations show a close match between reports and environmental conditions.

Suppose occupants describe an unsettling sensation only at night. Investigators should ask whether a fan, boiler cycle, traffic pattern, or ventilation setting changes after dark. If a building feels strange only in a basement, measurements should compare the basement with upper floors rather than averaging the whole structure.

Recent research has strengthened the case that infrasound can affect mood and physiological stress responses. Laboratory participants exposed to very low-frequency sound showed measurable changes in cortisol and emotional reactions even when they could not consciously identify the sound. Yet these findings do not mean every eerie feeling has the same cause. The practical value lies in checking whether a real-world source is present at the right time and place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.com/uk/science/article/bump-in-the-night-pipes-ghosts-h3nx3h3f0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.com">[The Times+2Popular Mechanics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Sensed a bump in the night?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It&#x27;s not ghosts - just bad vibrationsApril 27, 2026 — A recent study published in *Frontiers in Behavioural Neuroscience* suggests that e...</span></span></span>

## How to Avoid Overclaiming a Weak Match

The most common mistake in haunting investigations is moving too quickly from “there is low-frequency noise” to “the mystery is solved”.

Several cautions help prevent that leap:

**Do not confuse correlation with causation.** A building can contain infrasound without that sound causing reported experiences.

**Avoid single-reading conclusions.** One measurement session may miss changing conditions or temporary sources.

**Do not assume a specific frequency has magical properties.** Public discussions often focus on frequencies around 18–19 Hz because of the [Tandy case]({{ 'tandy-case/' | relative_url }}), but real buildings contain complex mixtures of frequencies and vibrations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24236415_A_Ghost_in_the_Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Richard Wiseman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Ghost in the MachineWe show how a 19hz standing air wave may under certain conditions create sensory phenomena sugges...</span></span></span>

**Treat subjective reports carefully.** People vary greatly in sensitivity to environmental conditions, and experiences can be influenced by stress, fatigue, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), and context.

**Separate atmosphere from extraordinary claims.** Low-frequency noise may plausibly contribute to feelings of dread, pressure, or unease. It is a much larger step to claim it explains detailed [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}), [moving objects]({{ 'moving-objects/' | relative_url }}), or complex paranormal narratives. Researchers who study the topic generally make this distinction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/infrasound/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Infrasound</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaInfrasound - Psi Encyclopedia5 Jun 2015 — Very low-frequency sound has been proposed as one normal cause of experiences t...</span></span></span>

## What Counts as a Good Noise Check?

A useful low-frequency noise investigation does not ask, “Is there any infrasound here?” It asks a more precise question: “Can a specific source explain the reported experience?”

The strongest result is a repeatable pattern in which a measurable source, operating at known times and locations, aligns with reports and changes when the source changes. The weakest result is simply detecting low-frequency sound somewhere in the building and declaring the case closed.

For hauntings associated with feelings of unease, discomfort, or an uncanny atmosphere, checking for low-frequency noise is a sensible first step. The value lies not in proving ghosts wrong, but in testing whether an ordinary environmental factor can account for at least part of what people are experiencing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.wind-watch.org/leventhall-defra-lowfreqnoise.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.wind-watch.org">[The Guardian+3Wind Watch+3Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.wind-watch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">leventhall defra lowfreqnoise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wind WatchLow Frequency Noise Report 2003May 7, 2003 — Low frequency noise causes extreme distress to a number of people who are sensitiv...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2003</span></span></span>
