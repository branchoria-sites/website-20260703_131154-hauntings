## Introduction

Mary, Queen of Scots is linked to ghost stories because her life gives haunted places an instantly recognisable emotional script: royal glamour, imprisonment, betrayal, forced abdication and execution. The reports are not one neat legend but a family of stories attached to buildings associated with her movements, especially places where she was confined or remembered after death. The best-known example is the Talbot Hotel in Oundle, where a staircase is said to have come from [Fotheringhay]({{ 'fotheringhay/' | relative_url }}) Castle, the place of her execution in 1587, and where her ghost is said to descend the stairs or appear in rooms nearby. Historic England records the Talbot story as a local haunting tradition, while heritage bodies separately confirm the hard historical anchors: Mary was imprisoned at sites such as [Lochleven]({{ 'lochleven/' | relative_url }}) and executed at Fotheringhay on 8 February 1587.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England+2Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGhost StoriesTalbot Hotel is allegedly haunted by the ghost of Mary, Queen of Scots. The interest of these legends is not that they prove Mary is a ghost. It is that they show how [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) borrow authority from famous names. A vague figure on a staircase becomes memorable when identified as Mary. A draughty ruin becomes more marketable when connected to a tragic queen. Her ghost legends sit where folklore, heritage tourism and historical memory overlap.

## What people report

Mary’s ghost is usually reported in places that can claim a real or alleged connection to her life, captivity or execution. The pattern is striking: the ghost is less often a random apparition than a repeated image of a queen still moving through the architecture of confinement.

At the Talbot Hotel in Oundle, Northamptonshire, the story depends on a physical object: the staircase. Historic England summarises the tradition that the hotel’s stonework is said to come from the ruins of nearby Fotheringhay Castle, where Mary was executed, and that the staircase is thought to have come from the castle too. In the legend, Mary walked down those stairs on the way to her death, gripped the banister, left a mark, and has since been seen walking down the staircase. The same account also notes the claim that a picture of her execution has allegedly jumped from the wall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGhost StoriesTalbot Hotel is allegedly haunted by the ghost of Mary, Queen of Scots. Later popular retellings add more theatrical detail. A 2026 report citing local ghost writer Marian Pipe describes the tragic queen as haunting the upper stairs and two top-floor bedrooms, where witnesses have allegedly seen a woman in a long black dress standing at the foot of a bed or gazing from arched windows by the stairs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dailyrecord.co.uk/news/weird-news/mary-queen-scots-ghost-haunts-36638625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dailyrecord.co.uk">[Daily Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dailyrecord.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mary queen scots ghost haunts 36638625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mary queen scots ghost haunts 36638625</span></span></span> These details matter because they show how the Talbot legend has grown from one portable relic-story — the staircase from Fotheringhay — into a fuller haunted-hotel narrative with rooms, windows, pictures and guest experiences.

A second cluster belongs to Mary’s prison sites. Lochleven Castle has one of the strongest historical connections: Historic Environment Scotland states that Mary first visited in 1561 as a guest, but her final stay in 1567–68 was as a prisoner; during that imprisonment she suffered a miscarriage, was compelled to abdicate in favour of her infant son James VI, and escaped across the loch in May 1568 before leaving Scotland for good.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/lochleven-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history and stories</span></span></span> Folklore sources and castle guides commonly turn that traumatic sequence into a haunting claim, saying that Mary’s spirit remains at Lochleven, sometimes with the added explanation that [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}) over the lost twins keeps her tied to the castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thecastlesofscotland.co.uk/the-best-castles/scenic-castles/lochleven-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecastlesofscotland.co.uk">[The Castles of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecastlesofscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lochleven castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lochleven castle</span></span></span>

[Tutbury]({{ 'tutbury/' | relative_url }}) Castle in Staffordshire is another repeated site in the Mary ghost tradition. It is historically plausible ground for a haunting story because Mary was held there during her English captivity; accounts of the castle’s history note that she was placed at Tutbury in 1569 under the custody of George Talbot, 6th Earl of Shrewsbury, and returned there at later points during her long confinement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutbury_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tutbury Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tutbury Castle</span></span></span> Paranormal and local-history pages describe her as the most famous of Tutbury’s ghosts, often alongside other stock castle figures such as a white lady, children or an armoured man.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tastesofhistory.co.uk/post/paranormal-thinking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tastesofhistory.co.uk">[Tastes Of History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tastesofhistory.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tastes Of History Paranormal thinking?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tastes Of History Paranormal thinking?</span></span></span> The point is not that every report is equally reliable; it is that Mary’s name gives an already haunted castle a star apparition.

Sheffield Manor Lodge shows a similar [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}). The Friends of Sheffield Manor Lodge state that Mary spent 14 years in Sheffield between the Manor Lodge and Sheffield Castle, while Green Estate’s visitor information uses the Turret House to interpret the story of Mary’s captivity and the political intrigue around her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fosml.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fosml.org.uk">[Friends of Sheffield Manor Lodge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fosml.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fosml.org.uk.</span></span></span> Some accounts say her ghost haunts the Turret House, but the stronger evidence is for the site’s historical association with Mary, not for the apparition itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_Manor_Lodge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sheffield Manor Lodge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sheffield Manor Lodge</span></span></span>

Other reports are more scattered. Mary is said in some ghost round-ups to appear at Bolton Castle, Nappa Hall, Borthwick Castle, Craignethan Castle, Doune Castle and other places connected to her travels or imprisonment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spookyscotland.net/mary-queen-of-scots/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spookyscotland.net">[Spooky Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spookyscotland.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spooky Scotland Mary Queen of Scots said to haunt many castles in Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spooky Scotland Mary Queen of Scots said to haunt many castles in Scotland</span></span></span> These should be treated as folklore leads rather than confirmed historical traditions. They often share the same ingredients: a named castle, a documented or alleged visit, a woman in black or white, and a claim that Mary’s tragedy left an imprint.

## Why the Talbot staircase became the strongest legend

The Talbot Hotel story is powerful because it gives the haunting a prop. Many ghost stories rely on [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) alone: a cold corridor, a shape in a window, a sound after closing. The Talbot legend has something more concrete for visitors to look at — a staircase said to have come from the castle where Mary died.

That object-story links three things in a memorable chain. First, Fotheringhay gives the story its historical weight: Mary was executed there on 8 February 1587 after nineteen years in captivity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/3237" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgalleries.org">[National Galleries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgalleries.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">art and artists</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">art and artists</span></span></span> Secondly, the staircase gives the hotel a claimed material connection to the execution site. Thirdly, the repeated apparition turns a historical death into a loop: Mary is imagined as forever descending towards the scaffold.

The legend also benefits from a simple visual. A queen in black on a staircase is easy to picture, easy to tell on a ghost tour and easy to attach to a hotel room. The Guardian, in a travel feature on [haunted hotels]({{ 'haunted-hotels/' | relative_url }}), described the Talbot as a place for “celebrity ghost hunting” because the staircase was said to have come from Fotheringhay and Mary was said to have passed down it on her final walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2008/oct/14/haunted-hotels-uk?page=2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Top 10 haunted houses &#124; Hotels</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Top 10 haunted houses &#124; Hotels</span></span></span> That phrase captures the commercial logic without needing to dismiss the story entirely: famous ghosts are easier to sell than anonymous ones.

There is also a useful ambiguity in the wording of many accounts. The stonework is “said to” come from Fotheringhay; the staircase is “thought to” have come from the castle; Mary’s ghost has “apparently” been seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGhost StoriesTalbot Hotel is allegedly haunted by the ghost of Mary, Queen of Scots. They allow the story to be repeated as heritage folklore without requiring the same standard of proof as a documentary history of the staircase’s removal, rebuilding and use.

## Natural explanations to check

Mary ghost legends usually contain two separate claims: that Mary had a real connection to the place, and that unexplained experiences there are caused by her spirit. The first claim can often be checked against historical records. The second usually cannot be confirmed in the same way.

The most useful sceptical approach is not to mock the story, but to separate the layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Historical link:** Was Mary definitely there, imprisoned there, or connected through a reliable object such as documented stonework or furniture?
* **Folklore link:** When did people first begin saying her ghost appeared there?
* **Witness detail:** Are reports first-hand, dated and specific, or are they repeated as “many people say”?
* **Environmental causes:** Could footsteps, cold spots, door movement or falling pictures be explained by old timber, draughts, vibration, humidity, loose fixings, plumbing, guest expectation or poor lighting?
* **Story pressure:** Does the visitor already know the Mary legend before having the experience?

</div>

The Talbot Hotel illustrates the problem well. If a guest sleeps in a “Mary Queen of Scots” room after reading that it is haunted, every ordinary discomfort may feel more meaningful. One hotel review described staying in the Mary room, knowing it was supposed to be haunted, and sleeping poorly partly because of that thought and because the room was warm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/ShowUserReviews-g504024-d270032-r636722405-The_Talbot_Hotel_Oundle-Oundle_Northamptonshire_England.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tripadvisor.co.uk.</span></span></span> That does not disprove every report, but it shows how expectation can shape perception before anything paranormal has happened.

Old buildings also generate their own effects. Staircases creak, pictures shift if fixings are loose, windows create reflections, and historic hotels are full of irregular sound paths. A figure glimpsed near a staircase or window can become “Mary” because the building already supplies the interpretation. In ghost folklore, the name often arrives after the sensation.

Costume is another clue. Mary’s ghosts are often described as wearing black, white or royal-looking dress. Black has some historical resonance because Mary’s captivity and execution imagery are bound up with mourning, Catholic martyrdom and dark clothing; accounts of her final hours describe black and crimson-brown garments at the scaffold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary%2C_Queen_of_Scots" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mary, Queen of Scots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mary, Queen of Scots</span></span></span> But in haunting reports, costume can also be a shorthand. A woman in black at a Tudor site is easier to identify as Mary than as an unknown servant, guest, widow or imaginative projection.

## Why the story persists

Mary, Queen of Scots is unusually well suited to ghost legend because she is both specific and flexible. She is specific enough to carry a recognisable biography: crowned as an infant, forced from Scotland, imprisoned, accused of plotting, executed by order of Elizabeth I. Yet she is flexible enough to be attached to many places, because her life genuinely involved movement through castles, palaces and prisons. Historic Environment Scotland’s Mary trail presents a sequence of places linked to her life, from Linlithgow Palace to Dundrennan Abbey, while Live Borders’ Mary Queen of Scots Visitor Centre in Jedburgh explicitly explores both her life and “the cult that has grown up around her story”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/things-to-see-and-do/mary-queen-of-scots/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That “cult” is central to the haunting tradition. Mary is not just a dead monarch; she is a memory industry. Portraits, letters, execution scenes, prison rooms, visitor centres and anniversary services keep her story visible. A 2019 account of the annual commemoration at Fotheringhay describes a service in the church attended by representatives of Mary-related societies, showing that her death is still ritually remembered in the place where it happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/2019/02/03/commemoration-of-mary-queen-of-scots-at-fotheringhay/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Francis Young Commemoration of Mary, Queen of Scots at Fotheringhay</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young Commemoration of Mary, Queen of Scots at Fotheringhay</span></span></span> Ghost legends grow well in that kind of commemorative soil.

Her story also has the emotional shape that ghost narratives prefer. Hauntings often attach to unresolved endings: murder, imprisonment, betrayal, exile, grief or a death thought to be unjust. Mary’s life contains all of these without needing invention. At Lochleven she was imprisoned, miscarried and forced to abdicate; at English sites she lived under guard; at Fotheringhay she was executed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/lochleven-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history and stories</span></span></span> A ghost story turns those historical pressures into a simple image: she cannot leave.

The legends also persist because they help places distinguish themselves. A ruined castle may be historically important, but a haunted castle is easier to market. A hotel with old beams is attractive, but a hotel with Mary’s staircase has a sharper hook. This does not mean every custodian cynically invents ghost stories; many legends are inherited, embroidered and repeated because visitors ask for them. But Mary’s name clearly adds value. A famous ghost gives a building instant narrative recognition.

There is a final reason: Mary’s ghost legends allow people to approach serious history through atmosphere. Her execution, imprisonment and political defeat are difficult subjects. A haunting softens them into a story that can be told on a tour, in a hotel brochure or at Halloween, while still pointing back to real events. The best reading of these legends is therefore neither blind belief nor flat dismissal. They are folklore built around documented historical trauma, made durable by place, repetition and the unforgettable image of a queen still walking through the architecture of her captivity.
