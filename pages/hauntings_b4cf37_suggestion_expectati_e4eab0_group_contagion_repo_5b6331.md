## Introduction

Haunted-room experiences are often described as deeply personal, yet many of the most influential reports emerge in groups. A common pattern begins when one person reacts to something uncertain—a chill, a noise, a feeling of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }})—and that reaction becomes a social cue for everyone else in the room. Other people start checking their own sensations, paying closer attention to ambiguous events, and discussing what they think happened. Over time, a collection of individual impressions can develop into a shared haunting narrative. Research on haunted locations, séance experiments, eyewitness testimony, and [memory conformity]({{ 'memory-conformity/' | relative_url }}) suggests that this process can occur without deception and without anyone consciously inventing experiences. The key mechanism is social contagion: perceptions, interpretations, and memories can spread through groups, especially when events are ambiguous and expectations are already high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/2334/1/902375.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uhra.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Archive+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uhra.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hertfordshire Research ArchivePublished in Journal of Parapsychology, 66(4), 387-408.April 26, 2005 — by R Wiseman · 2002 · Cited by 72 —...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 26, 2005</span></span></span>

## The First Report and the Social Cue

In a reputedly haunted room, most sensations are uncertain. A floorboard creaks, a draught moves across the skin, or someone briefly feels uneasy. On their own, such events may pass unnoticed. The situation changes when one person publicly interprets the experience.

A statement such as “Did you feel that cold spot?” or “I think someone just walked behind me” does more than communicate information. It directs the group's attention toward a specific possibility. Once attention has been focused, other people begin searching for confirming sensations of their own. This process is especially powerful in environments already associated with ghost stories, where visitors arrive expecting unusual events. Research at [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace found that reports of anomalous experiences were related to prior beliefs and suggestion, showing how expectations can shape what participants notice and how they interpret it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.ed.ac.uk">[Edinburgh Research+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">inburgh ResearchAn investigation into the alleged haunting of Hampton...by R Wiseman · 2002 · Cited by 72 — Hampton Court Palace is re...</span></span></span>

The first report does not need to be dramatic. In many cases, uncertainty is the critical ingredient. When people are unsure what they have perceived, they often look to others for guidance. Social psychologists have long recognised that ambiguous situations increase reliance on social information. Haunted rooms provide exactly those conditions: low certainty, heightened emotion, and strong cultural expectations.

## From Private Sensation to Group Story

### Attention Becomes Shared

Once a group begins discussing a possible haunting, attention becomes synchronised. People listen for the same sounds, watch the same dark corners, and monitor their bodies for similar feelings.

This does not mean everyone experiences identical sensations. Instead, the group's conversation creates a framework through which different experiences are interpreted. A slight temperature change, a moment of dizziness, and an unexplained noise may all become linked as evidence of a single unseen cause. Researchers studying anomalous experiences have described this process as perceptual contagion, in which reports spread and reinforce one another within a social setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216229/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCCase Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by J Houran · 2022 · Cited by 18 — Haunted People Syndrome (HP-S) denotes individuals who recurrently report various “supernatural” en...</span></span></span>

### Interpretation Can Spread Faster Than Perception

An important distinction is that the contagion often involves interpretation rather than raw sensory experience. One person may hear a noise. Another may not hear it at all but may nevertheless accept the explanation that it was paranormal.

This effect appears in experimental research beyond haunted locations. In séance-room studies, participants sometimes reported movement that had not occurred after hearing suggestions from others. In one experiment, roughly a third of participants reported apparent table movement despite the table remaining stationary. Believers in the paranormal were particularly likely to adopt the suggested interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/9078003_Belief_in_the_paranormal_and_suggestion_in_the_seance_room" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belief in the paranormal and suggestion in the seance room17 Mar 2019 — In Experiment 1, participants took part in a fake seance...</span></span></span>

The relevance to haunted rooms is straightforward. If a participant announces that a door moved by itself or that a presence entered the room, the statement itself becomes evidence for others, regardless of whether they independently observed the event.

### Memory Changes After Discussion

Group contagion does not stop when people leave the room. It can continue through later conversations.

Research on memory conformity and social contagion of memory shows that people sometimes incorporate details supplied by others into their own recollections. Over repeated retellings, individuals may become less certain which parts of a story they personally witnessed and which parts they learned from fellow [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tops.12348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2Psychnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibrarySocial Transmission of False Memory in Small Groups and...by R Maswood · 2019 · Cited by 85 — The social contagion p...</span></span></span>

This matters because many haunting accounts are documented after the event rather than during it. A room that initially produced several separate and modest experiences may later be remembered as the site of a single, dramatic encounter shared by everyone present. The resulting narrative can feel highly convincing precisely because multiple witnesses appear to support one another.

## Why Shared Reports Feel So Persuasive

A haunting story gains credibility when several people report similar experiences. Yet agreement alone does not prove that reports were independent.

People often assume that multiple witnesses provide multiple separate lines of evidence. In reality, the witnesses may have influenced one another throughout the experience. The first reaction can affect where others look, what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they later remember them. This creates a feedback loop:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. One person reports something unusual.
2. Others become more attentive to related sensations.
3. Additional reports appear.
4. The growing number of reports increases confidence.
5. Confidence encourages further reporting.

</div>

The result can be a self-reinforcing story that develops organically within the group. Social-contagion researchers describe similar dynamics in many domains where uncertainty exists and people rely on one another for cues about what is happening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02188" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Interdependent Diffusion: The social contagion of interacting beliefs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interdependent Diffusion: The social contagion of interacting beliefsOctober 5, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 5, 2020</span></span></span>

Importantly, this process does not require dishonesty. Participants may be accurately reporting what they believe they experienced. The issue is that the experience itself has been shaped by social interaction.

## How Investigators Can Separate Independent Reports

For investigators examining haunted-room claims, the central question is whether reports were genuinely independent.

Several practices help reduce the influence of group contagion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Record experiences immediately.** Written notes taken before discussion preserve individual impressions and reduce later memory contamination.
* **Separate witnesses before interviewing them.** Independent accounts are more informative than stories produced through group discussion.
* **Document timing carefully.** Investigators should determine who reported an experience first and whether others heard that report before describing their own.
* **Compare specific details.** Independent observations often contain meaningful differences, while socially influenced accounts may converge around a shared narrative.
* **Avoid leading questions.** Asking whether someone felt a presence can introduce new expectations and create additional suggestion effects.

</div>

These procedures mirror methods used in eyewitness research, where preventing post-event influence is essential for evaluating testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25431565/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magic and memory: using conjuring to explore the effects...by K Wilson · 2014 · Cited by 44 — This study uses conjuring to investi...</span></span></span>

## What Group Contagion Explains—and What It Does Not

Group contagion offers a plausible explanation for why haunting stories often grow stronger as more people become involved. It helps explain why reports cluster in social settings, why witness accounts can become increasingly similar over time, and why reputations for particular rooms can become self-sustaining.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01328/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Things That Go Bump in the Literature: An Environmental</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersThings That Go Bump in the Literature: An Environmental...June 12, 2020 — by N Dagnall · 2020 · Cited by 43 — This paper contai...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 12, 2020</span></span></span>

At the same time, the [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) does not automatically explain every reported experience. People sometimes report unusual sensations while alone, and not every group reaches a shared interpretation.
