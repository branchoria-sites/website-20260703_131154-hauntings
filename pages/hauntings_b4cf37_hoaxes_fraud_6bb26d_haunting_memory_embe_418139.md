## Introduction

Not every unreliable haunting story begins as a hoax. Many accounts become more dramatic through ordinary memory processes that occur after the story starts circulating. A witness may initially report a vague sound, an uneasy feeling, or a fleeting glimpse. Weeks, months, or years later, the same event may be remembered as clear [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), a freezing room, or a detailed apparition. This transformation does not necessarily require dishonesty. Research on memory shows that recollections are reconstructive rather than perfectly recorded, making them vulnerable to suggestion, discussion, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) and repeated retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psychology Hub+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BPS Psychology Hub The history of an idea: The misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psychology HubThe history of an idea: The misinformation effect - Loftus24 Dec 2025 — There it is defined as the notion that a person...</span></span></span>

Within the broader risks of embellishment surrounding [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), memory drift is important because it can make sincere testimony appear stronger over time. As stories spread through families, local communities, paranormal groups and media coverage, witnesses may gradually absorb details that were not part of their original experience. The result is often a haunting narrative that feels more coherent and convincing than the fragmented event from which it began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_conformity" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2McGraw Hill Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Memory conformity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Memory conformity</span></span></span>

## How Retelling Reshapes Details

Psychologists have long found that remembering is an active process. Rather than replaying a stored recording, people reconstruct past events from fragments of memory, expectations and later information. This means that every [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}) creates an opportunity for change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10567586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying false memoriesby AG Lentoor · 2023 · Cited by 13 — Memory distortions due to misinformation...</span></span></span>

In haunting narratives, repeated retelling often produces three common shifts:

* **Ambiguity becomes certainty.** A witness who originally said “I thought I saw something” later remembers seeing a figure.
* **Ordinary details become meaningful.** A creaking floorboard becomes footsteps, or a cold draught becomes a supernatural cold spot.
* **Disconnected events become a story.** Separate experiences are woven into a single narrative with a beginning, climax and explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mheducation.ca/blog/series-classic-learning-science-reconstructive-memory-schema-theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mheducation.ca">[McGraw Hill Canada+2online-learning-college.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mheducation.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">series classic learning science reconstructive memory schema theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McGraw Hill CanadaReconstructive Memory &amp; Schema Theory16 Apr 2019 — In the serial reproduction study, Bartlett found that the recollecte...</span></span></span>

This tendency was anticipated in classic memory research by Frederic Bartlett. His studies of repeated retelling showed that stories often become shorter, more coherent and more consistent with familiar expectations as they are passed from person to person. Unusual or confusing elements tend to be altered into forms that make better narrative sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mheducation.ca/blog/series-classic-learning-science-reconstructive-memory-schema-theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mheducation.ca">[McGraw Hill Canada+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mheducation.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">series classic learning science reconstructive memory schema theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McGraw Hill CanadaReconstructive Memory &amp; Schema Theory16 Apr 2019 — In the serial reproduction study, Bartlett found that the recollecte...</span></span></span>

A haunting account is particularly vulnerable to this process because ghost stories already come with familiar cultural templates. Once an event is interpreted as a haunting, later memories may unconsciously drift toward recognised ghost-story patterns: footsteps in empty rooms, [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}) at staircases, unexplained coldness, and spirits connected to tragic local history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mheducation.ca/blog/series-classic-learning-science-reconstructive-memory-schema-theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mheducation.ca">[McGraw Hill Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mheducation.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">series classic learning science reconstructive memory schema theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">McGraw Hill CanadaReconstructive Memory &amp; Schema Theory16 Apr 2019 — In the serial reproduction study, Bartlett found that the recollecte...</span></span></span>

## Suggestion After a Ghostly Label Appears

Memory distortion becomes more likely once a location, object or event acquires a supernatural reputation.

Research on the misinformation effect demonstrates that exposure to new information after an event can alter how people remember the original experience. Individuals frequently incorporate later suggestions into their own recollections and may eventually experience those additions as genuine memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psychology Hub+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BPS Psychology Hub The history of an idea: The misinformation effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psychology HubThe history of an idea: The misinformation effect - Loftus24 Dec 2025 — There it is defined as the notion that a person...</span></span></span>

3. Discussions generate additional details and interpretations.
4. Witnesses repeatedly hear those details.

For example, a witness may initially remember hearing a noise upstairs. After repeatedly hearing neighbours discuss “heavy footsteps,” the witness may later recall footsteps rather than an unspecified sound. The shift can occur without conscious deception because the remembered version feels authentic. [PMC+2PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10829763/)

The effect is especially powerful when people discuss events together. Studies of [memory conformity]({{ 'memory-conformity/' | relative_url }}), sometimes called the social contagion of memory, show that witnesses can absorb details from one another and later report them as part of their own recollections. Group discussion tends to make separate accounts grow more similar over time. [PMC+3Wikipedia+3ResearchGate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_conformity)

This [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) helps explain why multiple witnesses in a famous haunting may eventually provide strikingly consistent stories even if their earliest accounts differed substantially.

## Why Haunted Stories Often Become More Dramatic

Memory drift does not merely add details; it often favours dramatic details.

Research suggests that memories are shaped by existing beliefs, expectations and narrative frameworks. When people try to make sense of confusing experiences, they tend to organise them into meaningful stories. As a result, later recollections often emphasise emotionally significant or memorable elements while losing uncertainty and contradiction. [McGraw Hill Canada](https://www.mheducation.ca/blog/series-classic-learning-science-reconstructive-memory-schema-theory)

A haunting that attracts attention creates additional pressures:

* Journalists ask for compelling details.
* Visitors expect dramatic experiences.
* Paranormal investigators search for patterns.
* Friends and relatives repeatedly request retellings.
* Local folklore begins attaching historical explanations. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10829763/)

Each retelling rewards clarity and drama. A hesitant, confusing account is harder to repeat than a vivid story with a clear ghost, motive and location. Over time, the more memorable version tends to survive while the original ambiguities fade.

This process resembles what researchers studying collective memory have observed more broadly: social discussion can shape and stabilise shared versions of past events, even when those versions differ from what individuals originally experienced. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05627)

## Warning Signs of Publicity-Driven Embellishment

Memory embellishment is not always easy to detect, but certain patterns deserve caution when evaluating a haunting claim.

### Accounts Grow Stronger Long After the Event

A common warning sign is when the most detailed descriptions appear years after the original experience rather than in the earliest records. Memory research consistently finds that recollections become more vulnerable to distortion as time passes and as additional information accumulates. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225701/)

### Later Versions Contain Information Learned Afterwards

If witnesses begin describing historical facts, ghost identities or dramatic details that they only encountered after publicity began, it becomes difficult to separate original perception from later suggestion. The incorporation of post-event information is a well-established feature of memory distortion. [BPS Psychology Hub+2Wikipedia](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020)

### Independent Accounts Become Suspiciously Similar

When several witnesses initially reported different experiences but later converge on a single polished narrative, memory conformity may be a factor. Shared discussion often reduces differences between accounts. [ResearchGate+2Psychreg Journal of Psychology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227602548_Memory_Conformity_Exploring_misinformation_effects_when_presented_by_another_person)

### Uncertainty Disappears

[Early reports]({{ 'early-report/' | relative_url }}) frequently contain hesitation: “perhaps”, “I think”, or “I'm not sure”. Later retellings may replace uncertainty with confidence. Increased confidence does not necessarily indicate increased accuracy; memory studies show that confidence and correctness can diverge significantly after suggestion and repeated rehearsal. [SaskOER+2University of Portsmouth](https://www.saskoer.ca/introductiontopsychology/chapter/eyewitness-testimony-and-memory-biases/)

## Memory Drift Is Different From Deliberate Fraud

The key distinction is intent. A hoax involves conscious fabrication. Memory embellishment can occur among honest witnesses who genuinely believe what they remember.

This distinction matters because many famous haunting stories probably contain a mixture of processes. A witness may accurately remember some events, misremember others, absorb suggested details from conversations, and gradually retell the story in a more dramatic form. The resulting account can become less reliable even though nobody deliberately set out to deceive. [BPS Psychology Hub+2PMC](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lcrp.70020)

For investigators, historians and readers, the practical lesson is that the strongest evidence usually comes from records made closest to the original experience. Later recollections may still be valuable, but they should be read with an awareness that haunted stories often grow in the telling—not necessarily because people are lying, but because human memory is inherently reconstructive and socially influenced. [PMC+2University of Portsmouth](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9225701/)
