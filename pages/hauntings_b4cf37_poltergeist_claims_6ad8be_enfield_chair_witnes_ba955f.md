## Introduction

The [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) Poltergeist is often cited as one of the strongest cases of objects apparently moving without normal cause. Yet one incident in particular—the reported movement of a chair—illustrates a recurring problem in [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) and poltergeist investigations: even when credible witnesses are present, they do not always observe an event from beginning to end. The Enfield chair episode became famous because it involved police testimony and later attracted investigators, journalists and researchers. However, the evidential value of the incident remains disputed because witnesses described a result—a chair moving, wobbling or sliding—without being able to establish exactly how the movement began. That gap lies at the heart of the “witness problem” in the Enfield case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

## The Reported Chair Movement

The chair incident occurred during the early stages of the disturbances reported at the Hodgson family's house in Enfield in 1977. After reports of knocking sounds and [furniture movement]({{ 'moving-furniture/' | relative_url }}), police officers attended the property. According to contemporary accounts, a police constable observed a chair move across the floor. The officer later reported that the chair appeared to wobble and slide, but could not identify the cause of the movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2The College of Psychic Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

As the story spread, later retellings often described the event more dramatically. Some accounts stated that a female police officer, Carolyn Heeps, saw an armchair lift slightly from the floor and move several feet across the room. Affidavits and later interviews repeated this description, making the chair one of the most frequently cited pieces of physical evidence associated with Enfield.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyvshollywood.com/reelfaces/conjuring-2-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyvshollywood.com">[HistoryvsHollywood.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyvshollywood.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">conjuring 2 enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Conjuring 2 vs the True Story of the Enfield PoltergeistFeb 10, 2016 — Police constable Carolyn Heeps (right) said that she saw an ar...</span></span></span>

What made the report unusual was not the distance the chair supposedly travelled but the status of the witness. Unlike family members who were living through weeks of frightening events, the police officer was an outsider with no prior investment in proving a haunting. For supporters of the case, that gave the observation greater weight than many other reports from the house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyvshollywood.com/reelfaces/conjuring-2-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyvshollywood.com">[HistoryvsHollywood.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyvshollywood.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">conjuring 2 enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Conjuring 2 vs the True Story of the Enfield PoltergeistFeb 10, 2016 — Police constable Carolyn Heeps (right) said that she saw an ar...</span></span></span>

## Why Outside Witnesses Mattered

Poltergeist cases often depend heavily on testimony from household members. Critics can argue that family members are mistaken, emotionally involved, exaggerating, or participating in a hoax. The Enfield case appeared different because it attracted a wider range of witnesses, including neighbours, journalists, police officers and investigators from the Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}). More than thirty people eventually reported seeing unusual events, including furniture movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2HistoryvsHollywood.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

The chair report became especially important because it seemed to provide independent corroboration at a very early stage. If a police officer genuinely saw furniture move without human contact, supporters argued, the event could not simply be dismissed as a family invention. This is one reason the chair episode continues to appear prominently in discussions of the Enfield disturbances decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyvshollywood.com/reelfaces/conjuring-2-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyvshollywood.com">[HistoryvsHollywood.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyvshollywood.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">conjuring 2 enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Conjuring 2 vs the True Story of the Enfield PoltergeistFeb 10, 2016 — Police constable Carolyn Heeps (right) said that she saw an ar...</span></span></span>

The presence of outside witnesses also helped distinguish the chair claim from later allegations that were more difficult to evaluate. Reports of voices, levitations and [thrown objects]({{ 'thrown-objects/' | relative_url }}) often occurred in crowded rooms, in darkness, or under conditions where observation was incomplete. The chair incident seemed simpler: a solid piece of furniture reportedly moved in front of [observers]({{ 'observers/' | relative_url }}). That apparent simplicity gave the story lasting influence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

## The Witness Problem at the Centre of the Case

Despite its reputation, the chair episode demonstrates a fundamental challenge in evaluating moving-object claims. Witnesses reported seeing movement, but they did not necessarily witness the complete chain of events that produced it. The crucial question is not whether a chair changed position; it is whether observers could rule out every ordinary explanation for the change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

In many retellings, the police officer described the chair as wobbling and sliding but did not identify what initiated the motion. The observation therefore establishes that something unusual appeared to happen, yet it does not conclusively establish that the movement occurred without physical cause. A witness may see the middle or end of an event while missing the decisive moment that explains it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

This issue becomes even more significant in a crowded domestic environment. Furniture, children, pets, shadows, partial sightlines and momentary distractions can all affect observation. The distinction between “I saw a chair moving” and “I saw a chair begin moving without any possible physical contact” is much larger than it first appears. The Enfield chair story is frequently cited because it seems strong, yet it also illustrates exactly how difficult that distinction can be to establish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.timeout.com/london/blog/five-reasons-why-londons-most-famous-poltergeist-case-is-a-hoax-061616" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timeout.com">[Time Out Worldwide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timeout.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eyewitnesses are notoriously unreliable. ## Why the Case Remains Contested

Believers argue that the chair movement gains credibility from the number and variety of witnesses associated with the Enfield disturbances. They note that police officers, reporters and investigators all described unusual events, and they regard the chair as one example among many apparently anomalous occurrences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyvshollywood.com/reelfaces/conjuring-2-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyvshollywood.com">[HistoryvsHollywood.com+2JERRY ROTHWELL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyvshollywood.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">conjuring 2 enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Conjuring 2 vs the True Story of the Enfield PoltergeistFeb 10, 2016 — Police constable Carolyn Heeps (right) said that she saw an ar...</span></span></span>

Sceptics respond that witness testimony, even from sincere observers, is not the same as controlled evidence. They point out that later stages of the Enfield case produced documented instances of trickery by the children involved, including occasions on which investigators caught them manipulating objects or staging phenomena. Critics argue that these discoveries justify caution when assessing all earlier reports, including the chair incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

Another criticism is that the most dramatic claims often emerged through repeated [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}). A chair that “wobbled and slid” can become, in later accounts, a chair that “levitated” or “flew”. The existence of a genuine observation does not guarantee that every subsequent description preserves the same level of certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

The result is an unusual evidential position. The chair story is neither a simple anecdote nor a definitive demonstration of paranormal activity. It sits in an intermediate category: a report supported by apparently credible witnesses, yet lacking the complete observational record needed to settle the matter conclusively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Time Out Worldwide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

## What the Enfield Chair Actually Proved

The Enfield chair incident did not prove that a ghost or poltergeist moved furniture. Nor did it prove that every witness was mistaken or dishonest. What it demonstrated was something more subtle and important for the study of hauntings.

The event showed that multiple observers, including people outside the family, believed they had witnessed furniture moving in an unusual manner. At the same time, it exposed the limits of eyewitness testimony when the key moment of causation is uncertain. The chair became famous precisely because it appears stronger than many haunting stories, yet still leaves unanswered questions about what happened between the beginning and the end of the movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyvshollywood.com/reelfaces/conjuring-2-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyvshollywood.com">[HistoryvsHollywood.com+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyvshollywood.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">conjuring 2 enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Conjuring 2 vs the True Story of the Enfield PoltergeistFeb 10, 2016 — Police constable Carolyn Heeps (right) said that she saw an ar...</span></span></span>
