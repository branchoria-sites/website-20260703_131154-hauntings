## Introduction

Roman soldier ghost stories are a distinctive strand of British haunting folklore because they often join three things that usually sit apart: a reported apparition, a real Roman site, and a later discovery or interpretation that seems to make the sighting feel more than random. The best-known case is the marching Roman soldiers allegedly seen in the cellar of Treasurer’s House in York, where the National Trust says witnesses have reported figures passing through the cellar walls and where archaeology confirms Roman remains beneath the building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/yorkshire/treasurers-house-york/ghosts-of-treasurers-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_roman_soldier_ghosts_5f87ff-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Roman Soldiers" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The important point is not that these stories prove ghosts exist. It is that they show how [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) become convincing when place, history and perception reinforce one another. A vague figure in a basement is easy to dismiss; a file of soldiers walking where a Roman road once ran is much harder to forget. That is why Roman soldier ghosts remain so powerful in the folklore of hauntings: they feel less like a random scare and more like a buried past briefly becoming visible.

## What People Report

The classic Roman soldier ghost story is not usually a conversational ghost, a warning spirit or a named dead person. It is more often a procession: marching feet, a column of soldiers, armour or weapons, a horse, a trumpet-like sound, or figures moving along a route that appears indifferent to modern walls and floors. In folklore terms, that makes these stories feel “recorded” rather than interactive, as if the witnesses have stumbled across a loop from another layer of the place.

At Treasurer’s House in York, the National Trust gives an earlier report from the time of Frank Green, the Edwardian owner who gave the property to the Trust: a female guest reportedly went down to the cellar for wine and was stopped by a man in Roman soldier’s dress, only to be told that no such person was in the house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/yorkshire/treasurers-house-york/ghosts-of-treasurers-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span></span></span> The more famous sighting came in 1953, when apprentice plumber Harry Martindale said he was working in the cellar and saw Roman soldiers pass through the space. Historic England summarises the account as a band of soldiers, visible only from the knees up, marching through the cellar in rough green tunics and plumed helmets, carrying short swords and spears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The detail that made the York story famous is the lower half of the figures. The soldiers were reportedly cut off below the knees, which later became linked in popular retellings to the idea that they were walking on an older Roman surface below the modern floor level. The City of York Historic Environment Record records Roman features at Treasurer’s House, including column bases and cobbled pavement from the Roman fortress in the cellars, while the National Trust says excavations under the basement revealed the edge of a major Roman road roughly following today’s Chapter House Street.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.york.gov.uk/Monument/MYO1152" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.york.gov.uk">[York Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.york.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on york.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That archaeological setting matters. York was not merely a city with a distant Roman past. Its Roman fortress, [Eboracum]({{ 'eboracum/' | relative_url }}), was established by the Ninth Legion in AD 71–74 and later occupied by the Sixth Legion; the City of York Historic Environment Record describes the fortress as a normal-sized legionary base of about 20.25 hectares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.york.gov.uk/Monument/MYO2015" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.york.gov.uk">[York Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.york.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on york.gov.uk.</span></span></span> York Civic Trust similarly notes that the fortress shaped the later city, with modern streets such as Stonegate and Petergate running close to the line of Roman fortress roads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yorkcivictrust.co.uk/heritage/civic-trust-plaques/roman-fortress/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkcivictrust.co.uk">[York Civic Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkcivictrust.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">York Civic Trust Roman Fortress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">York Civic Trust Roman Fortress</span></span></span>

The York case is therefore memorable because the report, whether accepted or not, lands in the right historical landscape. A Roman military apparition in a random modern basement would be weaker as folklore. A Roman military apparition beneath York Minster precinct, where Roman military remains are documented, has a built-in narrative force.

## Why the York Story Became the Model

The Treasurer’s House haunting has become the template for Roman soldier ghost stories because it contains several features that make a haunting durable. It has a named witness, a specific room, a clear date, a dramatic image, and an archaeological hook. It also has repetition: the National Trust presents Roman figures as part of the house’s ghost-story tradition rather than as a single isolated anecdote.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/yorkshire/treasurers-house-york/ghosts-of-treasurers-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust The ghost stories of Treasurer&#x27;s House</span></span></span>

The building itself helps the story. Treasurer’s House stands next to York Minster, on a site with layers of Roman, medieval, early modern and Edwardian history. The National Trust describes it as built upon remains of the ancient Roman city and later associated with the medieval Treasurers of York Minster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/yorkshire/treasurers-house-york/history-of-treasurers-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span> The Royal Commission inventory, available through British History Online, notes that at least part of the site was built on in Roman times and that a Roman column base remains in situ in a cellar passage adjoining Chapter House Street.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/rchme/york/vol5/pp69-76" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This layering is exactly what haunting folklore feeds on. The cellar is not just an atmospheric underground room; it is a place where the modern visitor can imagine different historical levels stacked on top of one another. In that setting, the famous “knees up” detail works almost like a visual explanation of archaeology: the ghosts are not [floating]({{ 'floating/' | relative_url }}), in the story’s own logic, but walking on a buried surface.

The case also benefits from a good narrative contradiction. Some details in popular versions sound oddly unlike the standard image of Roman legionaries: green tunics, round shields, “scruffy” soldiers rather than polished imperial parade troops. Believers have sometimes treated such oddities as evidence that the witness was not merely copying a schoolbook image of Roman soldiers. Sceptics can read the same oddities differently: as signs of memory, later elaboration, imperfect observation or folklore reshaping the account over time. The strength of the story lies partly in that tension. It gives both believers and sceptics something to argue over.

## Natural Explanations to Check

A careful reading of Roman soldier ghost stories should separate three questions: whether the place has real Roman archaeology, whether the witness sincerely had an unusual experience, and whether that experience requires a supernatural explanation. The first may be well supported, the second may be plausible, and the third remains unproven.

For the York case, the Roman setting is not in doubt. The documented issue is interpretation. A basement can be a difficult place to judge sound, depth, light and movement. Low light, dust, old walls, reflective surfaces, fatigue and [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) can all affect perception. That does not mean a witness is lying; it means perception is not a recording device.

Research into hauntings gives several useful checks. Psychologist Richard Wiseman and colleagues investigated allegedly haunted sites including [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace and the [South Bridge Vaults]({{ 'vaults/' | relative_url }}) in Edinburgh, finding that participants reported more unusual experiences in areas already reputed to be haunted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-alleged-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into alleged hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">an investigation into alleged hauntings</span></span></span> Other research has explored whether environmental factors such as infrasound and electromagnetic fields might contribute to sensations commonly associated with haunted places, including dizziness, a sensed presence or unease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010945208001299" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

More recent reporting on infrasound research has made this point in a practical way: low-frequency sound from old pipes, boilers or ventilation can affect mood or bodily stress without being consciously heard, which may matter in old buildings and basements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span> This kind of explanation does not recreate a full marching legion in a cellar. It does, however, show how a person in the right place, under the right conditions, may experience fear, presence or misperception before any ghost story is added.

The strongest sceptical reading is therefore not “nothing happened”. It is that the story may combine a genuine frightening experience with later interpretation, repeated retelling, local historical knowledge and the suggestive power of a Roman site. Once a story is famous, future visitors do not enter a neutral cellar. They enter a place already loaded with expectation.

## Why Roman Soldier Ghosts Persist

Roman soldier ghosts endure because they offer a very particular kind of haunting: disciplined, ancient and place-bound. They do not behave like ordinary dead people with unfinished business. They march. They follow roads. They ignore modern architecture. That makes them feel tied to the land itself, not simply to a tragic biography.

Roads are central to the pattern. In York, the National Trust identifies the basement archaeology with the edge of a major Roman road; the City of York record also notes a metalled road surface found during excavation in the cellars, suggesting part of the north-eastern Roman route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/yorkshire/treasurers-house-york/history-of-treasurers-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span> Elsewhere, the same idea appears in modern road-haunting folklore. The Guardian reported in 2006 on alleged hauntings along the M6 and M6 Toll, including claims of Roman soldiers, with one witness describing about 20 soldiers moving through the tarmac “as you would through water”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2006/oct/31/britishidentity.martinwainwright" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Roman soldiers march on M6, Britain&#x27;s most haunted road</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Roman soldiers march on M6, Britain&#x27;s most haunted road</span></span></span>

This does not make all Roman-road ghost stories equally credible. Road folklore is especially prone to repetition, embellishment and local borrowing. A famous case like York can become a model that shapes later reports: once people know that Roman ghosts are supposed to march along buried roads, ambiguous figures, shadows or roadside impressions may be interpreted through that pattern. But that is also why the motif survives. It gives Britain’s Roman infrastructure a supernatural afterlife.

The stories also satisfy a strong emotional idea: that old places remember. Archaeology normally reveals the past through fragments: a wall line, a road surface, a column base, a tombstone, a coin. Roman soldier ghost stories imagine the same past returning as movement. The buried road is not merely found; it is used again. The dead garrison is not merely studied; it passes through.

## How to Weigh a Roman Soldier Ghost Story

The most useful way to assess these stories is not to ask only whether they are “real” or “fake”. A better question is what kind of evidence each story offers.

A stronger case usually has:

* **A precise location:** not just “near an old Roman road”, but a named cellar, street, fort, museum, inn or stretch of road.
* **Independent historical grounding:** documented Roman archaeology at or near the site, preferably from an official heritage record, excavation report or museum source.
* **Early witness details:** a report recorded close to the alleged experience, before the story has circulated widely.
* **Stable core features:** the same main details surviving across versions, rather than changing dramatically with each retelling.
* **Awareness of normal explanations:** lighting, sound, expectation, fatigue, publicity, [hoaxing]({{ 'hoaxing/' | relative_url }}) and local storytelling should be considered before the supernatural is inferred.

Treasurer’s House scores highly on location and historical grounding. It is weaker on the central supernatural claim because the famous event is still a witness report, not repeatable evidence. That mixed status is exactly why it remains interesting. The archaeology supports the setting, not the ghost.

## Why These Marching Figures Still Matter

Roman soldier ghost stories matter within the wider study of hauntings because they show how a haunting can act as folk archaeology. The story points people towards buried roads, old surfaces, vanished buildings and the Roman shape beneath modern towns. In York, the ghost story has arguably become one of the ways ordinary visitors first learn that the city’s visible medieval and Georgian layers sit over a major Roman military fortress.

That does not make the ghosts historical evidence in themselves. The apparition is not proof of Roman clothing, troop movement or survival after death. But the story is evidence of how people relate to archaeology: not only as data, but as atmosphere, identity and place memory. It turns a cellar into a crossing point between modern York and Eboracum.

The best Roman soldier ghost stories therefore sit in a productive uncertainty. They are not strong enough to settle the question of ghosts, but they are too place-specific to dismiss as generic spooky decoration. Their power comes from the fit between what is reported and where it is reported. A column of soldiers marching through a modern room is a frightening image; a column of soldiers marching through a room built over Roman York is folklore with foundations under it.
