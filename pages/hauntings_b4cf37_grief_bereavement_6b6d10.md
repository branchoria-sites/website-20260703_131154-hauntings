## Introduction

Grief can make someone sense a ghost, but not in the simple sense that bereavement “creates” a haunting out of nothing. A more careful answer is that loss can make presence unusually vivid: a person may hear the dead person’s voice, glimpse them in a doorway, smell their perfume, feel them in bed, dream of them, or simply know, with striking force, that they are nearby. Bereavement researchers usually call these experiences “sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased”, “bereavement hallucinations”, “sense of presence experiences”, or “after-death communications”. In everyday language, many people call them ghost experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 106 — Bereaved people often report having sen...</span></span></span>

This matters for [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) because grief shifts the centre of the story. Studies repeatedly find that these experiences are common, often comforting, and not automatically a sign of mental illness. ## Reported experiences around Grief

Bereavement-related “ghost” experiences are usually intimate rather than theatrical. They often happen in ordinary places: the kitchen where someone used to make tea, the side of the bed they slept on, a favourite chair, a hallway, a garden, a car, or a room where the bereaved person has just woken. The most common form is not a full apparition but a [felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }}): the sense that the dead person is nearby, watching, accompanying, or somehow “there”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-020-09666-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Link Sensed presence without sensory qualities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Link Sensed presence without sensory qualities</span></span></span>

The classic modern study is W. Dewi Rees’s 1971 British Medical Journal paper, “The Hallucinations of Widowhood”. Rees interviewed 227 widows and 66 widowers in a defined area of mid-Wales and found that almost half had experienced hallucinations or illusions of their dead spouse. The experiences were not limited to women, were often long-lasting, and were not explained by social isolation or known depressive illness. Rees also found that they were more likely after longer and happier marriages, and he regarded them as normal and often helpful accompaniments of widowhood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The proportion of men and women who had these experiences was similar.Read more...</span></span></span>

Later research has widened the picture beyond widowed spouses. A 2019 review and meta-analysis of bereavement hallucinations estimated that 56.6% of bereaved people had at least one such experience, although the estimate varied by study and by type of experience. A 2021 survey of 310 older Danish adults, conducted six to ten months after the death of a spouse, found that 42% reported sensory or quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30273885/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How many bereaved people hallucinate about their loved...by KS Kamp · 2019 · Cited by 11 — The prevalence of having one or more BH...</span></span></span>

The reports vary in sensory form:

* **Felt presence:** the deceased seems nearby, often without a clear sight or sound.
* **Auditory experiences:** hearing a voice, [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), a familiar cough, a greeting, or ordinary domestic sounds associated with the person.
* **Visual experiences:** glimpsing the person in a doorway, seeing them sitting in a familiar chair, or briefly mistaking another person for them.
* **Touch:** feeling a hand, hug, kiss, [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) on the bed, or bodily closeness.
* **Smell:** perfume, tobacco, food, soap, flowers, hospital smells, or another odour strongly linked to the deceased.
* **Dreams and waking transitions:** vivid encounters while falling asleep, waking, or in dreams that feel more like visits than ordinary dreaming.

The language people use is important. Some call the experience a hallucination, some call it a sign, some call it a visit, and some avoid naming it at all. Researchers often choose neutral terms because “hallucination” can wrongly imply pathology, while “ghost” can imply a supernatural conclusion that the study itself has not established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/166741/7/1363461520962887.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Bereavement Can Feel Like a Haunting

A bereavement haunting usually begins with a broken [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}). The mind and body have learned someone’s routines: their footsteps, voice, sleep rhythm, smell, keys in the door, preferred chair, small noises, and the emotional [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) they brought into a room. When that person dies, the world changes faster than those expectations do. For a while, the brain may still be tuned to detect them.

That does not mean the bereaved person is “making it up”. Perception is not a camera recording the world from scratch. It is an active process in which memory, expectation, attention and sensory evidence are constantly combined. In grief, the expectation of the loved one’s presence can remain unusually strong, while real-world cues are often ambiguous: a floorboard creaks, a coat hangs in a familiar shape, a smell drifts in from another room, or a half-awake state blurs dream and perception. Research on bereavement experiences repeatedly notes that felt presence and sensory impressions can arise in this space between perception, memory and attachment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/1534285/Sense_of_presence_experiences_in_bereavement_and_their_relationship_to_mental_health_A_critical_examination_of_a_continuing_controversy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

The attachment element is especially important. Rees’s findings suggested that experiences were more likely after longer, happier marriages and among people who had been parents, which points towards relationship depth rather than simple loneliness. More recent work on “[continuing bonds]({{ 'continuing-bonds/' | relative_url }})” also challenges the older idea that healthy grief must mean cutting emotional ties with the dead. In this newer model, the relationship changes rather than simply ends: the dead person may continue to matter through memory, values, rituals, objects, habits, and sometimes sensed presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scispace.com/papers/the-hallucinations-of-widowhood-40lm105dty" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scispace.com">[SciSpace+2Routledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scispace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the hallucinations of widowhood 40lm105dty</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the hallucinations of widowhood 40lm105dty</span></span></span>

This helps explain why bereavement-related hauntings often cluster around specific places. A bedroom, kitchen, workshop, car or garden is not merely a backdrop. It is a memory system. It contains routines, objects and sensory cues that once belonged to a living relationship. The place can therefore seem to “hold” the person, especially soon after death or at emotionally charged moments such as anniversaries, birthdays, funerals, house clearances and first nights alone.

## Environmental and perceptual factors around Grief

A grief-related ghost experience may be meaningful without being supernatural. The practical question is not whether the person “really felt it” — they often did — but what conditions might have shaped the experience. In bereavement, several natural explanations can overlap.

**Sleep and waking states** are a common first check. Experiences at the edge of sleep can feel intensely real: a voice, a figure beside the bed, pressure on the mattress, or a sense that someone has entered the room. Sleep disruption is also common in grief, and poor sleep can make perception less stable.

**Expectation and pattern recognition** can make familiar cues stand out. A bereaved spouse may be unusually alert to the sound of a key, a step on the stairs or a movement in the room. When the mind is already searching for the missing person, small cues can take on personal meaning.

**Environmental [triggers]({{ 'triggers/' | relative_url }})** can supply the raw material. Old houses creak, pipes knock, heating systems expand and contract, pets move at night, streetlights throw shadows, electrical devices click, and smells can linger in fabric, wardrobes, bedding and rooms. In the context of grief, these ordinary cues may be interpreted through the absent person.

**Memory and habit** can produce momentary misperceptions. People often briefly expect the dead person to call, walk in, sit in their chair or answer from another room. That expectation may be most intense in places where shared routines were strongest.

**Social silence** can intensify the mystery. Rees found that these experiences were often not disclosed, even to close friends or relatives. If people fear being dismissed as irrational, they may keep the story private, which can make it feel more extraordinary and less open to ordinary comparison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scispace.com/papers/the-hallucinations-of-widowhood-40lm105dty" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scispace.com">[SciSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scispace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the hallucinations of widowhood 40lm105dty</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the hallucinations of widowhood 40lm105dty</span></span></span>

None of these checks automatically cancels the emotional truth of the experience. A widower who hears his wife’s voice in the kitchen may not be helped by being told that it was “only” grief. A better explanation recognises both sides: the perception may have natural mechanisms, and it may still feel like a real encounter with the person missed.

## When It Is Comforting, and When It Is Troubling

Many bereavement presence experiences are comforting. Rees reported that most widowed people who had them experienced them as helpful or neutral rather than unpleasant. Later qualitative research has found that such experiences can support meaning-making, provide reassurance, affirm an ongoing bond, or soften the shock of absence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe Hallucinations of Widowhood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The proportion of men and women who had these experiences was similar.Read more...</span></span></span>

This is one reason bereavement hauntings should not be treated as automatically pathological. The British Psychological Society’s discussion of grief hallucinations, and bereavement organisations such as Cruse, both frame sensing the deceased as a recognised and often common feature of grief, even though it can surprise or unsettle people who have not heard others speak about it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/grief-hallucinations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bps.org.uk">[British Psychological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bps.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">grief hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">grief hallucinations</span></span></span>

But the experience can become troubling in several situations. It may frighten someone who believes any hallucination means they are “losing their mind”. It may become distressing if the presence seems angry, accusing or threatening. It may complicate grief if the person feels unable to leave the house, sort belongings, sleep in the room, form new relationships or accept the death. It may also be harder to integrate after traumatic, sudden, violent or ambiguous deaths, where the bereaved person is already struggling with shock and unanswered questions.

Clinically, the key issue is not the mere fact of sensing the dead. It is whether grief remains intense, persistent and disabling. The American Psychiatric Association describes prolonged grief disorder as involving persistent yearning or preoccupation after a close loss, together with symptoms such as identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance, intense emotional pain, emotional numbness, difficulty reintegrating into life, or a sense that life is meaningless. For adults, the DSM-5-TR timeframe requires that the death occurred at least a year earlier, and estimates suggest that 4% to 15% of bereaved adults may experience persistent symptoms severe enough to meet this kind of threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychiatry.org">[American Psychiatric Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychiatry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on psychiatry.org.</span></span></span>

For hauntings, this distinction matters. A brief comforting sense that a dead parent is nearby is very different from a distressing pattern that traps someone in fear, avoidance or despair. The first may be a normal part of grief. The second may need support, not because “ghosts are impossible”, but because suffering and impairment deserve attention.

## Why accounts of Grief endured

Bereavement ghost stories persist because they answer a human problem that facts alone cannot solve: how can someone be gone and still feel present? They give a shape to the contradiction at the heart of grief. The person has died, yet their routines, voice, influence, belongings and emotional place in the household remain. A haunting story turns that continuing presence into a narrative.

This is why grief-related hauntings are often less about proving the paranormal than about preserving relationship. A widow may say her husband still checks on her. A parent may feel a dead child in the room. A bereaved adult may smell a mother’s perfume at a moment of crisis. These accounts can be read as ghost stories, but they can also be read as continuing bonds: ways in which the dead remain psychologically and socially active in the lives of the living.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thelossfoundation.org/stages-of-grief/continuing-bonds-theory-klass-silverman-nickman-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelossfoundation.org">[The Loss Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelossfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thelossfoundation.org.</span></span></span>

They also persist because they are memorable. A normal grief emotion is hard to retell; an encounter has a beginning, setting and event. “I missed him terribly” becomes “I heard his footsteps on the stairs”. “I felt she was still with me” becomes “I smelled her perfume in the hallway on the day of the funeral”. The story carries emotional evidence, even when it does not settle the factual question of what caused the perception.

Finally, these stories persist because they sit in a culturally flexible zone. Religious believers may understand them as signs of survival after death. Sceptics may understand them as grief, memory and perception. Many people hold both possibilities open, especially when the experience feels loving, well-timed or too personal to dismiss. Research cannot prove that such encounters are spirits, but it does show that bereavement can make presence vivid, common and meaningful enough for many people to experience it as a haunting.

    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
