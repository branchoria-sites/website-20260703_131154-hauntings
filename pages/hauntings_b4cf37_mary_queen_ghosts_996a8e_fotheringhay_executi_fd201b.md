## Introduction

The strongest Mary, Queen of Scots ghost stories are not really about where a sighting occurs. They are about where a memory begins. In folklore connected with Mary, the execution at Fotheringhay Castle on 8 February 1587 functions as the emotional and narrative centre of the haunting tradition. Even when reports place her ghost in a hotel, castle, staircase or bedroom many miles away, the story often traces its power back to the final hours at Fotheringhay. The execution transformed a political prisoner into a tragic historical figure, and later ghost legends repeatedly return to that moment as the event that explains why her spirit is imagined as lingering at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_of_Mary%2C_Queen_of_Scots" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2The Tudor Travel Guide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span></span></span>

## The execution as historical anchor

Fotheringhay was the last stage of Mary's long imprisonment in England. She was tried there in 1586, spent her final months within the castle, and was executed in the Great Hall before witnesses on 8 February 1587. Contemporary accounts describe the scaffold, the procession into the hall and the ritualised nature of the execution, details that later generations would revisit again and again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_of_Mary%2C_Queen_of_Scots" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2readingroo.ms]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span></span></span>

For ghost folklore, the significance of Fotheringhay lies less in the physical site than in the cultural memory attached to it. Mary's death was unusual and shocking in European political terms: an anointed queen executed under legal authority. Historians of memory note that the event immediately generated narratives of martyrdom, tragedy and injustice. Those themes later blended easily with supernatural storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/2019/02/03/commemoration-of-mary-queen-of-scots-at-fotheringhay/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">commemoration of mary queen of scots at fotheringhay</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis YoungCommemoration of Mary, Queen of Scots at Fotheringhay3 Feb 2019 — Mary&#x27;s execution at Fotheringhay Castle on 8 February 1587...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1587</span></span></span>

This helps explain a recurring pattern in Mary ghost legends. Reports rarely focus on her governing Scotland, her marriages or even her years of captivity. Instead, they return to the final chapter. The haunting gains meaning because listeners already know the ending. Fotheringhay supplies the emotional script before any apparition appears.

## How death-site objects carry folklore

One of the most revealing features of Mary's ghost tradition is the way folklore follows objects believed to have come from Fotheringhay after the castle's demolition in the seventeenth century. The castle itself was dismantled, but stories claimed that parts of it survived elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fotheringhay_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fotheringhay Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fotheringhay Castle</span></span></span>

The best-known example is the tradition surrounding the Talbot Hotel in Oundle. Local history maintains that stonework from Fotheringhay was reused there and that a staircase incorporated into the building is the very staircase Mary descended before her execution. Whether the attribution can be proven is less important to folklore than the belief itself. Once the staircase became associated with Mary's last walk, ghost stories followed it. Witness accounts and local legends describe her apparition descending the stairs or appearing nearby, effectively transferring the emotional charge of Fotheringhay into a new setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverbuxton.co.uk/remembering-mary-queen-scots/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverbuxton.co.uk">[Discover Buxton+2The Freelance History Writer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverbuxton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Local history has</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover BuxtonGuest Blog: Remembering Mary Queen of ScotsThe staircase is believed to be that which Mary Queen of Scots famously descend...</span></span></span>

In Mary's case, the staircase serves as a physical bridge between a vanished execution site and a living folklore landscape. The haunting is therefore not just attached to a person but to a remembered route toward death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverbuxton.co.uk/remembering-mary-queen-scots/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverbuxton.co.uk">[Discover Buxton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverbuxton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Local history has</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover BuxtonGuest Blog: Remembering Mary Queen of ScotsThe staircase is believed to be that which Mary Queen of Scots famously descend...</span></span></span>

## Why the final walk keeps returning

Many ghost traditions have a signature image. For Mary, it is not a throne room, a coronation or a battlefield. It is movement toward the scaffold.

The image of the condemned queen walking to her execution has survived in historical writing, art, popular history and folklore. Contemporary descriptions emphasised her composure and the ceremonial nature of the procession. Later retellings condensed these details into a single memorable scene: Mary making her final walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_of_Mary%2C_Queen_of_Scots" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2readingroo.ms]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots</span></span></span>

That image lends itself naturally to haunting narratives because it is inherently repetitive. A ghost seen walking down a staircase, crossing a corridor or appearing at a doorway can be interpreted as reliving a final journey. The story requires little additional explanation. Audiences already understand where the figure is going and why the journey matters.

This also explains why many reported [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}) are described not as dramatic encounters but as fleeting glimpses: a woman in dark clothing on stairs, a figure near a window, a presence moving through a building.
