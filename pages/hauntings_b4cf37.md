## Introduction

A haunting is best understood as a repeated report of ghostly or unexplained activity linked to a particular place: [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}), [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), knocks, voices, objects moving, sudden cold spots, smells, or a strong sense that someone unseen is present. The important point is that “haunting” describes a pattern of experience, not a proven cause. Some people interpret such experiences as evidence of spirits; researchers more often examine memory, suggestion, sleep states, environmental conditions, folklore, grief, hoaxing, and the way stories attach themselves to buildings. Public interest remains strong: in 2025, YouGov found that 38% of Britons thought ghosts could exist, while a National Folklore Survey for England found that just over one in three respondents believed in ghosts or spirits of deceased people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/52619-ghosts-magic-do-britons-believe-in-the-supernatural" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span></span></span>

That tension is what makes hauntings durable. They sit at the crossroads of personal experience, local history, belief, entertainment and [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}). A good account of hauntings should therefore ask two questions at once: what did people say happened, and what else might explain why the story became convincing?

## What people usually mean by a haunting

In everyday use, a haunting is not just a single strange moment. It is usually a cluster of experiences that seem to recur in the same house, room, road, castle, hotel, churchyard or workplace. The Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}), founded in the nineteenth century to investigate claims such as apparitions and mediumship, treats “hauntings” as repeated sightings of the same apparition in a single location, with poltergeist cases often grouped nearby because both involve recurring unexplained disturbances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/9-hauntings-and-poltergeists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9. Common haunting reports tend to fall into a few broad types:

* **Apparitions:** figures seen briefly, sometimes identified as a deceased person, a monk, a soldier, a child, a former resident or a historical figure.
* **Auditory phenomena:** footsteps, knocking, voices, music, doors closing or furniture scraping when no obvious source is found.
* **Physical disturbances:** objects moving, pictures falling, lights switching, electrical faults, bedding pulled, or small items thrown.
* **Sensed presence:** the feeling of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }}), touched, followed or accompanied, often without a visible figure.
* **Atmospheric signs:** sudden cold, unusual smells, oppressive feelings, dizziness, nausea or fear.

These categories matter because they invite different explanations. A fleeting figure at the edge of vision is not the same kind of claim as repeated object movement; hearing footsteps in an old building is not the same evidential problem as a recorded voice. Treating all hauntings as one phenomenon usually makes the subject less clear, not more.

## Why hauntings often attach to old places

Hauntings thrive in places already loaded with memory. Historic England notes that ghost stories have long featured in English folklore and often involve figures thought to have died in unusual, violent or emotionally charged circumstances. Its examples include the White Lady of Ordsall Hall, Roman soldiers reportedly seen beneath Treasurer’s House in York, and the alleged ghost of Mary, Queen of Scots at the Talbot Hotel, whose staircase is said to have come from Fotheringhay Castle, where she was executed in 1587.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Ghost Stories &#124; Historic England</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Ghost Stories &#124; Historic England</span></span></span>

Old places help ghost stories in three ways. First, they provide physical atmosphere: creaking floors, draughts, cellars, uneven light, unfamiliar acoustics and layered architecture. Secondly, they offer narrative hooks: executions, wars, fires, plague, family tragedy, imprisonment, scandal or religious conflict. Thirdly, they make ambiguity feel meaningful. A noise in a new-build corridor may be dismissed as plumbing; the same noise in a ruined abbey or former prison is easier to fold into a story.

This does not mean every haunted place has a reliable historical basis. In many cases, the ghost story is only loosely attached to documented events. A named spirit may be a later invention, a merged version of several local tales, or a tourist-friendly simplification of a messier past. But that looseness is part of the cultural function of hauntings: they turn buildings into story-bearing places.

## The strongest natural explanations are usually layered

There is no single explanation that accounts for every haunting report. The most persuasive non-paranormal accounts usually combine several ordinary factors rather than relying on one debunking trick.

Psychological research into alleged hauntings has tested variables such as prior belief, suggestion, location and environmental readings. In one British Journal of Psychology study, Richard Wiseman and colleagues investigated alleged hauntings and examined psychological mechanisms behind reports of ghostly activity; related work at [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace looked at belief, suggestion and magnetic fields among hundreds of visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12803815/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An investigation into alleged &#x27;hauntings&#x27;by R Wiseman · 2003 · Cited by 121 — The two studies reported here explored the psychologi...</span></span></span>

A later controlled experiment, the “Haunt” project, attempted to create a haunted room by manipulating complex electromagnetic fields and infrasound. Participants did report unusual sensations, but the results did not support a simple claim that those environmental manipulations directly produced haunting experiences; expectation and suggestibility appeared to be important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18635163/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms are especially relevant:

**Suggestion and expectation.** If people are told a room is haunted, they may monitor their bodies and surroundings more closely. Ambiguous sensations then become easier to interpret as meaningful. This does not make the experience fake; it means perception is shaped by context.

**Sleep paralysis.** Some terrifying “bedroom haunting” reports resemble sleep paralysis: a transitional state between sleep and waking in which a person may be unable to move and may sense a presence, see figures, hear sounds or feel pressure. Research on sleep paralysis describes vivid sensory experiences and a distinct feeling of presence during these hybrid sleep-wake states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Sleep Paralysis: phenomenology, neurophysiology and treatment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Sleep Paralysis: phenomenology, neurophysiology and treatment</span></span></span>

**Low-frequency sound and bodily unease.** Infrasound, sound below the usual threshold of conscious hearing, has often been proposed as a contributor to eerie feelings. Recent reporting on laboratory work suggests infrasound may worsen mood or stress responses even when people cannot consciously hear it, although experts caution that this does not explain dramatic claims such as flying objects or full visual apparitions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span>

**Environmental hazards.** Carbon monoxide is a serious safety concern because exposure can cause headache, dizziness, confusion and, in some cases, hallucinations. The American Lung Association notes that carbon monoxide has been linked to several “ghost sighting” explanations and lists symptoms including nausea, confusion and sometimes hallucinations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lung.org/blog/spooky-things-in-house" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lung.org">[American Lung Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lung.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lung.org.</span></span></span>

**Memory and retelling.** Haunting stories often improve as stories. Details become sharper, coincidences line up, uncertain sounds acquire causes, and later witnesses inherit expectations from earlier ones. Over time, the story may become more coherent than the original events.

## Famous cases show why evidence is hard to settle

### Borley Rectory

Borley Rectory in Essex became one of Britain’s most famous haunted-house cases largely through the work of psychic researcher Harry Price, who publicised the site in the twentieth century. The case included claims of ghostly footsteps, bells ringing, writing on walls, thrown objects and a spectral nun. It became a model for the modern haunted house: a named location, a dramatic investigator, repeat phenomena, press attention and later dispute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.umbc.edu/specialcollections/garrett/hauntings/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.umbc.edu">[library.umbc.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.umbc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umbc.edu.</span></span></span>

Its importance lies less in proving a haunting than in showing how such cases are built. Borley combined architecture, religious imagery, local rumour, media appetite and a charismatic investigator. Later criticism, including a 1956 Society for Psychical Research report noted by the University of Maryland, Baltimore County’s special collections page, accused Price and his investigation of serious flaws.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.umbc.edu/specialcollections/garrett/hauntings/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.umbc.edu">[library.umbc.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.umbc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umbc.edu.</span></span></span>

Borley therefore remains useful as a cautionary case. The more famous a haunting becomes, the harder it is to separate original testimony from publicity, expectation, later embellishment and arguments among investigators.

### The Enfield poltergeist

The Enfield poltergeist is another revealing case because it contains both extensive testimony and serious reasons for doubt. The events were reported at a council house in Brimsdown, Enfield, between 1977 and 1979, centred on the Hodgson family and particularly the children Janet and Margaret. Claims included knocking, [furniture movement]({{ 'moving-furniture/' | relative_url }}), thrown objects, voices and levitation-like photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

Some investigators associated with the Society for Psychical Research, including Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair, believed the case contained genuine phenomena. Sceptics, including Joe Nickell, argued that key incidents were poorly controlled and that some evidence pointed towards children’s pranks, misinterpretation or credulous investigation. Janet Hodgson later admitted to faking some incidents, while maintaining that most were real, which leaves the case in precisely the kind of contested middle ground that keeps hauntings alive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

The Enfield case also shows a recurring pattern in haunting disputes: believers emphasise witness numbers, duration and emotional sincerity; sceptics emphasise weak controls, incentives, inconsistent testimony and ordinary opportunities for trickery.

## Why people still believe in hauntings

Belief in hauntings is not a fringe curiosity. Surveys repeatedly show that large minorities of people in Britain, the United States and other countries remain open to ghosts, spirits or unseen forces. YouGov reported in 2025 that 38% of Britons believed ghosts could exist. In England, the 2025 National Folklore Survey found that over 60% of respondents had heard a local ghost story or legend, whether or not they personally believed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/52619-ghosts-magic-do-britons-believe-in-the-supernatural" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yougov.com">[YouGov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yougov.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You Gov Ghosts? Magic? Do Britons believe in the supernatural?</span></span></span>

In the United States, Pew Research Center found in 2023 that 65% of adults believed spirits or unseen spiritual forces exist, while half said graveyards, cemeteries or other memorial sites can have spirits or spiritual energies. These figures are broader than belief in haunted houses specifically, but they show why haunting claims remain culturally legible: many people already accept some form of unseen spiritual reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/12/07/spiritual-beliefs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pewresearch.org">[Pew Research Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pewresearch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pew Research Center Americans’ spiritual beliefs &#124; Pew Research Center</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pew Research Center Americans’ spiritual beliefs &#124; Pew Research Center</span></span></span>

Belief also persists because hauntings answer emotional questions that bare facts often do not. They can make grief feel continuous rather than final, turn trauma into narrative, and give communities a shared way to talk about death, injustice or unresolved history. A person may not fully believe a ghost story and still find it meaningful.

## Ghost tours and haunted heritage

Hauntings have become a major part of heritage entertainment. Ghost walks, overnight investigations and haunted-site events allow visitors to experience history through suspense rather than lectures. Research discussed by Goldsmiths, University of London argues that ghost tours can make city history entertaining even for non-believers, because the uncertainty around ghosts creates distance from death and tragedy while still allowing guides to talk about real historical events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gold.ac.uk/news/spectral-cities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gold.ac.uk">[Goldsmiths, University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gold.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gold.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This is why ghost tourism can feel both playful and serious. A tour may include jokes, jump scares and theatrical storytelling, but it may also introduce visitors to plague, executions, poverty, religious violence, industrial accidents or domestic tragedy. The ghost becomes a doorway into history.

There is an ethical tension here. Hauntings can preserve local memory, but they can also flatten real suffering into entertainment. A responsible haunted-heritage account should distinguish between documented history, local legend and modern embellishment. It should also avoid treating every tragic death as raw material for a spooky attraction.

## How to assess a haunting claim

A careful reader does not need to choose immediately between “definitely a ghost” and “everyone is lying”. The better approach is to ask what kind of claim is being made and how well it has been checked.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **What was actually reported?** Separate first-hand testimony from later retellings, documentaries, tour scripts or internet summaries.
2. **How many independent witnesses were there?** Several people repeating the same story after discussion is not the same as independent observation.
3. **Were normal causes ruled out?** Plumbing, heating, wildlife, structural movement, neighbours, electrical faults, sleep states, intoxication and carbon monoxide should be considered before paranormal explanations.
4. **Was the event recorded under controlled conditions?** Photos, audio and video can help, but only if the circumstances are clear and manipulation or misinterpretation is unlikely.
5. **Did the story grow after publicity?** Media attention, tourism and local pride can all strengthen a haunting narrative.
6. **Is there a documented historical link?** A ghost identified as a famous person may be memorable, but the identification often needs more evidence than the sighting itself.
7. **Does the explanation fit the scale of the claim?** Infrasound might help explain unease; it does not, by itself, explain a table flying across a room.

</div>

This approach respects the experience without surrendering judgement. Many people who report hauntings are sincere. Sincerity, however, is not the same as accuracy about cause.

## What hauntings reveal

Hauntings endure because they are flexible. They can be religious experiences, family stories, tourist products, psychological events, local legends, grief narratives, [hoaxes]({{ 'hoaxes/' | relative_url }}), misperceptions or unresolved mysteries. Often they are several of these at once.

The strongest evidence does not establish hauntings as a single proven supernatural phenomenon. Instead, it shows that people reliably have strange experiences, that places shape interpretation, and that ghost stories help communities organise memory around death, fear and the past. The most interesting question is therefore not only “Are hauntings real?” but “What becomes real through a haunting?” A house becomes notorious. A staircase becomes connected to an execution. A city street becomes a stage for forgotten violence. A private fear becomes a story other people can share.

That is why hauntings survive scepticism. Even when a particular case is explained, the larger pattern remains: people encounter ambiguity in charged places, interpret it through the stories available to them, and pass those stories on.

      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }

      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
