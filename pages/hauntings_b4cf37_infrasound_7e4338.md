## Introduction

Low-frequency sound can make some places feel uncanny, but it is best treated as a possible contributor to haunting experiences rather than a complete explanation for them. Infrasound usually means sound below about 20 hertz, near or below the lower edge of ordinary hearing. At sufficient levels it may be felt as [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}), vibration, unease, irritation, nausea, chills, or a vague sense that “something is wrong”. In an old house, cellar, castle, theatre, tunnel or workplace already framed as haunted, that bodily discomfort can be interpreted as a presence, a warning, or a ghostly [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}).

The strongest version of the infrasound idea is modest: a fan, boiler, pipe, ventilation duct, wind resonance or traffic rumble may help produce eerie feelings in some settings. The weaker, more sensational version says infrasound neatly explains [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}), poltergeists and every dramatic haunting claim. The evidence does not support that. Controlled studies show mixed results, and the most careful researchers tend to describe infrasound as one environmental factor among several, alongside suggestion, expectation, lighting, acoustics, memory and [local folklore]({{ 'local-folklore/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.richardwiseman.com/resources/ghost-in-machine.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardwiseman.com">[Richard Wiseman+2Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardwiseman.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Richard Wiseman The Ghost in the Machine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Using the first author&#x27;s own experience as an example, we...</span></span></span>

## What People Report

People rarely report infrasound as “a low-frequency acoustic stimulus”. They report the human version of it: the room feels oppressive, the air feels heavy, the body feels uneasy, or a corner of the building seems to have a presence. In haunting accounts, these sensations often come before a more elaborate interpretation. Someone feels watched in a cellar, nervous in a corridor, cold and nauseous in a back room, or suddenly unwilling to stay in a place alone.

That pattern is why infrasound became attractive to paranormal investigators and sceptical researchers alike. It offered a physical [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) for some of the most common “atmospheric” haunting reports: dread without a visible cause, shivers, pressure in the ears, dizziness, odd bodily sensations, and heightened emotional response. It seemed especially relevant to places with machinery, enclosed rooms, basements, ducts, stone walls or resonant architecture.

The famous modern case is Vic Tandy’s “ghost in the machine”. Tandy, an engineer and lecturer, described feeling cold, depressed and watched while working in a laboratory, and seeing a grey shape in peripheral vision. He later noticed a fencing foil vibrating in the room and traced the effect to a standing wave of about 19 Hz, apparently linked to a fan. In a 1998 paper with Tony Lawrence, he argued that a 19 Hz standing air wave could, under some conditions, produce sensory phenomena suggestive of a ghost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.richardwiseman.com/resources/ghost-in-machine.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardwiseman.com">[Richard Wiseman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardwiseman.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Richard Wiseman The Ghost in the Machine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Using the first author&#x27;s own experience as an example, we...</span></span></span>

The story is memorable because it is concrete. It does not begin with a vague theory; it begins with a room, a person, a bodily sensation, a vibrating blade and a possible source. Tandy later investigated other allegedly haunted locations, including a cellar in Coventry, and the case helped push infrasound into the vocabulary of ghost hunters, sceptics and popular science writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ioa.org.uk/blogs/things-go-bump-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ioa.org.uk">[Institute of Acoustics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ioa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">things go bump night</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">things go bump night</span></span></span>

## How Low Sounds Could Make a Place Feel Haunted

Infrasound is not mystical “silent energy”. It is sound: pressure waves travelling through air or structures. The boundary around 20 Hz is a convention, not a hard wall between audible and inaudible. Acoustician Geoff Leventhall has pointed out that very low sound can be audible if intense enough, and that the idea that all sound below 20 Hz is simply inaudible is misleading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079610706000848" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

The haunting relevance lies in perception. Very low-frequency sound is often hard to localise, may be felt more than clearly heard, and can interact with rooms, ducts, windows, floors and bodies in odd ways. A person may not identify a “sound source” at all. They may simply feel pressure, vibration, discomfort or alertness. In a neutral setting, that may be dismissed as a bad room, tiredness, traffic rumble, a boiler, or stress. In a reputedly haunted building, the same sensation may feel meaningful.

The proposed mechanism usually has three stages:

1. **A source produces low-frequency sound or vibration.** Possible sources include extractor fans, air-conditioning systems, pumps, boilers, pipework, road traffic, wind passing across openings, industrial equipment, heavy vehicles, organ [pipes]({{ 'pipes/' | relative_url }}), generators, or building resonance.
2. **The body registers discomfort before the mind identifies a cause.** The person may feel anxious, chilled, irritated, dizzy, pressured, or physically uneasy without hearing a clear noise.
3. **The setting supplies the interpretation.** If the place is dark, old, historically charged, or already described as haunted, the discomfort can be folded into a ghost story: a presence, a warning, a hostile room, or “the place does not want us here”.

This is not a claim that infrasound creates ghosts. It is a claim about attribution. A bodily signal becomes part of a story when people cannot easily explain where it came from.

A 2026 controlled study gave the idea fresh support in a limited way. Researchers exposed 36 participants to music with or without 18 Hz infrasound. Participants could not reliably tell when infrasound was present, but exposure was linked to higher salivary cortisol and more negative self-reports, including irritability and lower interest. The authors concluded that infrasound may act as an aversive environmental irritant even without conscious detection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42125313/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That finding fits the haunting question neatly, but carefully. It supports the idea that low-frequency sound can shift mood and stress response. It does not prove that infrasound causes apparitions, [moving objects]({{ 'moving-objects/' | relative_url }}), voices, or complex ghost narratives. The Guardian’s report on the study captured the useful middle ground: infrasound may supply “bodily discomfort” to which a haunting explanation can attach, especially when the person is already primed by setting or belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span>

## What Experiments Have Actually Found

The research history is more mixed than the folklore version suggests. In 2003, Richard Wiseman and colleagues staged concerts in London in which some musical pieces were accompanied by infrasound. Audience members reported more unusual sensations during pieces containing infrasound, including anxiety, chills, odd feelings in the stomach and emotional memories. The effect was intriguing because it took place in a public setting rather than in a supposedly haunted building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2003/sep/08/sciencenews.science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Silent sounds hit emotional chords &#124; Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Silent sounds hit emotional chords &#124; Science</span></span></span>

The result helped popularise the idea of “the fear frequency”, but it did not settle the question. Concert halls, music, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), group setting and emotional response are all powerful variables. The experiment suggested that very low-frequency sound can alter experience, not that it single-handedly creates hauntings.

The “Haunt” project, published in *Cortex* in 2009, is one of the most important cautionary studies. Christopher French, Usman Haque and colleagues built an artificial haunted room and exposed 79 participants to infrasound, complex electromagnetic fields, both, or neither. Participants spent 50 minutes in the chamber and were told they might experience unusual sensations. Many did report odd experiences, but the number of reports was not related to whether they had been exposed to infrasound or electromagnetic fields. The researchers found a stronger link with suggestibility-related measures, making expectation a more parsimonious explanation in that set-up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18635163/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That study matters because it pushes against a simple mechanical story. If infrasound were a reliable ghost-making switch, the infrasound condition should have stood out. It did not. Yet the room still felt “haunted” to many participants, showing how easily a sparse, dim, cool, suggestive environment can invite anomalous sensations without a strong physical trigger.

A later paranormal-acoustics review by Steven Parsons also argued for caution. It noted that infrasound had become established within paranormal research as a proposed cause of subjective experiences, but stressed the importance of proper measurement, appropriate weighting scales and acoustic competence. It also criticised the way dramatic claims about infrasound had often outrun the evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/70007424/Infrasound_and_the_Paranormal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Infrasound and the Paranormal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Infrasound and the Paranormal</span></span></span>

The best reading of the experimental record is therefore balanced: infrasound can plausibly influence mood and bodily sensation, but controlled evidence does not justify using it as a universal explanation for hauntings.

## Natural Explanations to Check

In a supposedly haunted building, the practical question is not “is there infrasound somewhere?” Low-frequency noise is common. The better question is whether a specific source, level, timing and location match the experiences people report.

A useful investigation starts with the ordinary features of the building:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Fans and ventilation:** extractor fans, air-handling units and ducts can create low-frequency tones or resonances, especially in enclosed rooms.
* **Boilers, pumps and pipework:** older heating systems can produce vibration, pulsing, rumble, knocking or pressure changes that travel through walls and floors.
* **Traffic and industry:** heavy vehicles, trains, generators, compressors and plant equipment can create low-frequency noise that is hard to locate indoors.
* **Wind and building openings:** wind across chimneys, vents, cracks, windows or long corridors can excite resonant frequencies.
* **Rooms with strong resonance:** cellars, tunnels, vaults and thick-walled historic buildings may amplify or trap certain frequencies.

</div>

The timing test is crucial. If people feel uneasy only when the heating starts, when a nearby road is busy, when a fan is running, or when wind comes from a particular direction, the case for an environmental contributor becomes stronger. If reports occur across many unrelated times and include complex claims such as detailed apparitions, object movement or conversational voices, infrasound alone becomes a much weaker explanation.

Measurement also matters. Ordinary phone apps and standard sound meters may miss or misrepresent low-frequency sound. Infrasound and low-frequency noise require suitable microphones, calibration and analysis; otherwise, an investigator may simply turn a vague feeling into a vague graph. Acoustics specialists have repeatedly warned that low-frequency sound is easy to misunderstand because audibility, vibration, annoyance and health claims do not map neatly onto a single decibel number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/70007424/Infrasound_and_the_Paranormal" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia+2Wind Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Infrasound and the Paranormal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Infrasound and the Paranormal</span></span></span>

The wider public-health literature supports caution rather than panic. A systematic review of low-frequency noise and infrasound found associations with annoyance, sleep-related problems, concentration difficulties and headache, but also stressed that the evidence was limited, especially for chronic health effects. The World Health Organization treats environmental noise more broadly as a health issue linked to annoyance, sleep disturbance and cardiovascular outcomes, though that does not mean every low-frequency rumble is dangerous or paranormal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://postprint.nivel.nl/PPpp6138.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: postprint.nivel.nl">[Postprint]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">postprint.nivel.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nivel.nl.</span></span></span>

## Why the Story Persists

The infrasound explanation persists because it is satisfying in two directions. For sceptics, it offers a concrete physical cause for spooky feelings without dismissing witnesses as liars. For believers, it can sound exotic enough to preserve the atmosphere of mystery: an invisible force, below hearing, moving through a building and affecting the body. Both versions are more memorable than “a mixture of vibration, expectation, architecture, darkness and prior belief”.

It also fits the texture of many haunting stories. Haunted places are often old, enclosed, resonant and mechanically noisy. They have cellars, pipes, draughts, boilers, ducts, uneven floors, thick walls and long corridors. They are also places where visitors arrive expecting to feel something. Infrasound sits at the intersection of environment and interpretation: physical enough to measure, subtle enough to misread.

The danger is overreach. Some popular accounts imply that a specific frequency, often 18 or 19 Hz, is “the ghost frequency”. That is too neat. Frequencies, sound-pressure levels, exposure time, room geometry and individual sensitivity all matter. The visual-hallucination claim, especially the idea that around 19 Hz causes eyeballs to vibrate and produces apparitions, remains much less secure than the simpler claim that low-frequency sound can contribute to discomfort or unease. Chris French has warned that controlled evidence does not support using infrasound to explain visual apparitions or alleged poltergeist activity such as objects flying from shelves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span>

The more durable lesson is not that infrasound debunks every haunting. It is that a haunted atmosphere can be built from small inputs: a barely noticed vibration, a rumbling pipe, a dim corridor, a story told before entry, a suggestible moment, and the human habit of seeking agency in ambiguous sensations. Once a place has a ghost story, ordinary discomfort is no longer neutral. It becomes evidence in waiting.

## What This Explains, and What It Does Not

Infrasound is most useful for explaining the atmospheric layer of hauntings: dread, pressure, unease, chills, irritability, nausea, dizziness, a sense of presence, or the feeling that a room is hostile. It is especially plausible when reports cluster around a specific location and coincide with machinery, airflow, traffic, weather or building resonance.

It is much less useful for explaining highly structured claims: a named apparition seen clearly by several people, objects apparently moving under observation, detailed voices, repeated messages, or complex narratives that depend on memory and testimony rather than immediate bodily sensation. Those claims need different kinds of analysis, including perception, expectation, sleep states, witness reliability, fraud, folklore, acoustics above the infrasonic range, and ordinary building noises.

The honest conclusion is therefore partial but valuable. Low-frequency sound can help make a house, cellar or corridor feel haunted. It may supply the bodily unease that lets a ghost story take hold. But it is not a magic key to every haunting claim, and it becomes less convincing the more dramatic, specific and physically elaborate the reported phenomena become.

    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
