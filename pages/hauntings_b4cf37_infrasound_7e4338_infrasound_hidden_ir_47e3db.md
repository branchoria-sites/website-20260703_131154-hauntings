## Introduction

People often assume that infrasound affects us only if we can hear it. Research suggests the situation is more complicated. Very low-frequency sound can influence mood and bodily state even when listeners cannot reliably identify a sound source or consciously detect a tone. In the context of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), this matters because unexplained discomfort, tension, irritability, or a sense that something is “wrong” can become the raw material from which eerie interpretations are built. The strongest scientific claim is not that infrasound creates ghosts. Rather, it may act as a hidden irritant that subtly alters stress responses and emotional judgement, making an already suggestive environment feel more unsettling. Recent experiments have found changes in cortisol levels, mood ratings, and emotional perception during exposure to infrasound that participants could not consciously hear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://neurosciencenews.com/infrasound-stress-cortisol-mood-30611/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: neurosciencenews.com">[Neuroscience News+2Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">neurosciencenews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">infrasound stress cortisol mood 30611</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neuroscience NewsSound of Fear: Infrasound Mimics Supernatural Feelings27 Apr 2026 — 18 Hz infrasound, though inaudible, spikes cortisol...</span></span></span>

## Can Sound Bother You Before You Hear It?

The common definition of infrasound is sound below 20 hertz (Hz), but that boundary is often misunderstood. Human hearing does not suddenly switch off at 20 Hz. Instead, sensitivity gradually declines as frequency falls. Very low-frequency sounds can still be detected if they are strong enough, and different people vary in their sensitivity. Acoustician Geoff Leventhall has argued that the popular idea that all sound below 20 Hz is inaudible is incorrect; hearing thresholds have been measured below that frequency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079610706000848" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What is infrasound?by G Leventhall · 2007 · Cited by 189 — Infrasound, in its popular definition as sound below a frequency...</span></span></span>

This matters because many real-world exposures sit in a grey zone. A person may not experience a clear audible tone, yet their auditory and bodily systems are still receiving low-frequency stimulation. In [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}), basements, ventilation shafts, boiler rooms, tunnels, or spaces with resonant architecture, the result may be a vague feeling of discomfort rather than a recognisable sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10562056/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluation of Low-Frequency Noise, Infrasound, and Health...by SK Chiu · 2023 · Cited by 6 — Studies have shown noise-related annoyan...</span></span></span>

For haunting reports, that ambiguity is important. Humans are generally good at explaining obvious sensations. A loud bang is attributed to a noise source. A low-frequency [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) sensation with no obvious cause is much harder to interpret, leaving room for supernatural explanations when the setting already encourages them.

## Why the 20 Hz Boundary Is Not a Hard Cutoff

The phrase “below the range of hearing” can create a misleading picture. Hearing thresholds are curves, not walls. At lower frequencies, the ear becomes less sensitive, meaning stronger sound pressure is required before a person consciously recognises a tone. Yet the sound energy is still interacting with the auditory system and, at sufficient intensity, with other bodily systems as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079610706000848" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What is infrasound?by G Leventhall · 2007 · Cited by 189 — Infrasound, in its popular definition as sound below a frequency...</span></span></span>

Researchers studying low-frequency sound have noted that people often describe sensations such as:

* A feeling of vibration.
* Restlessness or irritation.

These reports can occur even when individuals struggle to identify a specific sound source. Reviews of low-frequency noise research repeatedly identify annoyance and discomfort as common outcomes, particularly when exposure is persistent or difficult to escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10562056/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[MDPI+3PMC+3MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluation of Low-Frequency Noise, Infrasound, and Health...by SK Chiu · 2023 · Cited by 6 — Studies have shown noise-related annoyan...</span></span></span>

The result is a paradox: a person may sincerely report that they heard nothing unusual while simultaneously experiencing effects that originated from an acoustic source.

## How Pressure and Vibration Become Bodily Discomfort

The key [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) is not necessarily “hearing” in the everyday sense. Low-frequency sound contains large pressure fluctuations and can be experienced partly as vibration. At levels above threshold, people may feel these vibrations in the chest, abdomen, walls, floors, or furnishings. Even when the sensation is subtle, it can create a mismatch between what the body feels and what conscious perception can explain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrasound" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Scientists have proposed several pathways through which this can affect mood:

**Physiological vigilance.** The nervous system appears capable of responding to environmental signals that never become a conscious sound. If the body interprets low-frequency stimulation as unusual or potentially important, it may shift into a more alert state.

**Autonomic stress responses.** Studies have linked low-frequency noise exposure to changes in cardiovascular measures and stress-related physiological responses. Such changes do not automatically produce fear, but they can create a background feeling of tension or unease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S001393511630250X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">more...</span></span></span>

**Persistent annoyance.** One of the most consistent findings in low-frequency noise research is annoyance. Unlike a brief loud noise, low-frequency sound can be difficult to localise and difficult to ignore. The resulting irritation may be experienced as an emotional state rather than as a reaction to a sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10562056/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluation of Low-Frequency Noise, Infrasound, and Health...by SK Chiu · 2023 · Cited by 6 — Studies have shown noise-related annoyan...</span></span></span>

In a supposedly haunted location, that bodily discomfort can be interpreted as evidence that something unseen is present. The physical sensation comes first; the paranormal explanation may follow afterwards.

## What Cortisol and Mood Findings Add

A particularly relevant line of evidence comes from recent laboratory experiments examining infrasound's effects on stress and emotional judgement. In one study, participants were exposed to approximately 18 Hz infrasound while listening to music. They generally could not tell when the infrasound was present. Nevertheless, researchers observed higher salivary cortisol levels and more negative mood responses during exposure. Participants also rated music as sadder and reported greater irritability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[The Guardian+3Frontiers+3ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">frasound exposure is linked to aversive responding...by KR Scatterty · 2026 · Cited by 2 — This study explored the non-audit...</span></span></span>

Cortisol is commonly called a stress hormone. It is involved in preparing the body to respond to challenges and can increase vigilance. The significance of these findings is not that infrasound creates panic or hallucinations. Rather, it suggests that a person's emotional baseline may shift in a subtly negative direction even when they are unaware of the acoustic stimulus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://neurosciencenews.com/infrasound-stress-cortisol-mood-30611/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: neurosciencenews.com">[Neuroscience News+2The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">neurosciencenews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">infrasound stress cortisol mood 30611</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neuroscience NewsSound of Fear: Infrasound Mimics Supernatural Feelings27 Apr 2026 — 18 Hz infrasound, though inaudible, spikes cortisol...</span></span></span>

For hauntings, this provides a plausible bridge between environment and experience. Someone entering an old building may feel more irritable, tense, or emotionally unsettled without understanding why. ## Why This Matters for Eerie Feelings

The most important point is that mood effects do not require dramatic symptoms. A haunting [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) is often built from subtle impressions: feeling watched, feeling uncomfortable, becoming unusually alert, or sensing that a room is oppressive. These experiences are emotional and bodily before they become narrative.

Infrasound offers a mechanism for how an environment might contribute to those feelings without producing an obvious audible cue. The sound may not be consciously recognised, yet pressure fluctuations, vibration, physiological stress responses, and low-level annoyance can still shape how a person interprets a place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10562056/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluation of Low-Frequency Noise, Infrasound, and Health...by SK Chiu · 2023 · Cited by 6 — Studies have shown noise-related annoyan...</span></span></span>
