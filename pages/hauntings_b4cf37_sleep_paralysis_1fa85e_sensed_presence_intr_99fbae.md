## Introduction

One of the most striking features of many bedroom-haunting reports is not the sight of a ghost but the certainty that someone is present. People describe waking in bed and instantly knowing that an intruder, watcher, or hostile figure is in the room, even before they see anything clearly. The feeling can be so convincing that it seems impossible to dismiss as imagination. Yet sleep-paralysis research repeatedly identifies this “sensed presence” as one of the most common and distinctive elements of the intruder experience. Studies of sleep paralysis consistently find a cluster of fear, presence-sensing, and visual or auditory impressions that create what researchers call the intruder hallucination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10487786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations during sleep...by JA Cheyne · 1999 · Cited by 400 — One factor, labeled Intruder, consis...</span></span></span>

What makes these reports important is that the sense of another being often arrives before any apparent figure appears. The brain first concludes that someone is nearby and then interprets shadows, sounds, and ambiguous details in ways that support that conclusion. This process can transform an ordinary bedroom into a scene that feels occupied, watched, and threatening even when no external person is present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

## Why an Empty Room Can Feel Occupied

Researchers use terms such as *sensed presence* or *[felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }})* for the experience of being certain that another being is nearby despite the absence of clear sensory evidence. Modern reviews define it as the perception that someone is present in the immediate environment even though the person is not directly seen, heard, or touched.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2National Elf Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

During sleep paralysis, this experience often occurs while a person is awake enough to recognise their bedroom but still carrying elements of REM dreaming into consciousness. The result is a hybrid state in which the room is real, the paralysis is real, but the interpretation of danger is shaped by dream-related processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6208952/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Ubie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe neuropharmacology of sleep paralysis hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by B Jalal · 2018 · Cited by 74 — Moreover, like hallucinogenic drugs, sleep paralysis is commonly associated with experiencing halluc...</span></span></span>

Researchers John Allan Cheyne and colleagues proposed that the intruder experience emerges from a state of extreme threat vigilance. A person wakes unable to move, feels vulnerable, and the brain's danger-detection systems become highly active. In that state, ambiguous sensations are interpreted as evidence that another agent is nearby. Rather than asking, “Why do I feel strange?”, the brain rapidly constructs a more urgent explanation: “Someone is here.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10487786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations during sleep...by JA Cheyne · 1999 · Cited by 400 — One factor, labeled Intruder, consis...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why many reports involve a human-like presence rather than an abstract feeling. Human beings are highly specialised for detecting other people, especially potential threats. Under conditions of fear and uncertainty, the mind tends to err on the side of detecting an agent rather than overlooking one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

## Footsteps, Watchers, and Figures at the Doorway

The intruder illusion often follows a recognisable pattern. The person awakens and immediately feels that someone is in the room. Only afterwards do additional details appear.

Common reports include:

* A figure standing in a doorway.
* Someone watching from the corner of the room.
* [Footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) approaching the bed.

* Breathing sounds nearby.
* A dark silhouette near a wardrobe or window.
* The certainty that a stranger has entered the house.
* A presence standing just outside the field of vision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16571587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+3PubMed+3PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spatial characteristics of hallucinations associated with...by JA Cheyne · 2004 · Cited by 58 — We present evidence from a web-bas...</span></span></span>

Interestingly, the perceived intruder is often vague rather than detailed. People frequently report shadow-like forms, faceless figures, or human outlines rather than clearly identifiable individuals. Neuroscientific explanations suggest that when the brain is assembling a threat representation from incomplete information, a rough human shape may be sufficient. The experience feels real not because it is visually detailed but because the emotional certainty attached to it is extraordinarily strong.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306987721000396" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These hypnogogic and hypnopompic visions are common...</span></span></span>

Studies examining the spatial characteristics of sleep-paralysis hallucinations found recurring patterns in where presences are perceived. Doorways, bedside locations, room corners, and positions just behind the sleeper appear repeatedly in reports. These are exactly the places where a threatening intruder would be expected to stand, suggesting that the hallucination is organised around a threat scenario rather than random imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16571587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spatial characteristics of hallucinations associated with...by JA Cheyne · 2004 · Cited by 58 — We present evidence from a web-bas...</span></span></span>

## How Intruder Experiences Differ from Ordinary Dreams

People who have never experienced sleep paralysis often assume that the intruder is simply part of a nightmare. Witnesses themselves commonly reject that explanation because the experience feels fundamentally different.

Several features distinguish the intruder illusion from a conventional dream:

**The bedroom remains recognisable.** People usually report seeing their actual room rather than a dream setting. Furniture, doors, windows, and lighting often appear exactly as expected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6208952/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe neuropharmacology of sleep paralysis hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by B Jalal · 2018 · Cited by 74 — Moreover, like hallucinogenic drugs, sleep paralysis is commonly associated with experiencing halluc...</span></span></span>

**The sense of wakefulness is strong.** Individuals frequently insist they were awake throughout the event. In many cases they genuinely were conscious, though consciousness remained mixed with REM-related processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6208952/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCThe neuropharmacology of sleep paralysis hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by B Jalal · 2018 · Cited by 74 — Moreover, like hallucinogenic drugs, sleep paralysis is commonly associated with experiencing halluc...</span></span></span>

**The fear feels immediate and situational.** Instead of following a dream narrative, the fear is tied to a specific perceived threat in the room. The experience resembles a real emergency rather than a symbolic dream story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10487786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations during sleep...by JA Cheyne · 1999 · Cited by 400 — One factor, labeled Intruder, consis...</span></span></span>

**The hallucination centres on presence.** In ordinary dreams, characters usually interact within a broader narrative. During an intruder episode, the dominant feature is often simply the certainty that another being is nearby. The figure may never fully appear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

## Why the Presence Often Feels Intelligent and Intentional

A notable feature of sensed-presence reports is that the perceived being rarely feels neutral. Witnesses often report that the presence seems aware of them, watching them, or intending to act.

This may arise because human social cognition is built around understanding the intentions of other agents. Once the brain has generated the conviction that someone is present, it naturally begins assigning motives, attention, and agency to that presence. A dark shape therefore becomes not merely an object but a watcher. A sound becomes an approaching person. A shadow becomes an intruder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

In this view, parts of the brain involved in modelling the self can generate the impression of another person occupying nearby space. ## Questions That Separate Sleep-State Presence from Other Claims

A sensed presence is not unique to sleep paralysis. ## The Lasting Power of the Intruder Illusion

The sensed-presence experience remains one of the most compelling components of bedroom-haunting reports because it attacks a basic assumption about perception. People generally trust that they know when another person is nearby. The intruder illusion demonstrates that this certainty can arise without an actual intruder.

Research on felt presence suggests that the experience occupies a boundary zone between perception, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), bodily awareness, and social cognition. During sleep paralysis, those systems can combine in a way that creates an overwhelming conviction that someone is standing in the room. The room itself may be real, the fear may be real, and the memory may remain vivid for years. What appears to be present, however, can be a product of the sleeping and waking brain temporarily overlapping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[Frontiers+3ScienceDirect+3PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>
