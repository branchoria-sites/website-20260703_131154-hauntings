## Introduction

Cold spots, odd smells and a heavy “haunted” atmosphere can make a ghost story feel far more convincing than a distant legend or a single strange noise. The short answer is that these sensations can be meaningful evidence of an experience, but they are not reliable evidence of a ghost by themselves. Sudden chill, musty air, a fleeting smell of perfume or tobacco, nausea, dread and the feeling of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }}) can all arise from ordinary building conditions, air movement, damp, mould, low-frequency vibration, indoor pollution, medical smell disturbances, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) and fear. That does not make witnesses dishonest. It means that haunting stories often begin where a real bodily sensation meets an already suggestive place.

These atmospheric details matter because they are immediate. A person can argue with a story they have been told, but it is harder to dismiss a room that suddenly feels cold, stale, oppressive or wrong. Good [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) therefore starts not by mocking the feeling, but by asking what physical, medical and psychological mechanisms could have produced it.

## What people report

In haunting accounts, atmosphere often arrives before any apparition. A room “drops cold”; a corridor smells of roses, smoke, decay or damp; visitors feel [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}) on the chest, tingling skin, dizziness, nausea, dread or the sensation that someone is standing close behind them. These details are especially persuasive when they appear to match a story already attached to the place: cigar smoke in a former gentleman’s room, perfume in a bedroom linked to a dead resident, a cold patch near a staircase, or a cellar that feels “oppressive”.

Cold spots are one of the most common claims. In paranormal interpretation, they are often described as areas where a spirit is present or “drawing energy” from the surroundings. Ghost-hunting guides and equipment retailers frequently frame [thermal cameras]({{ 'thermal-cameras/' | relative_url }}) as a way to detect localised temperature anomalies, while acknowledging that such anomalies can also come from ordinary environmental factors. Smells carry a different kind of force. They feel intimate and old-fashioned, and they can seem to identify a particular person or period. A smell of tobacco may be linked to a dead smoker; lavender or roses to a former occupant; rot or mould to burial, decay or neglect. Yet smell is also one of the senses most vulnerable to hidden sources and misinterpretation. Damp materials, fungal growth, old timber, blocked drains, cleaning residues, fuel-burning appliances, animals, insulation, dust and stale ventilation can all create striking odours in buildings.

The atmosphere of a supposedly haunted room is usually a cluster, not a single sign. A cold draught is more unsettling in dim light. A musty smell feels more ominous in a cellar. Dizziness becomes more memorable when the guide has just described a ghost. The haunting impression often comes from the way several weak signals combine: temperature, smell, lighting, silence, expectation, unfamiliar architecture and a body already primed for threat.

## Natural explanations to check first

A careful approach treats cold spots and smells as environmental clues before treating them as paranormal evidence. The question is not “did the witness feel something?” but “what else in this setting could have made that feeling happen?”

**Draughts and air movement.** Cold air can enter through gaps around windows, doors, chimneys, floorboards, loft hatches, service ducts and poorly sealed walls. It can also move through a building because of pressure differences, heating systems or the stack effect, where warm air rises and draws colder air in below. A person standing still in a moving air current may feel a sharp chill even if the room’s average temperature has barely changed.

**Cold surfaces and thermal bridges.** A “cold spot” may be a local patch of wall, floor or ceiling that loses heat faster than the surrounding material. Building scientists use infrared imaging to find thermal anomalies such as missing insulation, moisture problems, air leakage and cold bridges; these are real findings, but they belong first to building diagnosis rather than spirit detection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://buildingscience.com/documents/published-articles/pa-1902-how-look-house-building-scientist-part-2-heat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: buildingscience.com">[buildingscience.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">buildingscience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pa 1902 how look house building scientist part 2 heat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PA-1902: How to Look at a House like a Building Scientist...4 Dec 2019 — IR cameras are incredibly useful for scanning wide areas for co...</span></span></span>

**Damp and mould.** [Musty smells]({{ 'musty-smells/' | relative_url }}) are not just a mood-setting detail. Damp and mould indoors are linked with respiratory problems, infections, allergies and asthma, and health bodies advise identifying the source of excess moisture rather than simply masking the smell. UK government guidance also stresses that damp and mould can seriously affect health and should be addressed promptly, especially in homes with vulnerable occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/indoor-health/damp-and-mould-indoors/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhsinform.scot">[NHS inform]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhsinform.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">damp and mould indoors</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NHS informDamp and mould indoors1 Oct 2025 — Damp and mould indoors. **Microbial volatile organic compounds.** The “old house” smell often has chemistry behind it. Fungi and other microbes can release volatile compounds as they grow on damp materials, and research on fungal volatile organic compounds identifies 1-octen-3-ol as a major contributor to the musty odour associated with mould-contaminated damp indoor spaces. This does not mean every musty room is dangerous, but it does mean a smell can be a building-health signal rather than an atmospheric mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4591661/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAre Some Fungal Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCAre Some Fungal Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs</span></span></span>

**Carbon monoxide and combustion problems.** Carbon monoxide is especially important because it is colourless and odourless, so it will not announce itself as a “ghostly smell”. It can, however, produce symptoms that might be folded into a haunting narrative: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, tiredness, confusion, chest pain and shortness of breath. The NHS, CDC and the UK Health and Safety Executive all emphasise that carbon monoxide cannot be detected by smell, taste or sight, and that exposure can be serious or fatal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk+2CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carbon monoxide poisoning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carbon monoxide poisoning</span></span></span>

**Phantosmia.** Sometimes the smell is not in the room at all. Phantosmia is the perception of an odour when no identifiable smell source is present. Medical sources list possible causes including colds, allergies, nasal polyps, dental issues, sinus inflammation, head injury, ageing, temporal lobe seizures, certain medicines, Parkinson’s disease and COVID-19 infection. A phantom smell can be pleasant or foul, brief or recurrent, and it should not automatically be assigned to a haunting if it happens repeatedly or alongside other symptoms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24799-phantosmia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: my.clevelandclinic.org">[Cleveland Clinic+2Mayo Clinic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">my.clevelandclinic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cleveland Clinic Phantosmia (Olfactory Hallucinations): Causes &amp; Treatment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cleveland Clinic Phantosmia (Olfactory Hallucinations): Causes &amp; Treatment</span></span></span>

**Low-frequency sound and vibration.** Infrasound is sound below the normal range of human hearing. It can come from traffic, machinery, ventilation, boilers, [pipes]({{ 'pipes/' | relative_url }}), storms and other sources. A 2026 study in *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* found that exposure to 18 Hz infrasound was linked with higher salivary cortisol, greater irritability, lower interest and sadder appraisal of music, even though participants could not reliably tell whether infrasound was present. That makes infrasound a plausible contributor to uneasy atmospheres in some buildings, though not a complete explanation for every haunting claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span></span></span>

None of these checks proves that a particular haunting story is false. They do show why cold spots, smells and bodily unease are weak evidence unless they are measured, repeatable, localised, time-stamped and compared with mundane sources.

## Why the story persists

Cold spots and strange smells persist in ghost stories because they sit in the middle ground between objective event and private feeling. A door slamming can be blamed on wind. A figure can be dismissed as mistaken perception. But a chill down the back, a smell of flowers in an empty room or a sudden pressure of dread feels personal and difficult to argue away.

Expectation plays a large role. If someone enters a room after being told it is haunted, ambiguous sensations become easier to organise into a ghost story. A draught becomes a presence. A musty smell becomes decay. A vibration becomes dread. A flicker of bodily anxiety becomes the sense that the room itself is reacting. This is not simple gullibility; it is how perception works under uncertainty. The brain tries to explain bodily signals, and a haunted setting supplies a ready explanation.

Laboratory attempts to create haunted atmospheres have shown how difficult this is to separate. The “Haunt” project, published in *Cortex* in 2009, placed 79 participants in a specially constructed chamber and exposed them to infrasound, complex electromagnetic fields, both or neither. Many participants reported unusual sensations, but the study did not find that the manipulated environmental factors neatly produced those experiences in the predicted way. This points towards a mixed picture: atmosphere can matter, but expectation, suggestibility and the wider setting may matter just as much.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18635163/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The same caution applies to electromagnetic-field claims. Some researchers have proposed links between weak magnetic fields and sensed presence, but a 2005 double-blind replication attempt reported that mystical or sensed-presence experiences were predicted by suggestibility rather than by the application of weak complex magnetic fields. That does not end every debate about environmental factors, but it weakens any simple claim that a meter reading or odd field automatically explains a ghostly presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15849873/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The cultural appeal is also strong. Atmosphere is what turns a building into a haunted place. Without the smell of damp stone, the draught under the door, the creak in the corridor and the bodily pause before entering a room, many ghost stories would feel thin. These sensations give a haunting texture. They make the story feel local, physical and shareable: “stand here and you can feel it.”

## When atmosphere is evidence — and when it is a warning sign

Atmospheric evidence is most useful when it leads to careful observation. A cold patch that appears only when the heating switches on may point to air circulation. A smell that appears after rain may point to damp, drains or roof leaks. A heavy feeling in a cellar may suggest poor ventilation, low-frequency vibration, mould, fuel-burning appliances or anxiety triggered by darkness and confined space. A perfume-like odour that only one person notices may call for medical possibilities such as phantosmia, especially if it recurs outside the alleged haunted location.

A practical first pass should ask:

1. Does the cold spot repeat in the same place, at the same time, or under the same weather and heating conditions?
2. Is there a measurable temperature difference, or only a subjective chill?
3. Is there a draught source, cold wall, chimney, vent, pipe route, cellar, loft space or damp patch nearby?

5. Do several people notice it independently before being told what to expect?

7. Does the sensation disappear when people leave the building or when ventilation, heating or repairs change?

The safety point matters more than the ghost story. A “haunted” atmosphere caused by damp, mould, carbon monoxide, poor ventilation or faulty combustion can be harmful. Carbon monoxide alarms, gas-appliance servicing, ventilation checks, damp investigation and medical advice for recurring unexplained smells are not sceptical niceties; they are the sensible first line of response.

## The best reading of cold spots and haunted smells

Cold spots, smells and uneasy atmospheres are not meaningless. They tell us that people are responding to real sensations, suggestive cues or both. What they do not do is identify the cause on their own. A cold room may reveal a draught. A musty odour may reveal damp. A sense of dread may reflect infrasound, stress, expectation, poor air quality or the emotional power of a place already labelled haunted.

This is why atmospheric signs are powerful in ghost stories but fragile as evidence. They are memorable because they are embodied: the skin prickles, the nose catches something strange, the room feels different. Yet the same immediacy makes them easy to misread. The more responsible conclusion is not that every cold spot is “just” a draught or that every smell is “just” mould. It is that atmosphere should be investigated before it is mythologised.

A haunting may begin with a chill, but a good explanation starts by checking the window, the wall, the air, the building history, the witness’s expectations and the health risks that a ghost story can accidentally conceal.
