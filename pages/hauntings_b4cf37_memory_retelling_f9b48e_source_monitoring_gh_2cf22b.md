## Introduction

When people describe a haunting, they often assume they are reporting what they personally saw, heard or felt. Yet memory research shows that people can sometimes remember a detail vividly while misremembering where that detail came from. Psychologists call this a *source-monitoring error*: a mistake in identifying the origin of a memory. A person may sincerely believe that a detail came from direct experience when it actually came from a conversation, a television programme, a book, a ghost tour or repeated retellings by others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

Within the study of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), source-monitoring errors offer a powerful explanation for how “ghost memories” can develop. A witness may begin with a vague experience—a noise, a shadow, a feeling of unease—but later absorb details from local stories and eventually remember some of those details as part of the original event. The result is not necessarily deception. It is a normal consequence of the way human memory reconstructs the past.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

## What Source-Monitoring Errors Are

The source-monitoring framework, developed by memory researchers including Marcia Johnson and Stephen Lindsay, argues that memories do not come with permanent labels identifying their origin. Instead, people infer where a memory came from by examining its characteristics: how vivid it feels, whether it contains sensory details, whether it seems emotionally significant and whether it fits their expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

Most of the time this process works well. Problems arise when memories from different sources resemble one another. A remembered image from a story, for example, may later feel similar to a remembered image from actual perception. If the distinction becomes blurred, people can misattribute the source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/10/Lindsay-SM-Chap-4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca">[onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Memory Source Monitoring AppliedPeople are susceptible to source-monitoring errors when a mental event has characteris- tics that are typ...</span></span></span>

Researchers distinguish between remembering an event and remembering where knowledge of that event came from. Someone may accurately remember a detail while incorrectly remembering its source. A person can therefore be completely honest about recalling a ghostly woman on a staircase while being mistaken about whether the image came from direct observation, a guide's story or a photograph seen years earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

This is especially likely when:

* The original experience was ambiguous.
* The event was emotionally charged.
* Time has passed since the experience.
* The person has repeatedly discussed or imagined the event.
* Multiple similar stories circulate within a community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu+2onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

## How Haunting Stories Supply the Missing Details

Haunting traditions are unusually well suited to source-monitoring errors because they provide ready-made narrative material.

Imagine a visitor hears an unexplained creak in an old building. At first, the memory may be little more than “I heard something strange.” Later, the visitor learns that people claim a Victorian woman in grey walks the corridor. During subsequent retellings, the memory of the creak may become linked with the story. Eventually the person may recall not just the sound but a feeling that someone was present, or even a fleeting visual impression that seems connected to the legendary figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/10/Lindsay-SM-Chap-4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca">[onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Memory Source Monitoring AppliedPeople are susceptible to source-monitoring errors when a mental event has characteris- tics that are typ...</span></span></span>

The [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) works because memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive. People rebuild memories each time they recall them. Information acquired after an event can become integrated into the memory itself, a process demonstrated in decades of misinformation research. Experiments repeatedly show that people can come to remember suggested details as part of their original experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3213001/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Noba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCA Behavioral Account of the Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIHby DM Challies · 2011 · Cited by 35 — The misinformation effect is a term used in the cognitive psychological literature to desc...</span></span></span>

## Why Ghost Memories Feel Genuine

One reason ghost memories can be persuasive is that source-monitoring errors often preserve confidence.

People commonly assume that a vivid memory must be an accurate one. Research suggests otherwise. Memories can feel detailed, emotional and personally significant while still containing source confusions. A remembered image generated through imagination or repeated storytelling may acquire qualities normally associated with genuine experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>

This helps explain why witnesses sometimes insist that they “know what they saw”. The sincerity may be real. ## Examples from Reported Ghost Experiences

### The witness who learns the legend afterwards

A common pattern in haunting reports begins with an ambiguous event followed by later exposure to a story. Someone notices a cold feeling, an unexplained sound or a fleeting shape. Only afterwards do they discover that others associate the location with a particular ghost.

Once the legend becomes known, later recollections may become more specific. ## What Ghost Memories Reveal About Hauntings

Source-monitoring errors do not prove that every haunting report is mistaken. They do, however, explain how sincerely held memories can acquire details that were not part of the original experience. The mechanism is particularly relevant in locations with strong reputations, where stories, expectations and repeated discussion continually supply new material for memory reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/1993_Johnson_Hashtroudi_Lindsay_PsychBull.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memlab.yale.edu">[memlab.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memlab.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1993 Johnson Hashtroudi Lindsay PsychBull</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Johnson, Hashtroudi &amp; Lindsay (1993)May 18, 2011 — by MK Johnson · 1993 · Cited by 6864 — Source monitoring and failures in source monito...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 18, 2011</span></span></span>
