## Introduction

Carbon monoxide can make a house feel haunted in the most practical and dangerous sense: it can produce illness, confusion, visual problems, altered behaviour and, in some documented cases, ghost-like perceptions. It does not explain all ghost sightings, and it should not be treated as a catch-all debunking device. But when a haunting report includes headaches, dizziness, nausea, fatigue, confusion, chest pain, visual disturbance, odd behaviour, or symptoms that improve after leaving the building, carbon monoxide should be ruled out before any paranormal interpretation is taken seriously. The reason is simple: carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless, potentially fatal, and commonly linked to faulty or poorly maintained heating and cooking equipment. The NHS advises leaving the affected building and seeking medical advice if carbon monoxide poisoning is suspected; emergency care is needed for confusion, breathing difficulty, loss of consciousness, weakness, chest pain or muscle pain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Leave the building if poisoning is suspected.">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Leave the building if poisoning is suspected.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhs.uk.</span></span></span>

In the context of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), carbon monoxide matters because it sits at the overlap between subjective experience and household risk. A witness may sincerely report a sensed presence, figures at night, strange noises, oppressive dread or a belief that something unseen is in the room. The first useful question is not “Were they lying?” but “Was the building safe?”

## Why carbon monoxide can feel like a haunting

Carbon monoxide poisoning is often missed because its early symptoms are ordinary: headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness, breathlessness, weakness and confusion. The Health and Safety Executive warns that early symptoms can be mistaken for food poisoning, viral infections, flu or simple tiredness, and lists erratic behaviour and visual problems among warning signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/gas/domestic/co.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hse.gov.uk">[HSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hse.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HSEGas safety</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carbon monoxide awareness frequently asked questions - HSE...</span></span></span> The NHS gives a particularly haunting-relevant clue: symptoms may come and go, worsen in an affected room or building, and improve when the person leaves or goes outside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Leave the building if poisoning is suspected.">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Leave the building if poisoning is suspected.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhs.uk.</span></span></span>

That pattern maps neatly on to some ghost reports. A person feels oppressed in one room, hears noises at night, becomes unusually tired, sees something indistinct, or feels watched in a house where other people also feel unwell. In a story framework, those details become “the room is haunted”. In a safety framework, they become a prompt to check appliances, flues, ventilation and carbon monoxide alarms.

The [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) is not supernatural mimicry; it is toxic exposure. Carbon monoxide interferes with the body’s ability to deliver and use oxygen, and the brain and heart are especially vulnerable. UK government toxicology guidance lists acute effects such as headache, nausea, vomiting, vertigo, altered consciousness and weakness, with severe poisoning causing confusion, respiratory failure, loss of consciousness and death. It also notes that chronic low-level exposure may cause lethargy, headaches, flu-like symptoms and neuropsychological or cardiovascular problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/carbon-monoxide-properties-incident-management-and-toxicology/carbon-monoxide-toxicological-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carbon monoxide: toxicological overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carbon monoxide: toxicological overview</span></span></span>

That does not mean every “ghost” symptom has to be carbon monoxide. Creaking houses, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), sleep paralysis, [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}), suggestion, poor lighting and ordinary illness can all contribute to haunting experiences. Carbon monoxide is singled out because it is both plausible in some cases and urgent: a wrong paranormal interpretation can delay a life-saving inspection or medical assessment.

## What people report

Carbon monoxide-linked haunting stories tend to include a distinctive mix of “spooky” and bodily symptoms. The eerie material may be what people remember later, but the physical details are often the clue that the case is not just a folklore story.

Commonly reported features include:

* **A sensed presence or dread:** a feeling that the house is oppressive, watched, hostile or unnaturally still.
* **Visual disturbances:** shadows, figures, a person at the end of the bed, or loss or distortion of vision.
* **Auditory experiences:** [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}), bells, rushing sounds, wails, furniture-like noises or other unexplained sounds.
* **Bodily symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, chest pain, muscle pain, fatigue, sleepiness or confusion.
* **Patterned illness:** several people in the same building feeling unwell, or symptoms that ease away from the property.
* **Combustion sources nearby:** gas boilers, fires, cookers, heaters, generators, blocked flues, faulty furnaces or newly installed [water heaters]({{ 'water-heater/' | relative_url }}).

The best-known “haunted house” carbon monoxide case is William H. Wilmer’s 1921 medical paper, “Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eye”, published in the American Journal of Ophthalmology. The abstract describes a victim’s account of the subjective and general effects of carbon monoxide poisoning and says it “incidentally throws light on the origin of a story of a haunted house”. It also notes ocular symptoms and a tendency for the poison to affect the optic nerve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002939421908250" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eye</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eye</span></span></span>

Modern summaries of that case describe a family who moved into a long-empty house lit by gas lamps and heated by an old furnace. They reported depression, fatigue, headaches, strange sounds, apparent figures and other frightening experiences. The crucial turn was environmental, not spiritual: the heating system was found to be forcing fumes into the house rather than safely venting them away. A 2020 Frontiers in Psychology review of environmental factors in haunted-house experiences describes Wilmer’s case as one of the few reports in which carbon monoxide’s role is clearly substantiated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7304295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span></span></span>

A later emergency-medicine example is often cited because it shows the same lesson in a more modern setting. A 2005 report in the American Journal of Emergency Medicine described a 23-year-old woman found delirious and hyperventilating after seeing a “ghost” while showering; the problem was traced to a recently installed gas water heater that had filled the home with carbon monoxide. The Frontiers review includes this as another example of carbon monoxide exposure producing an apparition-like report in a domestic setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7304295/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCAn Environmental Appraisal of “Haunted Houses”</span></span></span>

## Natural explanations to check first

Carbon monoxide should be treated as a safety check, not as a rhetorical trick for winning arguments about ghosts. The point is not to tell frightened people that they are foolish. The point is to act on warning signs while there is still time.

The most urgent checks are practical:



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_carbon_monoxide_42c90c-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Carbon Monoxide illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_carbon_monoxide_42c90c-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_carbon_monoxide_42c90c-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

1. **Leave the building if poisoning is suspected.** The NHS advises going outside, stopping use of suspected appliances if this can be done safely, and getting medical advice as soon as possible. It also warns not to return to the affected building until advice has been obtained. [nhs.uk](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/)

4. **Look for building clues, but do not rely on them.** HSE guidance for construction and gas safety points to warning signs such as yellow or orange flames where a crisp blue flame is expected, sooty staining, pilot lights that blow out and increased condensation. These signs are useful, but carbon monoxide itself cannot be seen, tasted or smelt. [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/construction/healthrisks/hazardous-substances/carbon-monoxide.htm)

5. **Get a competent inspection and medical testing.** HSE medical guidance says carbon monoxide poisoning can be hard to recognise because its common symptoms overlap with flu, migraine, food poisoning and depression; tests can include carbon monoxide in expired air and carboxyhaemoglobin in venous blood. [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/gas/domestic/medical.htm)

There is also an important timing problem. Government toxicology guidance notes that blood carboxyhaemoglobin levels are not a reliable indicator of poisoning severity or clinical outcome, and HSE-linked safety material warns that carbon monoxide quickly leaves the blood after exposure ends, so delayed testing can miss the exposure. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/carbon-monoxide-properties-incident-management-and-toxicology/carbon-monoxide-toxicological-overview) This is one reason a “haunting” can persist as a story even after people feel better: by the time anyone thinks to test, the strongest biological trace may have faded.

## The mechanism behind the ghost-like effects

The haunting connection becomes more understandable when carbon monoxide is seen as a brain-and-body stressor rather than just a “gas leak”. It can produce vague illness at low or chronic exposures, obvious neurological impairment at higher exposures, and delayed neuropsychiatric effects after an acute poisoning event.

Carbon monoxide has a much higher affinity for haemoglobin than oxygen, reducing oxygen delivery to tissues; it also affects oxygen use at the cellular level. CDC clinical guidance describes carbon monoxide symptoms as variable and non-specific, with common symptoms including headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain and altered mental status. It emphasises that the effects involve impaired oxygen delivery and disrupted oxygen utilisation, especially in organs with high oxygen demand such as the brain and heart. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

That matters for ghost sightings because many haunting experiences rely on perception, memory and interpretation. A toxic exposure that causes headache, visual disturbance, confusion, drowsiness, anxiety, altered consciousness or erratic behaviour can change what a person notices and how they explain it. A shadow may become a figure. A sound may feel intentional. A half-waking sensation may be remembered as a presence at the bed.

The psychiatric and neurological literature also shows why the effects can be messy rather than neat. A Cambridge review of neuropsychiatric aspects of carbon monoxide poisoning notes that reports of such symptoms date back to 1914, and that delayed neuropsychiatric syndrome may range from personality changes and mild cognitive deficits to severe dementia and even psychosis. [Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/neuropsychiatric-aspects-of-carbon-monoxide-poisoning-diagnosis-and-management/B43C445C2EFFC1659484C5BAA8AC07CB) UK toxicology guidance similarly notes that long-term neurological effects can include cognitive and behavioural changes, and that neuropsychiatric features may appear in some people after acute exposure. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/carbon-monoxide-properties-incident-management-and-toxicology/carbon-monoxide-toxicological-overview)

This is why carbon monoxide should be understood as a mechanism of vulnerability, not a single guaranteed hallucination trigger. It can make the brain less reliable, the body unwell and the setting more frightening. In a house already primed as “haunted”, that may be enough for ordinary ambiguity to become a supernatural experience.

## Why the story persists

The carbon-monoxide ghost story persists because it is both useful and easy to overstate. It offers a satisfyingly concrete explanation for some haunted-house accounts: invisible gas, faulty appliance, frightened witnesses, medical symptoms, repairs, and the haunting stops. That narrative is memorable because it turns the ghost into a warning sign.

But the evidence is narrower than the internet version often suggests. The strongest material consists of a small number of documented or medically discussed cases, especially Wilmer’s 1921 haunted-house account and the 2005 shower case, plus the wider medical literature showing that carbon monoxide can cause confusion, visual disturbance, altered mental status and neuropsychiatric effects. The 2020 Frontiers in Psychology review found a relatively small and methodologically varied research literature on environmental factors in haunted-house experiences, with recurring attention to variables such as embedded cues, lighting, air quality, temperature, infrasound and electromagnetic fields. It specifically noted that carbon monoxide is clearly substantiated in only a few reports. [ScienceDirect+2GOV.UK](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002939421908250)

That caution is important. It would be wrong to say that “most hauntings are carbon monoxide” unless a particular dataset supports that claim. Most ghost reports are never medically investigated, many involve no illness at all, and some happen outdoors or in settings where carbon monoxide is unlikely. Carbon monoxide is not a universal answer to hauntings; it is a high-stakes exclusion check.

The persistence of the story also reflects a deeper cultural pattern. Hauntings often gather around old houses, poorly ventilated rooms, winter nights, fires, gaslight, boilers, cellars and bedrooms — exactly the kinds of settings where ordinary building conditions can shape perception and, in some cases, health. When a house already feels old, dim or uncanny, the body’s warning signals can be misread as the building’s personality.

## When a haunting should trigger a carbon monoxide check

A haunting report should prompt a carbon monoxide check whenever the experience is tied to a building with combustion appliances or enclosed fuel use, especially if bodily symptoms are present. The most important pattern is not “I saw a ghost”; it is “I saw or felt something strange and I also felt unwell in that place.”

Warning combinations include:

[* ghostly experiences plus headaches, dizziness, nausea, weakness, fatigue or confusion;](#endnote-19 "Endnote 19")
* more than one person or pet seeming affected in the same property;
* symptoms that worsen in one room, at night, during heating use, or in winter;
* symptoms that improve outdoors or away from the building;
* recent appliance installation, boiler problems, blocked chimneys, generator use, indoor barbecue or camping-stove use;
* visual disturbances, erratic behaviour, collapse, chest pain, breathlessness or loss of consciousness.

This does not require anyone to abandon their beliefs about ghosts before acting. A person can believe the experience felt paranormal and still check for carbon monoxide. In fact, that is the safest order of operations: rule out the invisible, lethal and testable hazard first; interpret the residue later.

## The practical takeaway for haunted houses

Carbon monoxide is one of the few haunting explanations where scepticism and compassion point to the same action. Take the report seriously enough to check the building. A witness who says a room feels oppressive, a figure appears at night, children are frightened, or everyone feels drained may be describing a meaningful experience, but the meaning may be medical and environmental before it is paranormal.

For hauntings, the carbon monoxide lesson is not “ghosts are never real”. It is sharper and more useful than that: some apparent hauntings may be warnings that a building is unsafe. When the signs fit, the responsible response is fresh air, urgent medical advice when needed, working carbon monoxide alarms, and a competent inspection of appliances, flues and ventilation. Only after that risk is ruled out does it make sense to debate what, if anything, remains unexplained.

      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
