## Introduction

Being told that a room is haunted can change what people notice, remember and report. It does not have to create a full-blown ghost sighting from nothing; often it works more subtly. A creak becomes a footstep, a draught becomes a cold spot, a vague unease becomes “a presence”, and ordinary uncertainty is interpreted through the story already attached to the place.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_suggestion_expectati_e4eab0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Suggestion" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This is one of the strongest natural explanations for many haunting experiences because haunted rooms are rarely neutral settings. Visitors may enter them primed by guidebooks, tours, warning signs, local legends, television programmes, friends’ stories or the room’s appearance. Psychological research on [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace, the Edinburgh [South Bridge Vaults]({{ 'vaults/' | relative_url }}), séance-room experiments, “haunted” laboratory rooms and allegedly haunted buildings suggests that expectation can shape both perception and interpretation, especially when people already believe ghosts are plausible. The key point is not that witnesses are lying. It is that human perception is active, selective and story-sensitive, especially in dark, unfamiliar or emotionally loaded places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2002). ## What people report after a haunted-room suggestion

A suggestion does not need to be dramatic. “This corridor is where people often feel cold,” “visitors hear [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) here,” or “this room has had recent ghost sightings” can be enough to make ordinary sensations feel meaningful. Once the idea has been planted, people may monitor their bodies and surroundings more closely than usual. They listen harder. They scan shadows. They become aware of tingling, dizziness, breath changes, temperature shifts or the feeling of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }}).

In haunting accounts, the most suggestion-sensitive reports are usually ambiguous experiences rather than clear, sustained observations. These include:

* **Cold spots:** a local chill may be interpreted as a ghostly presence rather than draughts, stonework, heating patterns or [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}).

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Footsteps and knocks:** old buildings produce many uncertain sounds, especially when visitors are already listening for them.
* **Sensed presence:** people may feel that someone unseen is nearby, a report common in haunting narratives but difficult to verify from outside.
* **Unease or oppression:** anxiety, darkness, social tension and anticipation can all be recast as a room’s “atmosphere”.
* **Brief visual impressions:** peripheral movement, reflections, low light and pattern recognition can produce fleeting impressions that are then fitted to a known story.

</div>

The Hampton Court Palace research is a useful concrete example because it involved a real historic site with a strong ghost reputation rather than a purely artificial laboratory setting. In a Journal of Parapsychology study of the palace, 678 participants reported their belief in ghosts and then walked around an allegedly haunted area. Believers reported more anomalous experiences than disbelievers and were more likely to attribute them to a ghost. ## Why expectation changes a room

Expectation changes haunting reports through several overlapping mechanisms. None requires fraud or foolishness. They are normal features of perception under uncertainty.

The first mechanism is **selective attention**. A visitor who expects nothing unusual may ignore minor noises, temperature changes or odd bodily sensations. A visitor who has just been told “people see something here” may treat the same details as evidence. The room has not necessarily changed; the visitor’s search pattern has.

The second is **interpretation**. Ambiguous sensations need a label. In a hotel room, a thump may be “the pipes”; in a reputedly haunted room, it may become “the ghost”. This is especially important in old buildings, where acoustics, draughts, settling materials, uneven floors and unfamiliar layouts produce plenty of raw material for interpretation.

The third is **memory shaping**. A person may leave with a slightly more coherent story than the moment itself supplied. “I felt odd near the fireplace” can become “that was where I sensed her.” This does not mean the memory is invented wholesale; it means later narrative can sharpen a vague experience into a haunting-shaped event.

The fourth is **social confirmation**. Haunted rooms are often visited in groups. One person says, “Did you feel that?” and others begin checking their own sensations. A hesitant report can become stronger when it is echoed by someone else. Recent psychological work on “haunted people” and perceptual contagion describes how anomalous experiences can be contextualised by paranormal beliefs and reinforced when perceptions spread through a group or setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.879163/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Case Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Case Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span></span></span>

The fifth is **personal belief and readiness**. Studies do not show that only believers have anomalous experiences. They do suggest that believers are more likely to report them and to interpret them paranormally. This distinction is crucial: expectation may increase the chance that people notice something, but belief often affects what they think it means.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2002). ## The strongest room-based evidence

The best evidence for suggestion in haunted settings comes from studies that compare what people report under different expectations or in different parts of a reputedly haunted site.

At Hampton Court Palace, researchers examined belief in ghosts, suggestion and local magnetic fields. Participants who believed in ghosts reported more unusual phenomena and were more likely to interpret them as ghostly. Suggestion also mattered in interaction with belief: telling people about unusual activity did not simply affect everyone in the same way, but it changed reports in relation to what participants already thought possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Profiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchprofiles.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2002). A later British Journal of Psychology paper reported two studies at Hampton Court Palace and the South Bridge Vaults in Edinburgh. Participants walked around reputedly haunted locations and marked where they experienced unusual phenomena. Reports clustered more heavily in areas with a reputation for haunting. The paper treated this as evidence that psychological mechanisms, including prior knowledge and expectation, are relevant to why ghostly activity appears to attach to particular places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12803815/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The Edinburgh South Bridge Vaults are especially useful for understanding why expectation has room to work. They are underground, dark, historically associated with poverty and overcrowding, and already framed by [ghost tours]({{ 'ghost-tours/' | relative_url }}) and local legend. Richard Wiseman’s research page describes the vaults as eighteenth-century spaces that became damp, overcrowded and unhealthy, giving visitors a strong historical and sensory backdrop before any ghost claim is considered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://richardwiseman.wordpress.com/research/psychology-of-ghosts-and-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardwiseman.wordpress.com">[Richard Wiseman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardwiseman.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Richard Wiseman Ghosts and Hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Richard Wiseman Ghosts and Hauntings</span></span></span>

## Natural explanations to check before calling it a haunting

Suggestion is strongest when the physical situation is ambiguous, so the most useful investigation starts with ordinary room conditions. The aim is not to dismiss the witness, but to separate a strange experience from the story attached to it.

**Check the soundscape.** Old rooms carry sound unpredictably. Footsteps may travel through beams, pipes, stairwells, service corridors or neighbouring rooms. Repeated knocks can come from heating systems, refrigeration, plumbing, loose fittings or building movement.

**Check airflow and temperature.** Cold spots may be caused by draughts, fireplaces, vents, poorly sealed windows, thick stone, damp patches, stairwells or intermittent heating. A visitor who has been told that coldness signals a ghost may notice a normal temperature gradient more intensely.

**Check light and sightlines.** Reflections, mirrors, polished glass, car headlights, moving branches, curtains, peripheral vision and low contrast can all produce brief figures or movement impressions. Haunted rooms often have exactly the kind of dim, uneven lighting that makes visual certainty harder.

**Check visitor instructions.** The wording matters. “This room is haunted” is not the same as “some visitors have reported unusual sensations here” or “please record anything unusual without discussing it.” A good investigation avoids telling participants where the “active” spots are before they report their experiences.

**Check social contamination.** If people discuss sensations during the visit, reports can converge. Independent written reports taken immediately are more useful than group recollections after a tour guide, friend or investigator has framed the experience.

**Check prior belief and prior knowledge.** Someone who knows the legend may interpret the room differently from someone who enters without context. This does not make the report worthless, but it changes how much weight should be put on the ghostly interpretation.

A 2020 Frontiers in Psychology review of environmental research on haunted houses found that the literature remained relatively sparse and methodologically diverse, with only 66 studies meeting the review’s scope. That is a warning against overclaiming from either side. Environmental factors, psychology and belief may all matter, but many haunted-room claims are not studied under conditions that can separate them cleanly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01328/pdf?isPublishedV2=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiersan Environmental Appraisal of &quot;Haunted Houses</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiersan Environmental Appraisal of &quot;Haunted Houses</span></span></span>

## When suggestion is not the whole explanation

Suggestion is powerful, but it is not a universal solvent. Some studies complicate the simple claim that “telling people a place is haunted makes them experience ghosts”.

One study on an allegedly haunted building used a slideshow about a fictitious abandoned hospital and varied the final suggestion: one version said the building had a history of ghostly activity, while another said it had structural problems. In that experiment, suggestion itself did not significantly affect haunt-related ratings; paranormal belief and proneness to reality-testing deficits did.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282948909_Suggestion_belief_in_the_paranormal_proneness_to_reality_testing_deficits_and_perception_of_an_allegedly_haunted_building" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This matters because expectation is not only about a sentence someone hears just before entering a room. It also includes deeper expectations built from personality, belief, culture, previous experiences, anxiety, sleep disruption, [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}), media exposure and the reputation of the place. A short suggestion may fail in a weak setting, while a long-standing legend attached to a dark historic room may be much more influential.

There is also a difference between **noticing** and **explaining**. A person may genuinely feel dizzy, cold or watched. The question is whether the best explanation is a ghost, the room, the body, the social situation, the prior story, or some mixture. In practical terms, suggestion usually works as an amplifier and organiser: it increases attention to ambiguous signals and supplies a ready-made meaning.

The “Haunt” project illustrates this nicely. Participants often reported unusual sensations in an artificial haunted room, but the pattern did not strongly support the specific idea that infrasound or electromagnetic fields were causing those sensations in the predicted places. That result leaves room for physical ambience, personal suggestibility and expectation without proving that any single factor explains every haunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010945208001299" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The “Haunt” project: An attempt to build a “haunted” room...by CC French · 2009 · Cited by 86 — An attempt was made to cons...</span></span></span>

## Why the story persists

Haunted-room stories persist because they are unusually good at turning ambiguity into memory. A room with a name, a legend and a repeated script gives visitors a way to interpret small sensations as part of something larger. Once enough people report similar feelings, the reports become part of the room’s reputation, and the reputation shapes later reports.

This feedback loop is especially strong in public haunted places. A palace gallery, underground vault, hotel room or old theatre may begin with a handful of stories. Guides repeat them. Visitors arrive prepared. Some visitors report coldness, footsteps or presence. Those reports are added back into the tour, the website, the local press or the folklore. The haunted room becomes not just a physical space but a managed expectation.

The persistence is not only commercial. Haunted rooms also give people a socially acceptable language for unease. A dark cellar can feel oppressive; a former prison cell can feel morally heavy; a bedroom associated with death can feel intimate and uncomfortable. Calling that feeling a haunting may be a way of making emotional, historical or bodily discomfort intelligible. Research on haunted people and anomalous experiences has increasingly treated ghostly reports as interactions between person, setting, belief and context rather than as isolated errors of perception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.879163/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Case Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Case Study of Recognition Patterns in Haunted People</span></span></span>

That is why the most careful answer to “Can being told a place is haunted change it?” is: it can change the experience of the place. The walls do not need to move. The story changes what people attend to, how they interpret sensations, what they remember, and how confidently they retell it. In haunted rooms, expectation is not a side detail. It is often part of the haunting’s machinery.
