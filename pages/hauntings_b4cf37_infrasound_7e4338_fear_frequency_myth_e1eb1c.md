## Introduction

The idea of a single “fear frequency” has become one of the most popular scientific-sounding explanations for [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}). In its strongest popular form, the claim is simple: a specific infrasonic frequency, often around 19 Hz, [triggers]({{ 'triggers/' | relative_url }}) fear, ghost sightings and paranormal experiences. The real evidence is more complicated. Research suggests that very low-frequency sound can influence mood, bodily comfort and emotional responses under some conditions, but the findings are mixed, the effects vary between people, and no single frequency reliably produces haunting experiences. The evidence supports a possible contributing factor, not a universal ghost explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24236415_A_Ghost_in_the_Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Ghost in the MachineWe show how a 19hz standing air wave may under certain conditions create sensory phenomena sugges...</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction matters because many discussions of hauntings present infrasound as either a complete solution or a complete myth. The research points somewhere in between: low-frequency sound can sometimes make places feel unsettling, yet many reported hauntings occur without identifiable infrasound sources, and many environments rich in infrasound do not generate ghost stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researchers discovered that even though participants could not consciously hear infrasound, exposure led to increased irritability, eleva...</span></span></span>

## How the fear frequency idea became popular

The modern story largely begins with engineer Vic Tandy's famous “Ghost in the Machine” account. While working in a laboratory, Tandy reported feeling watched, anxious and physically uncomfortable. He also thought he saw a grey figure in peripheral vision. After noticing a fencing foil vibrating unexpectedly, he traced the effect to a standing wave near 19 Hz produced by an extractor fan. Tandy and Tony Lawrence proposed that a 19 Hz standing wave might create sensory effects suggestive of a haunting under particular conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24236415_A_Ghost_in_the_Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Ghost in the MachineWe show how a 19hz standing air wave may under certain conditions create sensory phenomena sugges...</span></span></span>

The story spread because it offered a memorable narrative: a haunted room, a mysterious apparition and a measurable physical cause. Popular media soon transformed a limited hypothesis into something broader. The phrase “fear frequency” emerged as shorthand for the idea that 19 Hz directly causes terror or ghost experiences. Articles often highlighted proposed effects such as dread, dizziness and blurred vision, sometimes presenting them as established facts rather than a tentative explanation for specific cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2003/oct/16/science.farout" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianThe fear frequency &#124; ScienceOct 15, 2003 — 19hz is in the range known as infrasound, below the range of human hearing, which...</span></span></span>

A key problem is that the original claim was narrower than later retellings. Tandy's paper argued that a 19 Hz standing wave could contribute to some haunting-like sensations under certain circumstances. It did not claim that all hauntings stem from a single acoustic frequency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24236415_A_Ghost_in_the_Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Ghost in the MachineWe show how a 19hz standing air wave may under certain conditions create sensory phenomena sugges...</span></span></span>

## What concert and laboratory studies really show

When researchers moved beyond anecdotal reports and tested people systematically, the results became more nuanced.

One influential experiment secretly added infrasound to selected musical performances. Audience members did not know which pieces contained the low-frequency component. Researchers found that reports of unusual sensations increased when infrasound was present. Participants described chills, unease, sorrow, revulsion and similar feelings. The increase was statistically meaningful, but it did not mean that everyone reacted, nor did it produce dramatic ghost sightings. The effect was an increase in odd emotional responses rather than a reliable haunting trigger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2003/sep/08/sciencenews.science" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[IFLScience+3The Guardian+3ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianSilent sounds hit emotional chords &#124; ScienceSep 8, 2003 — To test the theory that infrasound could trigger sensations, the re...</span></span></span>

More recent laboratory work has also found evidence that infrasound can influence emotional state. In controlled experiments, participants exposed to low-frequency sound often showed increased irritability and elevated cortisol, a stress-related hormone, even when they could not consciously detect the sound. These findings support the idea that infrasound can affect the body and mood without obvious awareness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/04/that-spooky-sensation-likely-due-to-rumbling-pipes-not-spirits/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arstechnica.com">[Ars Technica+2Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arstechnica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">that spooky sensation likely due to rumbling pipes not spirits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ars TechnicaStudy: Infrasound likely a key factor in alleged hauntingsApr 27, 2026 — Instead, the results showed that, across the board...</span></span></span>

However, these studies also highlight the [limits]({{ 'limits/' | relative_url }}) of the phenomenon:

* The observed effects are generally modest rather than overwhelming.
* Not everyone responds in the same way.
* Researchers find changes in mood, discomfort or stress more consistently than reports of [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}).
* Results depend on sound intensity, environment, individual sensitivity and experimental design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2Ars Technica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researchers discovered that even though participants could not consciously hear infrasound, exposure led to increased irritability, eleva...</span></span></span>

This is why the evidence is often described as mixed rather than definitive. There is support for an influence on emotional experience, but much less support for claims that a particular frequency reliably generates paranormal perceptions.

## Why 19 Hz is not a magic number

The popularity of the fear-frequency idea has encouraged a misleading focus on one specific number. In reality, researchers do not regard 19 Hz as a universal human trigger.

Part of the confusion comes from the way Tandy's original experience became simplified in [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}). His laboratory happened to contain a standing wave near 19 Hz, but that does not mean 19 Hz possesses unique psychological properties. Different studies have used somewhat different infrasonic frequencies and still reported emotional or physiological effects. The broader issue appears to be exposure to certain kinds of low-frequency sound rather than one special frequency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24236415_A_Ghost_in_the_Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[Frontiers+3ResearchGate+3ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) A Ghost in the MachineWe show how a 19hz standing air wave may under certain conditions create sensory phenomena sugges...</span></span></span>

Researchers have also challenged oversimplified claims about infrasound itself. Acoustician Geoff Leventhall noted that the common idea that all sound below 20 Hz is completely inaudible is incorrect; under suitable conditions, humans can detect very low frequencies. This matters because popular accounts often rely on a dramatic contrast between “silent” sound and paranormal sensations. The real acoustics are more complicated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079610706000848" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What is infrasound?by G Leventhall · 2007 · Cited by 186 — Infrasound, in its popular definition as sound below a frequency...</span></span></span>

The evidence therefore points away from a magical threshold and towards a spectrum of possible responses influenced by acoustics, physiology and context.

## Where the explanation stops working

The strongest evidence for infrasound concerns [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) rather than apparitions. Low-frequency sound may help explain why some locations feel oppressive, uncomfortable or uncanny. ## The balanced interpretation

The fear-frequency story survives because it contains a genuine scientific insight wrapped inside an exaggerated claim. The genuine insight is that low-frequency sound can influence how people feel. Under some circumstances it can increase unease, stress, chills or a sense that something is wrong. That makes it relevant to the study of eerie environments and some haunting experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2003-09-08/soundless-music-shown-to-produce-weird-sensations/1475258" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News+2Ars Technica]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">soundless music shown to produce weird sensations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsSoundless music shown to produce weird sensationsSep 7, 2003 — British scientists have shown in a controlled experiment that the...</span></span></span>
