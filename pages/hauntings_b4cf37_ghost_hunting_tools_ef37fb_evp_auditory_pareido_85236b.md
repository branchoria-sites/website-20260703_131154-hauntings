## Introduction

Electronic voice phenomena (EVP) are recordings that listeners interpret as voices that were not heard during the original recording session. Within ghost hunting, EVP clips are often presented as possible evidence of communication from unseen entities. The central difficulty is that most [EVP recordings]({{ 'evp-recordings/' | relative_url }}) are extremely ambiguous: they typically consist of faint sounds, static, radio-like noise, environmental audio, or heavily processed recordings. Research on perception suggests that people are remarkably good at finding meaningful speech in such ambiguous sounds, especially when they expect a voice to be present. This process is known as auditory [pareidolia]({{ 'pareidolia/' | relative_url }})—the tendency to hear familiar patterns, particularly speech, in random or unclear audio.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelanees.com/s/Nees-Phillips-2015-Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-author-preprint.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelanees.com">[michaelanees.com+2Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michaelanees.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUDITORY PAREIDOLIAby MA Nees · Cited by 51 — For EVP stimuli, participants who were told the study was about paranormal EVP were signifi...</span></span></span>

Understanding auditory pareidolia does not require assuming that witnesses are dishonest or gullible. Instead, it highlights a normal feature of human perception: the brain constantly tries to turn incomplete sensory information into meaningful patterns. In the context of EVP recordings, that tendency creates a major challenge for evaluating whether a recording contains a genuine anomalous voice or merely sounds as though it does.

## What EVP Recordings Claim to Capture

EVP refers to sounds discovered on recordings after the fact and interpreted as words, phrases, or responses. Investigators may record in allegedly haunted locations and later review the audio for sounds that seem to resemble speech. The purported voices are often brief, fragmented, and difficult to understand without repeated listening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

A recurring feature of EVP reports is that the “message” is frequently recognised only after enhancement, filtering, slowing, looping, or suggestion from another listener. In many cases, captions are added to recordings to indicate what listeners are supposed to hear. Once a phrase is proposed, it can become difficult to hear the sound any other way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

This creates a problem for evidence assessment. A recording that appears compelling after interpretation may not have been recognisable as speech before the listener was told what to expect.

## Why Static Starts Sounding Like Speech

Human hearing evolved to detect voices in noisy environments. In everyday life, this ability is useful. People can often understand speech in a crowded room, recognise familiar voices over poor telephone connections, or fill in missing words when part of a sentence is obscured.

The same perceptual system can also generate false positives. When presented with ambiguous sounds, the brain searches for familiar structures such as syllables, words, and rhythms. Random fluctuations in noise can accidentally resemble fragments of speech, and listeners may unconsciously assemble those fragments into meaningful language. This is the essence of auditory pareidolia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

Researchers studying EVP have noted several factors that make speech-like perceptions more likely:

* **Background noise** provides raw material that can be interpreted as speech.
* **[Expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }})** increases the likelihood of hearing meaningful words.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Repetition** allows listeners to refine an interpretation over multiple playbacks.
* **Language familiarity** encourages people to hear words from languages they already know.
* **Audio enhancement** can introduce artefacts that sound voice-like even when they were not present in the original recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

</div>

Notably, many reported EVP messages are heard in the listener's own language. If the sounds originated from a source independent of the listener's interpretation, one might expect less dependence on language familiarity. Researchers have cited this pattern as consistent with perceptual interpretation rather than objective speech content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

## How Suggestion Changes What Listeners Hear

One of the strongest findings in EVP research concerns contextual priming—the effect of prior information on perception.

In a widely cited study by Michael Nees and Charlotte Phillips, participants listened to ambiguous audio samples, including purported EVP recordings. Some participants were told the sounds might contain paranormal voices, while others received a more neutral explanation. Those given paranormal framing were significantly more likely to report hearing voices within the ambiguous recordings. Later research similarly found that priming affected the tendency to perceive words or voices in ambiguous audio. Participants exposed to suggestions about ghostly voices were more likely to identify meaningful speech than participants who approached the recordings without that context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9473424/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal experiences, sensory-processing sensitivity, and...by JM Williams · 2022 · Cited by 15 — &#x27; Participants were assessed for...</span></span></span>

A simple demonstration illustrates the effect. If listeners hear a vague recording without guidance, they may disagree completely about what it says. After being shown a transcript, however, many suddenly hear the proposed phrase clearly and repeatedly. The recording itself has not changed; only the listener's expectations have.

This is one reason sceptical investigators often prefer blind listening tests. If a phrase can only be recognised after being suggested, its evidential value becomes much weaker.

## Ways to Test Whether Listeners Agree Independently

The key question for evaluating an EVP recording is not whether one person hears words. Given enough noise and enough listening, many people will hear something. The more important question is whether independent listeners hear the same thing without prompts.

Several approaches can help assess this.

### Blind Transcription

Listeners are given a recording without captions or explanations and asked to write what they hear. If a genuine phrase exists in the audio, independent listeners should converge on similar transcriptions. If responses vary widely, the recording may be too ambiguous to support a specific interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelanees.com/s/Nees-Phillips-2015-Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-author-preprint.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelanees.com">[michaelanees.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michaelanees.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUDITORY PAREIDOLIAby MA Nees · Cited by 51 — For EVP stimuli, participants who were told the study was about paranormal EVP were signifi...</span></span></span>

### Comparing Agreement Rates

Researchers can compare how often listeners agree on EVP recordings versus ordinary speech recordings. Clear speech typically produces high agreement. Ambiguous EVP clips often generate much lower consensus, indicating that interpretation plays a substantial role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelanees.com/s/Nees-Phillips-2015-Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-author-preprint.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelanees.com">[michaelanees.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michaelanees.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUDITORY PAREIDOLIAby MA Nees · Cited by 51 — For EVP stimuli, participants who were told the study was about paranormal EVP were signifi...</span></span></span>

### Controlling Suggestion

Listeners should not be told what the recording supposedly says. Nor should they know the claimed context, such as whether the recording came from a reputedly haunted location. ## What the Evidence Suggests

Studies of auditory perception consistently show that people can hear convincing words and voices in ambiguous sounds, particularly when expectation and suggestion are involved. Experimental work on EVP has repeatedly demonstrated that contextual information influences whether listeners report hearing voices at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://michaelanees.com/s/Nees-Phillips-2015-Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-author-preprint.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: michaelanees.com">[PLOS+3michaelanees.com+3Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">michaelanees.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUDITORY PAREIDOLIAby MA Nees · Cited by 51 — For EVP stimuli, participants who were told the study was about paranormal EVP were signifi...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that every EVP claim is explained by auditory pareidolia. However, it does establish a well-documented [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) capable of producing many of the reported experiences. A clip that sounds meaningful only after prompting, produces low agreement among independent listeners, or relies heavily on audio enhancement provides weak support for extraordinary claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2UCLan - University of Central Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>
