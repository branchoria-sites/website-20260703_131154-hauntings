## Introduction

A haunting often begins with a simple claim: someone saw a figure where no one should have been. Apparitions matter because they are vivid, personal and hard to dismiss for the witness, yet they are also exactly the kind of experience that can arise from ordinary mechanisms: poor light, peripheral vision, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), sleep states, [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}), illness, suggestion and the brain’s habit of turning uncertain signals into human forms. An apparition report is therefore not best treated as either proof of a ghost or proof that the witness was lying. It is a perception event inside a story-rich setting.

The useful question is not only “Was there really a figure?” but “What made a figure the most compelling interpretation at that moment?” In haunted places, that answer often combines the scene, the witness’s state of mind, the building’s reputation and the way later retellings sharpen a fleeting impression into a memorable ghost.

## What people report when they say they saw an apparition

In ghost research and folklore writing, an apparition usually means a figure that appears to be present without an ordinary physical person being there. The Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }})’s Psi Encyclopedia defines apparitions broadly as anomalous experiences in which a person is briefly seen, or otherwise felt to be present, even though no ordinary person is available to explain the event. That definition is useful because many “apparitions” are not crisp, cinematic figures; they may be a glimpse, a shadow, a person-shaped outline, a face, a movement at the edge of vision, or a presence that the witness only later describes as a figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/ghosts-and-apparitions-psi-research-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghosts and apparitions psi research overview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi EncyclopediaGhosts and Apparitions in Psi Research (Overview)17 Oct 2016 — In psi research, an &#x27;apparition&#x27; is an anomalous experienc...</span></span></span>

Classic haunting stories show how varied the reports can be. At [Hampton Court]({{ 'hampton-court/' | relative_url }}) Palace, the figure associated with Catherine Howard is tied to a specific route and historical drama: the Haunted Gallery, where tradition says she ran screaming for mercy after her arrest before her execution in 1542. Historic Royal Palaces presents the story as a reported haunting, not a verified event, but the setting gives the apparition a strong narrative shape: a corridor, a doomed queen, a repeated scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrp.org.uk/hampton-court-palace/history-and-stories/historic-hauntings-at-hampton-court-palace/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrp.org.uk">[Historic Royal Palaces]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Her sightings are more regularly reported</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Royal PalacesHistoric hauntings at Hampton Court PalaceJune 27, 2023 — The ghost of Catherine Howard, Henry VIII&#x27;s fifth queen...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 27, 2023</span></span></span>

The Roman soldiers of Treasurer’s House in York offer a different kind of apparition story. Historic England summarises the well-known 1953 account in which a plumber reportedly saw Roman soldiers, visible only from the knees up, marching through the cellar; the National Trust also presents Treasurer’s House as a place where ghost sightings, including the Roman soldiers, have become part of the property’s visitor story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGhost StoriesThe most renowned ghost story associated with York is that of the Roman Legion marching through the Cellar o...</span></span></span> The detail that the figures were only partly visible is especially important: apparition stories often depend on fragments, not full confirmation. A partial body, a movement, a costume-like impression or a vanishing figure can feel more convincing precisely because the mind tries to complete what it cannot fully inspect.

Several features recur in apparition reports:

* **Brief duration:** the figure is often seen for seconds, sometimes at a threshold, corner, staircase, doorway, mirror or window.
* **Ambiguous visibility:** dim light, distance, fog, [reflections]({{ 'reflections/' | relative_url }}), peripheral vision or obstructed sightlines often matter.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Human form:** the report becomes memorable because the shape is interpreted as a person, not just an object.
* **Narrative fit:** the figure is later connected to a known death, former resident, historic role, local legend or previous sighting.
* **Emotional force:** fear, shock, grief or awe helps fix the experience in memory.

</div>

That does not mean witnesses invent the whole event. It means an apparition is usually a joined-up experience: perception supplies a shape, emotion supplies urgency, memory supplies coherence and local story supplies identity.

## Why the brain is ready to find a figure

Human perception is not a passive camera. It is a predictive system that uses limited sensory information to make rapid guesses about the world. That is normally useful. In a dark hallway or unfamiliar old building, it is safer and faster to over-detect a possible person than to miss one. But the same tendency can turn shadows, coats, reflections, carved shapes, curtains, trees or passing headlights into figure-like impressions.

One key mechanism is **pareidolia**, the tendency to perceive meaningful patterns, especially faces and bodies, in ambiguous material. A 2024 computer-vision paper on face pareidolia notes that the human visual system is highly tuned to detect faces, which has obvious survival value but also produces false positives: faces in clouds, stains, rock formations or random visual texture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16143" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Seeing Faces in Things: A Model and Dataset for Pareidolia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Seeing Faces in Things: A Model and Dataset for Pareidolia</span></span></span> In a haunted setting, a false-positive face or body can become more than a visual mistake because the witness is already in a place where a human-like presence would feel meaningful.

Peripheral vision also plays a role. The edge of vision is good at detecting motion and broad scene information, but it is poor at detail. A dark vertical shape glimpsed sideways may be registered as “someone standing there” before the eyes turn and discover a coat stand, doorframe or shadow. If the figure vanishes when looked at directly, that can strengthen the ghost interpretation, even though it may simply reflect how peripheral detection and central inspection work differently.

Memory then edits the event. People do not store every pixel of a sighting; they remember the meaning they attached to it. A witness may first think, “I saw someone,” then later, after hearing the building’s story, remember the event as “I saw the old woman on the stairs.” This is not necessarily dishonest. It is how memory becomes a story. The stronger the emotion, the more confident the person may feel, but confidence is not the same as independent verification.

## Natural explanations to check before calling it a ghost

The most useful way to assess an apparition report is to begin with the conditions that can make human figures appear where none exist. These explanations do not cover every case, and they do not tell a witness what they “really” experienced. They simply identify the mechanisms most often worth checking.

**Light, reflection and sightlines.** Windows at night, mirrors, polished glass, security screens, framed pictures and glossy doors can produce person-shaped reflections. Passing cars can move shadows along walls or across staircases. In historic buildings, uneven floors, layered doorways and narrow corridors can create brief occlusions that make a real person, guide, visitor or staff member seem to appear and disappear.

**Expectation and suggestion.** If visitors are told that a particular corridor is haunted, ambiguous sensations in that corridor are more likely to be interpreted as paranormal. Richard Wiseman and colleagues investigated alleged [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) and argued that some reported experiences may reflect people responding to environmental cues in places already framed as haunted. Their work is important because it does not require witnesses to be foolish; it shows how place, expectation and perception can interact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12803815/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

**Sleep and waking states.** Some apparition reports happen in bedrooms, guest rooms, hotels or during the night, where sleep-related hallucinations become relevant. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine explains that sleep hallucinations can seem very real and are mainly visual, though they can involve other senses too.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/sleep-hallucinations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sleepeducation.org">[Sleep Education]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sleepeducation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sleep Education Sleep Hallucinations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep Education Sleep Hallucinations</span></span></span> Sleep paralysis is especially relevant to “figure in the room” stories: recent reviews describe it as a state in which a person wakes unable to move or speak and may experience vivid hallucinations, often with fear and a sensed presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11344621/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

**Bereavement and familiar figures.** Seeing, hearing or sensing someone who has died can occur in grief without meaning that the person is psychotic or deliberately imagining it. Research on sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased reports that bereaved people often describe experiences of the dead, and a 2021 study of “after-death communications” found that such encounters can be common and comforting for some people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> In a haunting context, a bereavement experience may become attached to a place: a bedroom, chair, hallway, hospital room or family home.

**Visual impairment and medical causes.** Not every apparition-like experience belongs in folklore. The NHS notes that hallucinations can include seeing people, objects, shapes or lights that are not there, and it identifies a range of possible causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/hallucinations-hearing-voices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hallucinations and hearing voices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hallucinations and hearing voices</span></span></span> Charles Bonnet syndrome is a particularly important example: the NHS describes it as visual hallucinations that can happen after significant sight loss, sometimes involving people, animals, objects or places, and not caused by dementia or a mental health problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/charles-bonnet-syndrome/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Charles Bonnet syndrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charles Bonnet syndrome</span></span></span>

**Environmental hazards.** Some “haunted house” symptoms can point to practical danger. Carbon monoxide is the classic cautionary example because it can cause confusion, illness and hallucination-like experiences. Smithsonian Magazine discusses a frequently cited case in which a faulty furnace produced symptoms that were interpreted as haunting until carbon monoxide was identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/five-scientific-explanations-spooky-sensations-180973436/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Five Scientific Explanations for Spooky Sensations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Five Scientific Explanations for Spooky Sensations</span></span></span> Any apparition report accompanied by headaches, nausea, dizziness, weakness, confusion or multiple people feeling unwell in the same building should be treated first as a safety issue, not a ghost story.

**Low-frequency sound and bodily unease.** Infrasound, sound below the usual threshold of hearing, has often been proposed as a contributor to eerie feelings in old buildings. Wiseman’s research page notes that some researchers have suggested infrasound could be present in allegedly haunted locations and make people feel uneasy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://richardwiseman.wordpress.com/research/psychology-of-ghosts-and-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardwiseman.wordpress.com">[Richard Wiseman]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardwiseman.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Richard Wiseman Ghosts and Hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Richard Wiseman Ghosts and Hauntings</span></span></span> A 2026 report on recent experiments described low-frequency sound raising stress markers and influencing mood, while also noting expert caution: such effects may help explain unease, but they do not by themselves prove why someone sees a detailed visual figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span>

The practical point is simple: an apparition report should be investigated from the outside in. Check the room, light, route, mirrors, windows, sleep timing, health factors, substances, stress, grief, prior stories and possible hazards before treating the figure as evidence of a haunting.

## The “felt presence” can become a seen figure

Some haunting reports begin not with sight but with a feeling: someone is behind me; someone is standing at the door; I am not alone. The brain can generate this sensation without a visible person, and once the feeling is strong, the mind may search the environment for a matching figure.

Neurological research on the “feeling of presence” is especially relevant here. In a 2014 study, Olaf Blanke and colleagues used a robotic system to create sensorimotor conflict: participants moved one device while another touched their back in a delayed or altered way. The mismatch could induce the illusion that another person was nearby. The authors argued that this kind of presence illusion can arise when the brain misattributes signals from the body to an external agent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982214012123" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Accessible summaries of the same research explain why it matters for ghostly experiences. BrainFacts describes presence hallucinations as the strange sensation that someone is nearby without reliable sensory evidence, and notes that they can range from mild experiences to a looming figure in darkness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brainfacts.org/thinking-sensing-and-behaving/thinking-and-awareness/2024/presence-hallucination-research-shows-how-our-brains-conjure-ghostly-illusions-103024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brainfacts.org">[BrainFacts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brainfacts.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on brainfacts.org.</span></span></span> Science’s report on the robot study likewise describes participants and patients experiencing a human-like presence close behind them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.science.org/content/article/robot-makes-people-feel-ghost-nearby" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.org">[Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">robot makes people feel ghost nearby</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">robot makes people feel ghost nearby</span></span></span>

This helps explain a common haunting pattern. A person in an old house feels watched, turns quickly and sees a shape in the corner. The seen figure may be brief and ambiguous, but it arrives after the body has already produced the alarm signal. In that moment, the “ghost” is not just a visual object. It is the brain trying to match a powerful felt presence with something in the room.

## Why haunted settings make figures easier to believe

A figure seen in an office corridor may be dismissed as a cleaner, reflection or tiredness. The same figure seen in a castle corridor called the Haunted Gallery is more likely to become an apparition. Place changes interpretation.

Historic sites often give apparition stories a ready-made identity. Hampton Court has Catherine Howard; Treasurer’s House has Roman soldiers; many houses have a white lady, monk, child, soldier or former owner. These identities help stories travel because they compress uncertainty into a recognisable character. A person does not merely say, “I saw a shape.” They say, “I saw the queen,” “I saw the Roman soldiers,” or “I saw the woman people talk about.”

This can make a haunting feel cumulative even when the individual reports are weak. One person sees a shadow near the stairs; another hears a sound; a guide mentions an old story; a later visitor photographs a blur. The reports differ, but the place supplies a single interpretive frame. Over time, the haunting becomes a local pattern, and new ambiguous experiences are sorted into it.

Tourism, media and architecture reinforce the effect. A labelled “haunted” room primes attention. A darkened tour encourages scanning for movement. A story told immediately before entering a corridor makes ordinary creaks more meaningful. None of this proves that witnesses are pretending. It shows why haunted places are good at converting uncertainty into figures.

## Why apparition stories persist after the sighting

Apparition stories persist because they do social work. They make places memorable, give grief a form, turn history into encounter and let people discuss death without speaking only in abstract terms. A ghostly figure is easier to remember than a vague [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}). It has a body, costume, route and motive.

They also persist because they are hard to disprove in the form people usually tell them. A fleeting figure leaves little evidence. A witness’s sincerity may be genuine. The setting may be old, emotionally charged or historically violent. The story may contain one striking detail, such as the partly visible Roman soldiers at Treasurer’s House, that makes it feel resistant to easy dismissal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/ghost-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGhost StoriesThe most renowned ghost story associated with York is that of the Roman Legion marching through the Cellar o...</span></span></span>

But the same qualities that make apparition stories powerful also make them fragile as evidence. They are often private, brief, unrecorded and retold after the meaning has settled. They may contain accurate historical colour, but accurate colour does not prove a supernatural cause. A witness can correctly describe a location, costume or local tradition while still having misperceived a shadow, experienced a sleep-state hallucination, or had memory reshaped by later information.

The fairest reading is therefore double-sided. Apparition reports should be taken seriously as human experiences, especially when they involve fear, grief or possible medical and environmental causes. They should not be treated as automatic proof that the dead appear in buildings. Haunted places produce apparitions because human perception is tuned for people, human memory is tuned for stories, and old places are unusually good at making a half-seen figure feel as though it belongs.
