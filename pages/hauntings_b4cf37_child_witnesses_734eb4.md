## Introduction

Poltergeist stories often put children at the centre of the disturbance: a girl hears the first knocks, a boy is blamed for [thrown objects]({{ 'thrown-objects/' | relative_url }}), siblings become the main witnesses, or a teenager is treated as the “focus” around whom activity gathers. That pattern matters because it changes the evidential problem. A child may be a truthful observer, a frightened participant, a playful experimenter, a target of adult [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}), or a convenient explanation for events nobody has properly checked. In many famous cases, the real question is not simply “did a ghost move the object?” but “what happens when a household under stress, adult belief, media attention and a child’s testimony all meet inside a haunting claim?”



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_child_witnesses_734eb4-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Child Witnesses" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The fairest reading is cautious in both directions. Children should not be dismissed automatically as unreliable or deceitful, but poltergeist cases create unusually poor conditions for judging children’s reports: repeated [questioning]({{ 'questioning/' | relative_url }}), [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), fear, family strain, journalists, investigators, and sometimes rewards for producing “phenomena”. Research on child witnesses shows that children can give useful accounts, especially when interviewed carefully, but suggestive questioning and repeated retelling can distort evidence. Poltergeist cases sit exactly in that danger zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2180422/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Structured forensic interview protocols improve the quality and...by ME Lamb · 2007 · Cited by 1166 — We review studies designed to e...</span></span></span>

## What people report

In ordinary haunting reports, witnesses often describe apparitions, footsteps, cold spots or a sensed presence. Poltergeist reports are different because they usually involve disruption: knocking, thrown objects, furniture moving, fires, writing on walls, electrical faults, voices, or bedding and household items behaving strangely. The Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}) places poltergeists near haunting cases, while noting that many such cases have been interpreted not as the activity of the dead but as effects somehow associated with living minds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/9-hauntings-and-poltergeists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[SPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on spr.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That “living focus” idea is where children enter the story. In many accounts, disturbances appear to cluster around a particular child or adolescent. The child may be the first person to report a sound, the person present when objects move, the person accused of faking, or the person whom paranormal investigators describe as an “agent”. This does not prove anything paranormal; it simply marks where attention, suspicion and interpretation converge.

The Enfield case is the best-known British example. The reported disturbances at 284 Green Street in north London began in 1977 and became associated especially with sisters Janet and Margaret Hodgson, then aged 11 and 13. Claims included knocking, moving furniture, thrown objects and a gruff voice. The case drew police, journalists, neighbours and investigators, and later became part of popular culture through documentaries and horror films. The Psi Encyclopedia, published by the SPR, describes Enfield as among the most closely recorded poltergeist-type cases, with more than 180 hours of tape recordings made by Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grosse, Playfair, Mrs Hodgson and other witnesses...Read more...</span></span></span>

The same case also shows why child-centred poltergeist stories are difficult to assess. Some investigators treated the Hodgson children’s experiences as strong evidence; others thought at least some incidents were staged. Sceptical accounts have argued that adult investigators and reporters sometimes accepted ambiguous events too readily, while believers have pointed to the number of witnesses and the volume of recordings. The important point for this subtopic is not to re-try Enfield in full, but to notice its structure: once children became the centre of the case, their behaviour, stress, playfulness, fear and credibility became inseparable from the haunting narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enfield_poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enfield poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enfield poltergeist</span></span></span>

Other cases repeat the same pattern in different settings. The Battersea Poltergeist, revived for a BBC drama-documentary podcast, centred on Shirley Hitchings, who was 15 when disturbances were reported at her family’s home in Wycliffe Road, Battersea, from 1956 onwards. Reports included knocking, objects moving, writing and the idea of a communicative “Donald” presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://podcasts.apple.com/au/podcast/the-battersea-poltergeist/id1548946506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: podcasts.apple.com">[Apple Podcasts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">podcasts.apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Podcasts The Battersea Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Podcasts The Battersea Poltergeist</span></span></span> In the Columbus Poltergeist case of 1984, teenage Tina Resch became famous after photographs appeared to show objects flying around her; sceptical investigators later argued that the evidence was weak and consistent with deliberate staging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/exclusive/demons-and-saviors-revisiting-the-columbus-poltergeist-case/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer&#x27;Demons and Saviors&#x27;: Revisiting the Columbus Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical Inquirer&#x27;Demons and Saviors&#x27;: Revisiting the Columbus Poltergeist</span></span></span>

These examples show why children become narratively powerful in poltergeist cases. A child witness gives the story intimacy and vulnerability. A child “agent” gives investigators a focus. A frightened family gives the case emotional force. But the same features that make the story compelling also make it vulnerable to error.

## Why children become the focus

A common paranormal interpretation is that poltergeist disturbances are connected to adolescent stress or “recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis”, often shortened to RSPK: the proposed idea that emotional tension might unconsciously produce physical effects. This remains speculative and is not accepted as established science. Even within psychical research, it has been one interpretation among several, rather than a settled explanation. Psi Encyclopedia material on psychological aspects of poltergeist cases discusses older attempts to link cases with repression, emotional conflict or particular “agents”, while also showing how much of the literature depends on case interpretation rather than controlled evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases</span></span></span>

A more grounded explanation begins with social dynamics. Children and adolescents are often in a household position where they are highly observed but not fully in control. They may be under family stress, moving home, experiencing puberty, facing school pressures, coping with bereavement or conflict, or trying to gain adult attention. In a tense home, small unexplained events can become a shared focus: a knock becomes a sign, a fallen object becomes a message, a joke becomes a test, and the child learns that adults are watching.

That does not mean “the child is lying” is the default answer. Children can misperceive, exaggerate, copy adult language, become frightened, try to please questioners, or occasionally stage events for reasons that are not simple malice. In a poltergeist setting, several motives can overlap:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Fear:** a child may genuinely believe something is happening and report it as honestly as they can.
* **Attention:** sudden adult concern, media visits or investigator interest can reinforce a child’s central role.
* **Play and experiment:** knocks, thrown objects or hidden tricks may begin as games, dares or attempts to test what adults will believe.
* **Pressure:** once a case becomes public, a child may feel expected to produce fresh events.
* **Protection:** a child may hide ordinary causes because admitting them would embarrass the family or anger adults.
* **Contagion:** siblings, parents and neighbours can unintentionally shape each other’s memories by repeated discussion.

</div>

Research on child testimony is useful here because it avoids two bad extremes. It does not say children are inherently unreliable; nor does it treat every child report as pristine evidence. Reviews of child interviewing emphasise that children’s accounts depend both on the child’s development and on the behaviour of the interviewer. Open, non-leading questions tend to improve the quality of evidence, while suggestive questioning, repeated prompts and adult assumptions can undermine it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/7e659cb7-0114-4f1f-9d39-845228f9022d/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge University Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cam.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That is exactly the problem with many poltergeist cases. They rarely begin with calm, neutral interviews. They unfold in kitchens, bedrooms and living rooms where adults are anxious, journalists want a story, investigators may already hold a theory, and children may be questioned again and again. By the time a formal account is written, the child’s original observation may have passed through several layers of [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}).

## Natural explanations to check

The first task in a child-centred poltergeist case is not to decide whether the child is good or bad. It is to separate types of evidence. A report that a child heard knocking is different from an adult seeing a chair move. A photograph of an object in mid-air is different from footage showing how it began moving. A child’s statement immediately after an event is different from a dramatic retelling months later.

The most useful checks are practical and humane:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Where was the child?** Many poltergeist claims depend on whether the child was alone, partially observed, behind furniture, under bedding, near the object, or genuinely away from the scene.
* **Who saw the start of the event?** It is weaker evidence if witnesses saw only the object after it was already moving or after it had landed.
* **Were adults asking leading questions?** “Did the ghost throw it?” is not the same as “Tell me what happened.”
* **Were there ordinary triggers?** Loose shelves, draughts, pets, plumbing, heating, traffic vibration, faulty wiring, prank objects and house movement should be checked before symbolic meanings are added.
* **Was there an audience?** Events that intensify around visitors, reporters or investigators may reflect performance pressure as much as haunting activity.
* **Did the story change?** Growing detail is not always suspicious, but it should be treated carefully when it follows repeated questioning or media attention.

</div>

The Columbus case is a useful warning about photographic drama. Images of Tina Resch with objects apparently flying near her became famous, but sceptical reviews argued that still photographs could not show how the movement began and that some evidence suggested ordinary throwing or staging. Later discussion of the case has also stressed the danger of turning a troubled adolescent into a paranormal symbol before the evidence has been properly tested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/exclusive/demons-and-saviors-revisiting-the-columbus-poltergeist-case/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer&#x27;Demons and Saviors&#x27;: Revisiting the Columbus Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical Inquirer&#x27;Demons and Saviors&#x27;: Revisiting the Columbus Poltergeist</span></span></span>

Environmental explanations can also be surprisingly effective. Sceptical discussions of poltergeist claims have pointed to hoaxes, exaggerated testimony and ordinary physical causes such as air currents, vibration or structural effects. These explanations do not cover every reported detail in every case, but they are the first things to eliminate because they require fewer assumptions than invisible agency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Psychological explanations also need care. Saying a case may involve stress, dissociation, suggestion or family dynamics should not become a polite way of blaming a child. A child in a poltergeist case may be distressed by the events, distressed by adult attention, or distressed by being accused. The ethical question is not just “is the claim true?” but “what is happening to the child while adults investigate, publicise or dispute it?”

## Why the story persists

Child-centred poltergeist stories last because they combine three powerful ingredients: domestic intimacy, physical mystery and moral uncertainty. A ghost in a castle can feel distant; a child in a bedroom saying that furniture moved is harder to treat as mere [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}). The reader or viewer is pulled into a family scene and asked to judge fear, trust and deception at the same time.

Media attention strengthens that pattern. Enfield was not only a household case; it became a press, radio, television and film story. The tapes, photographs and witness interviews created a large archive, but they also created competing versions of the children: victims, pranksters, psychic agents, frightened witnesses, unreliable narrators or symbols of a haunted council house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grosse, Playfair, Mrs Hodgson and other witnesses...Read more...</span></span></span> Battersea has similarly survived through retelling, with Shirley Hitchings’ teenage experience reframed decades later through podcast drama, interviews and stage adaptation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://podcasts.apple.com/au/podcast/the-battersea-poltergeist/id1548946506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: podcasts.apple.com">[Apple Podcasts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">podcasts.apple.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Podcasts The Battersea Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Podcasts The Battersea Poltergeist</span></span></span>

The child witness also gives poltergeist stories an unresolved emotional charge. If adults reject the child’s report too quickly, they may seem cruel or dismissive. If they believe too quickly, they may encourage fear, performance or false memory. If they accuse the child of trickery, they may miss genuine distress. If they refuse to consider trickery, they may let a weak case harden into legend.

That tension explains why these stories remain attached to hauntings even when the “ghost” explanation is uncertain. They are not only stories about objects moving. They are stories about how adults listen to children under strange conditions. A careful approach keeps several possibilities open at first: misperception, ordinary household causes, deliberate play, family stress, social contagion, media amplification, and, for believers, unexplained phenomena. The mistake is to collapse the child into a single role too early.

The best standard is therefore neither automatic belief nor automatic debunking. Children in poltergeist cases should be treated as people before they are treated as evidence. Their accounts deserve patient, non-leading listening; their welfare deserves protection from publicity and accusation; and the physical claims around them deserve ordinary checks before extraordinary explanations are invoked. That approach may make the story less dramatic, but it makes the haunting easier to understand.
