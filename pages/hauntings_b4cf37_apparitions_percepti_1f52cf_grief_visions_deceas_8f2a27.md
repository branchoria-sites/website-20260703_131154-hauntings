## Introduction

Reports of seeing, hearing, sensing, or briefly encountering a deceased loved one occupy a distinctive place within the wider subject of hauntings and [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}). Unlike stories about anonymous figures in [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}), these experiences are deeply personal. A widow may hear her partner’s footsteps in the house, a parent may glimpse a deceased child standing in a familiar doorway, or someone may wake with a powerful sense that a lost relative is sitting beside them. Research over several decades suggests that such experiences are remarkably common in bereavement and are not, by themselves, evidence of mental illness or deception. Many people who report them are otherwise psychologically healthy, and most describe the experiences as comforting rather than frightening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30273885/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How many bereaved people hallucinate about their loved...by KS Kamp · 2019 · Cited by 11 — The prevalence of having one or more BH...</span></span></span>

Within discussions of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), grief visions are important because they show that not every apparition report involves a supposedly haunted location or a stranger from the past. Sometimes the “figure” is someone the witness loved and expected to encounter every day until death abruptly disrupted that [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}).

## When Grief Feels Like a Visitation

Researchers use terms such as “sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased” or “post-bereavement hallucinatory experiences” to describe encounters in which bereaved people perceive the continued presence of someone who has died. These experiences vary widely. Some involve a vivid visual sighting, while others consist of hearing a familiar voice, smelling a characteristic scent, sensing a touch, or simply feeling with certainty that the person is present nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 105 — Bereaved people often report having sen...</span></span></span>

One reason these reports attract attention is their frequency. A meta-analysis of bereavement studies estimated that roughly 56.6% of bereaved individuals experience at least one such event, while reviews focused on widowed people commonly report prevalence estimates between 30% and 60%.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30273885/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How many bereaved people hallucinate about their loved...by KS Kamp · 2019 · Cited by 11 — The prevalence of having one or more BH...</span></span></span>

The experiences are often more subtle than popular ghost stories suggest. Research repeatedly finds that the most common report is not a full-bodied apparition but a [felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }}): the sense that the deceased person is somehow nearby, occupying the same space, or accompanying the bereaved individual during daily life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d-nb.info/1212036654/34" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d-nb.info">[D-NB+2Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d-nb.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This was followed by visual.Read more...</span></span></span>

A classic study by physician Dewi Rees found that many widowed people reported sensing or encountering their deceased spouse, often over extended periods rather than as a single isolated incident. Most participants regarded the experiences as helpful or neutral rather than disturbing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/news/datablog/2024/oct/21/witnessing-dead-spouse-study" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian What are the odds of witnessing the presence of a deceased spouse?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">datablogOctober 21, 2024 — A recent initiative by Ring, tied to Halloween festivities, offers $100,000 for anyone capturing ghost sightin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 21, 2024</span></span></span>

## What Bereaved People Report Seeing or Sensing

The range of reported experiences is broader than many people realise.

Common descriptions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Seeing the deceased briefly in a familiar room.
* Hearing their voice call a name or speak a short phrase.
* Feeling them sit on a bed or stand nearby.
* Smelling a distinctive perfume, tobacco scent, or other familiar odour associated with them.
* Experiencing vivid dreams that feel unusually real or interactive.
* Having a strong sense of presence without any clear sensory component.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 105 — Bereaved people often report having sen...</span></span></span>

</div>

Many accounts occur during ordinary activities rather than dramatic moments. Someone may turn towards the kitchen expecting to see a spouse who always stood there, or momentarily glimpse a familiar figure while entering the home. These experiences often emerge during the period when the brain is still adapting to the person's absence.

Neuroscientists and [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}) researchers note that close relationships become deeply embedded in everyday expectations and routines. The bereaved person's brain has spent years predicting where the loved one will be, how they will sound, and how they will respond. After a death, those predictive systems do not instantly disappear. The result can be brief experiences in which the lost person feels perceptually present despite the conscious knowledge that they have died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.axios.com/2023/05/07/neuroscience-grief" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: axios.com">[Axios]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">axios.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How the brain changes when we grieve</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researchers highlight that grief, while emotionally painful, is a biologically rooted and protective response to the loss of a close conn...</span></span></span>

## Why Familiar Places Can Anchor the Experience

Grief visions frequently occur in locations strongly associated with the deceased: the family home, a favourite chair, a workplace, a garden, or a route once travelled together. These settings matter because they are saturated with memory and expectation.

A person who spent decades hearing a spouse climb the stairs each evening may continue to anticipate that sound. Someone who always greeted a parent in a particular doorway may unconsciously orient attention towards that spot. Familiar places therefore provide a powerful framework through which fleeting sensory impressions, memories, and expectations can merge into an experience of presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.axios.com/2023/05/07/neuroscience-grief" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: axios.com">[Axios]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">axios.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How the brain changes when we grieve</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Researchers highlight that grief, while emotionally painful, is a biologically rooted and protective response to the loss of a close conn...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why grief apparitions often differ from traditional haunting narratives. In a haunted-house story, the witness typically encounters an unknown figure and later assigns it an identity. In bereavement experiences, identity comes first. The witness already knows exactly who the perceived person is because the experience emerges from an existing relationship and a deeply established pattern of daily life.

Researchers studying bereavement have also noted that the deceased often appear in ways consistent with how they were remembered. The encounter may involve characteristic gestures, habits, expressions, or emotional qualities that were central to the relationship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-025-10064-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensing presence: deathbots and bereavement hallucinationby P Stokes · 2025 · Cited by 14 — This paper considers recent discussio...</span></span></span>

## Why These Experiences Are Not Automatically Signs of Illness

One of the most significant findings in the literature is that bereavement-related apparitional experiences are often compatible with healthy grieving. Historically, psychiatry sometimes treated reports of seeing or hearing the dead as symptoms requiring concern. More recent research paints a more nuanced picture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 105 — Bereaved people often report having sen...</span></span></span>

Large reviews conclude that most sensory experiences of the deceased are benign. ## When Comfort and Concern Can Coexist

Although many grief visions are reassuring, they are not universally positive. Some people find them unsettling, particularly if they challenge personal beliefs about death or repeatedly reactivate feelings of loss. Research has found that a minority of bereaved individuals report increased sadness or distress following such encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Many respondents in the current study (60.1%)...</span></span></span>

Comfort and pain often coexist because the experience briefly restores a sense of connection while simultaneously reminding the person that the loved one is gone. A widow who hears a familiar voice may feel warmth and relief in the moment, then renewed grief when the experience ends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Many respondents in the current study (60.1%)...</span></span></span>

* Does the experience provide comfort or cause severe distress?
* Is the person otherwise functioning well in daily life?
* Does the experience fit within a normal grief process?

## Why Grief Visions Matter in the Study of Apparitions

Grief-related encounters challenge simplistic explanations of apparition reports. They demonstrate that seeing or sensing a figure is not always linked to fear, haunted locations, or ghost legends. Many of the most commonly reported apparition-like experiences occur in ordinary homes among people coping with loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 105 — Bereaved people often report having sen...</span></span></span>

For researchers, these experiences reveal how perception, memory, attachment, and emotion can combine to create a compelling sense of presence.
