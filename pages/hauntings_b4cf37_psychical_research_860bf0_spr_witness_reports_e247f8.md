## Introduction

When someone reports a haunting to the Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}) (SPR), the first stage is not an [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) of a building but the collection of testimony. The society’s reporting process is designed to capture fragile, often emotionally charged accounts while avoiding premature conclusions. Witnesses are encouraged to describe what happened, when it happened, who was present, and what evidence may exist, but the SPR does not treat a report as proof that a haunting has occurred. Instead, it treats the report as the beginning of a case file that may later be examined, compared with similar reports, or investigated further. The process reflects a long-standing SPR principle: gather information carefully, remain open to different explanations, and preserve records in a form that allows later scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. ## What the SPR Asks Witnesses to Provide

A haunting report is most useful when it contains specific details rather than general impressions. The SPR's guidance indicates that reports of ongoing [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) or poltergeist activity are reviewed through follow-up contact, which may include discussion of circumstances and, in some cases, an investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. ## Why Anonymity and Follow-Up Matter

One of the most distinctive features of the SPR's public reporting guidance is its explicit commitment to considering "the need to preserve anonymity" when dealing with haunting and poltergeist reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. In some...Read more...</span></span></span>

This is not merely a courtesy. Many witnesses fear ridicule, damage to professional reputations, unwanted media attention, or conflict with neighbours and family members. A person who believes they have experienced unexplained events may be more willing to provide a detailed account if they know their identity will not automatically become public. Anonymity can therefore improve the quality and completeness of testimony by reducing the social risks of reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. In some...Read more...</span></span></span>

Follow-up contact serves a second purpose. Initial reports are often incomplete. Witnesses may remember additional details, provide supporting documents, clarify timelines, or identify other people who were present. Investigators can also ask questions designed to separate direct observations from assumptions and rumours.

This process matters because haunting reports frequently evolve. A dramatic story told months later may differ significantly from notes taken close to the event. By maintaining contact with witnesses and preserving original accounts, researchers create a record that can be checked against later retellings. The resulting file may contain multiple versions of events, correspondence, investigator notes, and witness statements rather than a single narrative. That layered record is often more valuable than any individual claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/notes-investigators" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Notes for InvestigatorsIt is essential to ensure that during a &#x27;ghost-hunt&#x27; the times of all events and of the comings and goings of pers...</span></span></span>

### The Tension Between Privacy and Verification

Anonymity brings benefits but also creates challenges. Independent researchers cannot easily verify testimony when names, addresses, or identifying details are withheld. Critics of psychical research have long argued that anonymous reports can make assessment more difficult. At the same time, organisations that receive sensitive reports must balance public transparency against witness welfare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_Psychical_Research" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Society for Psychical Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society for Psychical Research</span></span></span>

The SPR's solution has generally been to preserve identifying information within case records while limiting unnecessary public disclosure. This allows investigators to conduct follow-up work without exposing witnesses to unwanted attention. The approach does not eliminate disputes over credibility, but it attempts to protect both evidence and participants.

## What the Society Will Not Promise

The SPR's public guidance is unusually clear about what reporting a haunting does not guarantee. The society states that it may discuss options, offer advice on the possible nature of reported phenomena, and in some circumstances conduct an investigation. However, it explicitly says that it cannot undertake to rid a location of apparent phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. In some...Read more...</span></span></span>

This limitation is important because many people contact paranormal organisations while seeking reassurance, confirmation, or a practical solution. The SPR instead positions itself as a research body. Its role is to collect information, evaluate claims, and preserve records rather than to certify that a haunting is genuine or to provide a remedy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the situation and available options. In some...Read more...</span></span></span>

The society's broader historical mission reinforces this stance. Since its foundation in the nineteenth century, the SPR has emphasised systematic inquiry and the examination of unusual experiences without adopting a corporate position on their ultimate cause. Reports are therefore treated as material for investigation rather than evidence of a predetermined conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/statement-aims-and-objectives-1883" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">statement aims and objectives 1883</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statement of Aims and Objectives (1883)The aim of the Society will be to approach these various problems, without prejudice or prepossess...</span></span></span>

## Why the Reporting Process Matters

The most significant contribution of the SPR reporting system may be neither investigation nor publicity but preservation. Haunting claims often begin as private experiences known only to a few people. Without a structured reporting process, those experiences can disappear, become distorted through [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}), or survive only as folklore.

By encouraging detailed reports, protecting witness anonymity when necessary, and conducting follow-up inquiries without promising a verdict, the SPR creates records that can be revisited years later. Whether a case ultimately appears paranormal, psychological, environmental, fraudulent, or unresolved, the value of the file lies in preserving what witnesses actually reported and how those reports were assessed.
