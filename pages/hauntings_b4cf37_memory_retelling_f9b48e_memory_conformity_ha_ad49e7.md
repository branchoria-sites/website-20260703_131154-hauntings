## Introduction

When several people experience a supposedly haunted event together, their stories often become more alike over time. This does not necessarily mean that anyone is inventing details. Research on memory conformity shows that people can unintentionally absorb information from one another during later conversations and then remember those details as if they had witnessed them personally. In the context of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), where experiences are frequently ambiguous and emotionally charged, this process can make separate accounts appear to independently confirm one another even when some details originated in discussion rather than direct observation. Studies of eyewitness memory, social influence and paranormal belief suggest that group [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}) can be an important mechanism in the growth of haunted reputations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1385&amp;context=ajacourtreview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcommons.unl.edu">[Digital Commons+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcommons.unl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital CommonsMemory Conformity Between Eyewitnessesby F Gabbert · 2012 · Cited by 38 — The consequences of source-monitoring errors can...</span></span></span>

## How Memory Conformity Works

Memory conformity refers to the tendency for one person's recollection to influence another person's memory of the same event. The effect is well established in eyewitness research. In classic experiments, participants viewed slightly different versions of an event, discussed what they saw and then completed individual memory tests. After discussion, people frequently reported details that had only been visible to the other witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.885" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMemory conformity: can eyewitnesses influence each other&#x27;s...The current study investigated memory conformity effect...</span></span></span>

Several psychological processes help explain why this happens:

* **[Source-monitoring]({{ 'ghost-memories/' | relative_url }}) errors:** People remember a detail but later forget whether they observed it directly or heard it from someone else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1385&amp;context=ajacourtreview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcommons.unl.edu">[Digital Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcommons.unl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital CommonsMemory Conformity Between Eyewitnessesby F Gabbert · 2012 · Cited by 38 — The consequences of source-monitoring errors can...</span></span></span>
* **Informational influence:** When individuals are uncertain, they may assume another witness noticed something they missed and adopt that information as more accurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/108300/7/WRAP-eyewitness-memory-distortion-following-discussion-countries-Wade-2018.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wrap.warwick.ac.uk">[Warwick Research Archive Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wrap.warwick.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">WRAP eyewitness memory distortion following discussion countries Wade 2018</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Warwick Research Archive PortalIto et al co-witness memory multilab study JARMAC_Accepted...by H Ito · 2019 · Cited by 46 — Research sho...</span></span></span>
* **Social contagion of memory:** Repeated exposure to another person's recollection can make that recollection feel familiar and therefore true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196174" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkSocial contagion of memory &#124; Psychonomic Bulletin &amp; ReviewWe report a new paradigm for studying false memories implan...</span></span></span>

Importantly, memory conformity does not require deliberate deception. Participants in laboratory studies often express genuine confidence in details that originated from another person. In some cases, confidence can even increase after group discussion, making altered memories feel more convincing to both the witness and later audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/files/64063698/Sauerland_2018_The_effects_of_co_witness_discussion_on_confidence.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl">[Maastricht University+2Pure Portsmouth]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sauerland 2018 The effects of co witness discussion on confidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maastricht UniversityThe effects of co-witness discussion on confidence and...by J Rechdan · 2018 · Cited by 17 — It is a well-establish...</span></span></span>

For haunting reports, this matters because many experiences begin as uncertain perceptions: a fleeting shape, an unexplained sound, a sensation of [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }}) or a glimpse seen in poor lighting. Such experiences leave room for interpretation, making them especially vulnerable to later social influence.

## Why Haunted Experiences Are Especially Susceptible

Haunting accounts often involve circumstances known to increase memory distortion. Witnesses may be frightened, surprised, tired, emotionally aroused or exposed to strong expectations about a location. Under those conditions, memory tends to be less complete and more open to reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13218710802620380" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online@WalshMedical]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There was no difference in memory...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Ghost Reports After Group Discussion

The influence of discussion is particularly relevant because many ghost reports are collected after witnesses have already spoken extensively with one another.

Eyewitness studies repeatedly show that discussion can cause accounts to converge. Participants exposed to another witness's mistaken information often later reproduce that information during individual recall tests. Researchers refer to this as co-witness contamination because the resulting agreement may create the appearance of independent corroboration when the memories are no longer truly independent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.1640" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryCombating Co‐witness contamination: Attempting to...by HM Paterson · 2011 · Cited by 87 — Witnesses who discuss an e...</span></span></span>

In haunted settings, group conversations frequently occur immediately after an incident:

* Guests discuss what happened during a ghost tour.
* Family members compare impressions after a strange night in a house.
* [Investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) teams review experiences together.
* Online communities collectively analyse alleged paranormal encounters.

Each discussion provides opportunities for memories to become aligned. A witness who initially recalls only a shadow may later remember a "woman in white" after hearing another person's description. Someone uncertain about where a sound originated may come to remember it as occurring in a specific room because that version became dominant within the group.

Research also suggests that conformity becomes more likely when people believe another observer had a better view, better memory or greater expertise. In paranormal investigations, experienced investigators, tour guides or highly confident witnesses may therefore exert disproportionate influence on collective recollection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6762151/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The effects of perceived memory ability on memory conformity...by LA Monds · 2019 · Cited by 19 — The current study aimed to expand o...</span></span></span>

## Why Similar Stories Are Not Automatically Independent Evidence

One reason memory conformity matters is that agreement between witnesses is often treated as a marker of reliability. If three people independently describe the same apparition, many listeners assume the account is stronger than a single report.

Psychological research shows that this assumption can be misleading when witnesses have communicated. After discussion, memories can become increasingly similar even if some of the shared details are inaccurate. The resulting consistency may reflect social influence rather than multiple independent observations of the same phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264905204_Memory_Conformity_Between_Eyewitnesses" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Memory Conformity Between Eyewitnesses</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Memory Conformity Between Eyewitnesses - ResearchGateWhen false details encountered during a discussion are later repor...</span></span></span>

This does not mean witness agreement is meaningless. Shared reports can still contain valuable information. The key issue is whether the agreement emerged before or after witnesses compared notes. A detail independently reported by multiple people before discussion carries different evidential weight from a detail that appeared only after group conversation.

For hauntings, this distinction is often difficult to reconstruct because stories may be recorded long after the original event, by which time witnesses have retold and refined their experiences many times.

## Separating Independent Testimony

Researchers studying eyewitness reliability have developed practices intended to reduce memory conformity. Although designed largely for legal investigations, the same principles are useful when evaluating haunting reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1385&amp;context=ajacourtreview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcommons.unl.edu">[Digital Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcommons.unl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital CommonsMemory Conformity Between Eyewitnessesby F Gabbert · 2012 · Cited by 38 — The consequences of source-monitoring errors can...</span></span></span>

Key indicators of more independent testimony include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Witnesses record experiences before discussing them with others.
* Accounts are collected separately and promptly.
* Early notes or recordings can be compared with later retellings.
* Investigators preserve uncertainties rather than forcing agreement.
* Differences between accounts are documented instead of edited away.

## What Memory Conformity Means for Haunted Reputations

Within the broader cycle of memory, retelling and haunted reputation, memory conformity helps explain how a collection of loosely connected experiences can evolve into a stable and seemingly well-corroborated ghost story. Group discussion can gradually standardise descriptions, reinforce particular interpretations and eliminate ambiguity.

The result is often a haunting narrative that appears supported by many matching testimonies. Yet psychological research suggests that some of this convergence may arise from ordinary social processes rather than from repeated encounters with the same paranormal phenomenon. Understanding memory conformity does not prove or disprove any specific haunting claim. Instead, it highlights a critical question for evaluating witness stories: not only what people remember, but when, how and with whom those memories were formed and shared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196174" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Nature Link+2Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer Nature LinkSocial contagion of memory &#124; Psychonomic Bulletin &amp; ReviewWe report a new paradigm for studying false memories implan...</span></span></span>
