## Introduction

Electronic voice phenomena, usually shortened to EVP, are alleged voices found on audio recordings after a haunting [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}), séance-style session or night-time vigil. The claim is simple and powerful: a recorder placed in a quiet room captures words that no one present heard at the time. The evidence problem is just as simple: the “voice” is usually brief, noisy, low in volume and open to interpretation. In practice, EVP sits where haunting belief meets the psychology of listening. A clip can feel compelling because the human brain is excellent at finding speech-like patterns in unclear sound, especially when a suggested phrase, location story or [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) of ghosts has already framed what the listener is about to hear. Laboratory work has repeatedly shown that priming, degraded speech and random noise can make people report voices even when the paranormal interpretation is not required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">129-134 Applied... These shows commonly feature electronic voice phenomena (EVP)...Read more...</span></span></span>

That does not mean every EVP witness is lying or foolish. It means that recordings are not neutral evidence just because a machine captured them. Audio has to pass through microphones, compression, interference, room acoustics, editing choices and, finally, a listener’s expectations. EVP remains one of the most popular tools in modern ghost hunting precisely because it turns the invisible haunting into something replayable — but replayable is not the same as reliable.

## What people report when they say they captured a ghost voice

EVP reports usually begin with an apparently quiet recording session. Investigators may ask questions aloud in a supposedly haunted room, leave pauses for a response, place a recorder in an empty space overnight, or review background audio from a vigil. Later, while listening back through headphones, they identify a word, whisper, breath, name, answer or short phrase that appears to respond to the setting or question. In haunting culture, this feels especially persuasive when the phrase seems personal: a name associated with the building, a reply to a question, or a sentence that appears to match local ghost lore.

The phenomenon became prominent through mid-twentieth-century experiments with tape recorders and radio noise. Swedish artist and film-maker Friedrich Jürgenson reported hearing unexpected voices while recording birdsong in 1959, and Latvian psychologist Konstantin Raudive later popularised the idea through large numbers of claimed recordings, often described as brief words or phrases. Earlier experiments by Attila von Szalay and Raymond Bayless had also tried to record voices of the dead, using insulated cabinets and tape equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

The modern version is more portable and more theatrical. Digital recorders, phone apps, “spirit boxes”, radio sweep devices and night-vision investigation formats have made EVP a routine part of ghost-hunting practice. The core claim, however, has barely changed: a weak signal appears in noisy audio, and the listener treats it as a voice from someone unseen.

Within haunting reports, “disembodied voice” can mean two slightly different things. Sometimes witnesses say they heard a voice directly in the room, without any recorder. At other times, no one hears anything live, but a voice-like sound is found later on a recording. The second kind is EVP in the stricter sense. It is also the more testable claim, because other listeners can replay the same clip — although, as the evidence shows, they may not agree on what it says.

## Why unclear audio becomes meaningful

The central natural explanation for EVP is auditory [pareidolia]({{ 'pareidolia/' | relative_url }}): the perception of meaningful sound, especially speech, within ambiguous noise. This is the hearing equivalent of seeing a face in clouds or a figure in shadows. It is not a rare defect. It is part of ordinary perception. Human beings are tuned to notice speech, and in uncertain conditions the brain often uses expectation to fill gaps.

That is especially important for EVP because the clips are commonly surrounded by cues. A listener may be told the recording was made in a haunted prison, that investigators had just asked “Who are you?”, or that the faint sound says “Get out.” Once a phrase has been suggested, it can become difficult not to hear it. Research on auditory pareidolia and EVP has directly examined this problem. ## Natural explanations to check before calling a voice paranormal

A responsible EVP assessment starts with ordinary audio problems. Most alleged clips are not recorded in controlled studio conditions. They are made in [old buildings]({{ 'old-buildings/' | relative_url }}), empty corridors, basements, hotels, outdoor ruins, abandoned sites or domestic spaces where acoustics, electronics and human movement are hard to control.

Common non-paranormal sources include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Distant human speech:** neighbours, passers-by, other investigators, staff, radio chatter, traffic, televisions and conversations through walls can all become distorted by distance and reverberation.
* **Recorder handling noise:** fingers on a casing, cable movement, clothing rustle, breath, footsteps or tripod vibration can sound voice-like when amplified.
* **Radio-frequency interference:** older recording methods and some EVP practices deliberately involve radio noise or untuned frequencies, which increases the chance of stray speech fragments or broadcast artefacts.
* **Compression and digital artefacts:** small recorders and phone apps may reduce file size or process sound in ways that create burbles, clipped transients and unnatural textures.
* **Environmental repetition:** fans, boilers, pipes, wind, rain, insects, floorboards and distant engines can form rhythmic patterns that invite speech-like interpretation.
* **Prompting and subtitles:** once a phrase is written on screen or announced by an investigator, later listeners often hear the clip through that suggestion.

</div>

Forensic audio research is relevant here even though EVP is not a courtroom discipline. Specialists dealing with poor-quality recordings know that indistinct audio is vulnerable to transcription and interpretation problems. A 2024 Journal of the Audio Engineering Society article on forensic speech enhancement warned that one of the easiest ways to make unclear audio seem clearer is to provide a transcript — but if the transcript is wrong, it can still strongly prime the listener into hearing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://naturefilm.montana.edu/rmaher/publications/fraser_maher_etal_jaes_1124.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturefilm.montana.edu">[naturefilm.montana.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturefilm.montana.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forensic Speech Enhancement: Toward Reliable Handling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forensic Speech Enhancement: Toward Reliable Handling</span></span></span>

That warning maps neatly onto EVP videos and investigation shows. A faint hiss is played once, then replayed with a caption. The caption may not merely help the audience understand the sound; it may help create the experience of understanding it. A clip labelled “I died here” is not the same perceptual object as the same clip played without any [suggested words]({{ 'suggested-words/' | relative_url }}).

There is also the problem of selection. A long vigil may produce hours of silence, bumps and hiss, but only the most interesting few seconds are shared. The audience rarely hears the failed sessions, alternative interpretations, raw files, microphone positions, full timeline or competing mundane sounds. By the time an EVP reaches a viewer, it is often a highlight, not a dataset.

## What controlled attempts have found

The strongest EVP claims would need more than a spooky phrase. They would need repeatable capture under conditions that rule out ordinary sound, interference, fraud, expectation and post-hoc interpretation. Controlled attempts have struggled to reach that level.

One important case is psychologist Imants Barušs’s “Failure to Replicate Electronic Voice Phenomenon”, published in the Journal of Scientific Exploration in 2001. Barušs attempted EVP-style recording across 81 sessions, totalling more than 60 hours, using methods informed by Raudive and instrumental transcommunication practice. He reported some voice-like events, but concluded that the results were too few, too random and too open to interpretation to count as a strong replication of paranormal EVP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwo.scholaris.ca/items/cad630f5-9022-4913-9e27-26c16c3860f0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwo.scholaris.ca">[UWO Scholaris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwo.scholaris.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scholaris.ca.</span></span></span>

This finding is useful because it does not depend on dismissing the subject without testing. Barušs could find sounds that resembled voices in a weak sense, but that is precisely the issue: finding voice-like sound is not enough. The harder question is whether the sound is anomalous, intelligible without prompting, independently agreed upon by listeners, and linked to information not already available by normal means.

EVP supporters sometimes respond that spirit voices are faint, intermittent or dependent on special conditions. That keeps the claim alive, but it also makes it difficult to test. If the voice appears only after extensive filtering, only to some listeners, only in a suggestive setting, or only when a phrase is supplied in advance, then the evidence shifts away from a recorded external speaker and towards listener interpretation.

Some parapsychological and EVP-oriented publications have argued for more favourable interpretations, including experiments in screened rooms or listening-panel approaches. But the recurring difficulty is independent agreement. If seven people are asked to choose from five possible phrases, they may converge more than chance in some conditions, but that is still very different from open-ended listeners independently hearing the same sentence without guidance. The stricter the test becomes, the less robust EVP tends to look as evidence of haunting.

## Why EVP still feels persuasive in hauntings

EVP persists because it meets several human needs at once. It makes a haunting seem measurable, it produces a souvenir from an investigation, and it gives an apparently direct voice to a story that would otherwise be only [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}). In a dark building, a recorded whisper can feel like the missing link between local legend and personal encounter.

It also has a distinctive emotional structure. Many EVP sessions are built around questions: “Are you here?”, “What is your name?”, “Can you speak to us?” A later sound that seems to answer one of those questions is more compelling than a random creak because it appears intentional. Even a fragmentary word can feel like communication if it arrives in the right narrative slot.

The history of EVP also helps sustain it. Jürgenson, Raudive and later EVP groups framed the tape recorder not as a passive machine but as a bridge between worlds. That idea fits a broader haunting tradition in which new technologies repeatedly become ghost technologies: photography, telephones, radios, televisions, tape recorders, digital cameras and apps have all been used to turn unseen presences into evidence-like artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronic voice phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic voice phenomenon</span></span></span>

There is a cultural reason too. EVP is easy to share. A witness account asks the audience to trust memory; a recording lets the audience participate. Viewers can put on headphones, replay the clip and decide whether they hear the phrase. That participation is part of the attraction, but it is also part of the risk. The listener becomes a collaborator in making meaning from noise.

Modern media amplifies the effect. Paranormal television and online clips often present EVP as a dramatic reveal: silence, headphones, shocked faces, then the replay. Nees and Phillips explicitly noted the role of paranormal reality television in popularising EVP-style evidence while often borrowing the appearance of investigation without the safeguards of controlled research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">129-134 Applied... ## How to listen critically without dismissing the experience

A fair test of an EVP clip should separate three questions: whether there is a sound, whether it is speech, and whether it is paranormal speech. Those are not the same claim. A clip may contain a real acoustic event but not a voice. It may contain a voice but not a ghost. It may sound like a specific phrase only after the listener has been told what to expect.

A useful critical approach is to ask for the least processed version first. Heavy filtering, noise reduction, reversing, slowing, boosting and repeated looping can make audio feel more revealing while also creating artefacts. The raw file, recording time, device model, microphone placement and full session context matter. So does the question of who was present, where other people were, whether radios or phones were operating, and whether the same phrase is heard by blind listeners.

The best quick test is blind listening. Play the clip to several people without telling them it is paranormal, where it was recorded, or what words they are “supposed” to hear. Ask them to write down what they hear before any discussion. If their answers scatter across unrelated phrases, the clip is probably ambiguous. If they independently report the same words, the next question is still not “therefore ghost”, but “what ordinary source could have produced those words?”

The transcript problem deserves special caution. Captions should come after independent listening, not before. In EVP culture, the phrase is often part of the evidence; in perception science, the phrase may be part of the stimulus. Once supplied, it can change the sound the listener experiences.

A strong EVP claim would therefore need several features at once: clean audio, secure control of the recording environment, no plausible human or electronic source, minimal processing, blind agreement among independent listeners, preservation of the full recording, and ideally repeated results under comparable conditions. Most haunting EVP clips fall far short of that standard.

## What EVP can and cannot prove

EVP recordings can prove that people found a sound meaningful. They can sometimes prove that a recording captured a noise, a voice, or an artefact not noticed at the time. They can also document how a haunting investigation unfolded, what participants expected, and how a place’s story shaped interpretation. That makes EVP valuable as cultural evidence: it shows how ghost stories are produced, shared and made audible.

What EVP has not established is a reliable method for recording the dead. The best-supported explanations remain ambiguity, expectation, auditory pareidolia, environmental noise, technical artefacts, contamination and selective interpretation. Controlled work has not produced a stable body of recordings that are clearly anomalous and independently intelligible as disembodied voices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uwo.scholaris.ca/items/cad630f5-9022-4913-9e27-26c16c3860f0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwo.scholaris.ca">[UWO Scholaris+2PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwo.scholaris.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scholaris.ca.</span></span></span>

This is why EVP is such a revealing subtopic within [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}). It looks, at first, like the most technological form of ghost evidence: a machine catches what the human ear missed. On closer inspection, it exposes the human part of haunting evidence more sharply than almost anything else. The recorder may capture sound, but the haunting often appears in the act of listening.

    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
