## Introduction

One of the most striking findings in the science of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) is that the brain can sometimes generate the feeling of another person nearby by misidentifying signals that actually come from the self. In certain circumstances, the systems that track body position, movement, touch, and personal identity fall temporarily out of sync. When that happens, a person may experience a vivid sense that someone is standing behind them, following them, or occupying the same space. Neuroscientists call this a [felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }}) or presence hallucination. In some cases, witnesses describe it as a shadowy figure that mirrors their actions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

This [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) is especially relevant to reports from allegedly haunted locations because it offers a neurological explanation for one of the most common paranormal experiences: the certainty that an unseen person is nearby. Rather than being a visual ghost sighting, the experience begins with a disturbance in how the brain constructs the sense of one's own body and self.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MeSH terms.Read more...</span></span></span>

## When the Brain Invents Someone Behind You

The brain continuously combines information from muscles, joints, skin, balance organs, and movement commands to maintain an internal model of the body. Most of the time this process is seamless. We know where our limbs are, which movements we initiated, and which sensations belong to us.

Research suggests that when this body model becomes unstable, the brain can misattribute some of its own signals to an external agent. Instead of recognising a sensation as self-generated, it interprets it as evidence that another person is nearby. The result is not simply a feeling of anxiety or unease. It is often a highly specific social perception: someone is behind me, beside me, or watching me.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25447995/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Cell]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and robot-controlled induction of an apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — These data show that the illusion o...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why many haunted-place reports involve an unseen presence rather than a detailed apparition. The experience emerges from systems involved in self-location and agency before any visual image is constructed. The witness may never see a ghost, yet still feel absolutely certain that another individual occupies the room.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

## The Temporoparietal Junction Case

The most famous demonstration came from a neurological study involving a woman undergoing evaluation for epilepsy surgery. While researchers electrically stimulated a region called the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), she repeatedly reported the presence of another person standing close behind her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MeSH terms.Read more...</span></span></span>

The TPJ sits where information about the body, movement, touch, and spatial awareness is integrated. It plays a major role in distinguishing self from other and in maintaining a coherent sense of bodily ownership.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632275/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">disturbed self-processing at the temporo-parietal junctionby O Blanke · 2005 · Cited by 752 — OBEs are related to a failure to inte...</span></span></span>

What made the case remarkable was the behaviour of the perceived figure. The patient described a "shadow" person that copied her posture and movements. When she sat, the figure sat. When she changed position, it changed position. During one task, she even felt that the presence was interfering with her actions. Researchers concluded that the brain had created an externalised representation of aspects of her own body rather than detecting a genuine person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6805163_Induction_of_an_illusory_shadow_person" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Induction of an illusory shadow person Arzy et al</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2006) found that stimulating the left temporoparietal junction in epilepsy patients induced an own-body illusion, creating the perceptio...</span></span></span>

The experiment provided unusually direct evidence that a sensed presence can arise from disrupted body processing rather than from an external stimulus. It also gave neuroscience one of its clearest examples of the so-called "shadow person" effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MeSH terms.Read more...</span></span></span>

## Mirrored Posture and Movement

The shadow-person phenomenon is distinctive because the perceived figure often behaves like a distorted duplicate of the experiencer.

In the TPJ stimulation study, the sensed person appeared to mirror the patient's body configuration. Rather than acting independently, it seemed linked to her movements. This suggested that the brain was projecting part of its own body representation into nearby space and then interpreting that projection as someone else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6805163_Induction_of_an_illusory_shadow_person" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Induction of an illusory shadow person Arzy et al</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2006) found that stimulating the left temporoparietal junction in epilepsy patients induced an own-body illusion, creating the perceptio...</span></span></span>

Researchers later explored the same idea using robotic systems. In a landmark experiment, volunteers moved a device with their hands while a robot delivered corresponding touches to their backs. When the timing was accurate, participants simply felt touched. When researchers introduced a delay, creating a mismatch between movement and sensory feedback, many participants began reporting a strong feeling that another person was standing nearby, often directly behind them. Some found the sensation unsettling enough to stop the experiment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822%2814%2901212-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cell.com">[Cell+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cell.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and Robot-Controlled Induction of an Apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — These data show that the illusion of...</span></span></span>

The key factor was not fear, suggestion, or belief in ghosts. It was the disruption of sensorimotor synchrony. The brain expected one pattern of feedback and received another. Faced with that mismatch, it generated the impression of a second agent occupying nearby space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822%2814%2901212-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cell.com">[Cell+2Scientific American]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cell.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and Robot-Controlled Induction of an Apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — These data show that the illusion of...</span></span></span>

These studies support a broader conclusion: under certain conditions, the feeling of "someone else" may originate from the brain's attempt to explain conflicting information about the self.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25447995/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and robot-controlled induction of an apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — These data show that the illusion o...</span></span></span>

## Why the Presence Feels So Real

A common misunderstanding is that people who report a sensed presence are merely imagining something vague. Neurological evidence suggests otherwise.

The brain systems involved are the same systems used to determine where the body is located and where other people are located. When those systems produce an error, the resulting experience inherits the realism normally associated with ordinary perception. The witness is not consciously inventing a presence; they are experiencing what feels like a genuine social perception generated by the same machinery that normally tracks real people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

## What This Means for Ghost Reports

The body-signal mismatch model does not explain every haunting claim, nor does it prove that all reports arise from the same mechanism. What it does show is that one of the most compelling features of hauntings—the feeling that an unseen person is standing nearby—can be produced through identifiable changes in brain processing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 32 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

This is particularly important because many haunting accounts begin not with a visual apparition but with a sensed presence. A witness enters a corridor, staircase, bedroom, or cellar and suddenly feels watched. They may later interpret that sensation through local ghost stories, personal beliefs, or the reputation of the location. Yet the initial experience itself may originate from a disturbance in how the brain integrates body-related information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25447995/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and robot-controlled induction of an apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — These data show that the illusion o...</span></span></span>
