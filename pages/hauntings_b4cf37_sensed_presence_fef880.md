## Introduction

A sensed presence is the feeling that someone unseen is close by: behind the shoulder, at the edge of a room, standing beside the bed, or watching from a doorway. In haunted places, this can be more powerful than a visible apparition because it arrives as certainty rather than as a doubtful glimpse. The person may hear nothing and see nothing, yet still feel that the room is occupied.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_sensed_presence_fef880-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Sensed Presence" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The strongest evidence does not show that sensed presence is proof of a ghost. It shows that the experience is real to the person having it and can arise from several overlapping mechanisms: [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), darkness, stress, [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}), sleep states, odd acoustics, low-frequency vibration, and the brain’s own handling of body and self. Neuroscience has even induced presence-like experiences by disrupting how the brain matches bodily signals, while haunting studies suggest that suggestion and setting can make people more likely to report strange sensations in particular rooms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25447995/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and robot-controlled induction of an apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — The strange sensation that somebody...</span></span></span>

That makes sensed presence one of the most useful haunting experiences to examine. It sits exactly where ghost stories, perception, place, and physiology meet.

## What visitors describe at Sensed Presence

People who describe a sensed presence rarely present it as a vague mood. They often describe it as spatial, social, and directed: “someone is behind me”, “the room was not empty”, “I was [being watched]({{ 'being-watched/' | relative_url }})”, or “it felt as if a person had come in”. The important feature is that the “other” is felt as nearby even when there is no clear sight, voice, touch, or footprint.

Researchers often call this a “[felt presence]({{ 'felt-presence/' | relative_url }})” or “feeling of presence”. A 2023 clinical and cognitive review defines it as the basic feeling that someone else is present in the immediate environment without clear sensory evidence. The review also notes that these experiences can be ambiguous, comforting, menacing, personified, or hard to identify, and that they occur in contexts ranging from neurological illness and sleep paralysis to endurance situations and spiritual communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036623000342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The felt-presence experience: from cognition to the clinicby JM Barnby · 2023 · Cited by 33 — The felt presence experience i...</span></span></span>

In haunting reports, the presence often has three qualities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Location:** it is not merely “I felt odd”, but “someone was in that corner”, “behind me”, or “at the end of the corridor”.
* **Agency:** the presence seems aware, watching, approaching, following, guarding, or waiting.
* **Emotional charge:** it can feel protective, sorrowful, hostile, intrusive, or oppressive, depending on the setting and the story already attached to the place.

</div>

This is why a haunted room can feel “occupied” even when nothing dramatic happens. The experience is not built from a full apparition; it is built from the mind’s social alarm system filling in an unseen agent.

## Why a Room Can Feel Occupied Without a Ghost

A sensed presence is often treated as a ghost report because it feels interpersonal. The person does not simply notice a cold draught or a creaking floorboard; they feel another mind nearby. That leap from sensation to presence is the key [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}).

Human perception is not a passive recording of the world. The brain constantly predicts what is likely to be there, especially under uncertain conditions. In a dark old building with unfamiliar sounds, poor sightlines, uneven temperature, and a reputation for haunting, small signals can be interpreted as social signals. A creak becomes movement. A draught becomes a passing body. A sudden feeling of dread becomes “someone is here”.

This does not mean the witness is pretending. It means that the experience may be a genuine perception-like event, assembled from incomplete sensory information and strong expectation. Studies of allegedly haunted places have repeatedly found that belief, suggestion, and environmental ambiguity can shape reports of unusual experiences. In investigations at Hampton Court Palace and the [South Bridge Vaults]({{ 'vaults/' | relative_url }}) in Edinburgh, Richard Wiseman and colleagues examined reports in places with established haunted reputations and concluded that some “haunting” experiences could be partly explained by people responding to normal factors in their surroundings rather than by ghostly activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.richardwiseman.com/resources/BJP-hauntings.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: richardwiseman.com">[richardwiseman.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">richardwiseman.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">An investigation into alleged &#x27;hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An investigation into alleged &#x27;hauntings</span></span></span>

The effect is especially plausible for sensed presence because it requires less external evidence than a sighting. A person does not need to misidentify a figure; they only need to misattribute unease, bodily arousal, or an ambiguous cue to an unseen agent.

## Environmental and perceptual factors around Sensed Presence

A careful haunting assessment should not begin by asking whether the witness is gullible. It should ask what conditions might have made the experience more likely. Sensed presence is often produced by a stack of small influences rather than one simple cause.

**Suggestion and reputation** are among the most obvious. If visitors are told that a particular room is haunted, they may monitor their body and surroundings more closely. Normal sensations that would pass unnoticed elsewhere can become evidence. In the “Haunt” project, Christopher French and colleagues built an artificial haunted room and exposed 79 participants to infrasound, complex electromagnetic fields, both, or neither. Many participants reported unusual sensations, including experiences associated with haunted places, but the pattern was not clearly tied to the manipulated environmental conditions; the findings pointed strongly towards suggestibility and expectation as important contributors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010945208001299" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

**Infrasound** is another candidate, but it should be handled carefully. Infrasound means very low-frequency sound, below the usual range of conscious human hearing. Older buildings, basements, ventilation systems, traffic, machinery, boilers, and vibrating pipes can all produce low-frequency vibration. A 2026 study in *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* found that exposure to 18 Hz infrasound was linked to higher salivary cortisol and more negative self-reported mood, even though participants could not reliably tell whether the infrasound was present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span></span></span>

That finding supports a modest but useful explanation: infrasound may not “make people see ghosts”, but it can create discomfort that is easy to misread in a haunted setting. Reporting on the same study, the *Guardian* quoted psychologist Chris French cautioning that infrasound may help explain vague haunted feelings but should not be stretched into an explanation for alleged moving objects or full visual apparitions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/apr/27/spooky-feelings-in-old-houses-may-be-caused-by-boiler-sounds-study-suggests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led by Prof. Rodney Schmaltz at MacEwan University, previous experiments at haunted attractions showed inconclusive results, prompting fu...</span></span></span>

**Sleep paralysis** is especially important for bedrooms, hotels, old houses, and overnight vigils. During sleep paralysis, a person wakes partly into consciousness while the body remains temporarily paralysed from rapid-eye-movement sleep. The experience can include pressure on the chest, fear, buzzing, footsteps, shadowy figures, and a strong sense of an intruder. A 2017 paper in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* describes sleep paralysis hallucinations as occurring across sensory modes and including the sensing or seeing of menacing bedroom intruders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00092/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Sleep Paralysis, “The Ghostly Bedroom Intruder” and Out</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Sleep Paralysis, “The Ghostly Bedroom Intruder” and Out</span></span></span>

**Grief and bereavement** can also matter, though it belongs only partly to haunted-place reports. Some people sense a deceased loved one nearby, especially in familiar domestic spaces. A large study of after-death communications found that sensory experiences of the deceased are commonly reported by bereaved people and can be meaningful or comforting rather than frightening. This does not prove a haunting, but it explains why a “presence” in a house may be interpreted through memory and attachment as much as through fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8388006/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

**Building conditions** should be checked before a ghost story hardens into local fact. Low lighting, mirrors, blind corners, draughts, mouldy smells, carbon monoxide risk, loose windows, animals in roof spaces, intermittent heating, old plumbing, and sound travelling through walls can all create cues that feel like occupancy. The more a place already looks and sounds like a haunted setting, the easier it is for the body’s alert state to become a story about an unseen resident.

## What Brain Research Adds

The most striking research on sensed presence comes from neurology rather than ghost hunting. It suggests that the brain’s sense of “someone else” can sometimes arise from disrupted self-perception.

In 2006, Shahar Arzy, Olaf Blanke and colleagues reported a famous case in *Nature*. Electrical stimulation near the left temporoparietal junction, a region involved in combining sensory information about the body, induced the feeling of a “shadow person” close behind a patient being evaluated for epilepsy surgery. The presence seemed to mirror the patient’s posture and movements, suggesting that the brain had misattributed aspects of the person’s own body representation to another agent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988702/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Induction of an illusory shadow personby S Arzy · 2006 · Cited by 325 — Stimulation of a site on the brain&#x27;s left hemisphere prompt...</span></span></span>

In 2014, Blanke’s team went further. They studied people who had experienced a feeling of presence after neurological damage and then used a robot-controlled system to induce a similar illusion in healthy participants. When the robot created a mismatch between a person’s movement and a delayed touch on their back, some participants felt as if another person was nearby. The study argued that the illusion can arise when the brain misperceives the source and identity of sensorimotor signals: information from movement, touch, and body position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25447995/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neurological and robot-controlled induction of an apparitionby O Blanke · 2014 · Cited by 208 — The strange sensation that somebody...</span></span></span>

This does not explain every haunted room. It does explain why a presence can feel so definite without a visible figure. The brain is built to distinguish “me” from “not me”. When that boundary is stressed, delayed, confused, or interpreted in a threatening setting, the result can feel less like a thought and more like another person.

## Why Haunted Settings Make the Feeling Stick

A sensed presence becomes a haunting when the experience attaches to a place and survives [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}). One person may feel watched in a corridor; the next person enters already primed to notice the same corridor; the story then becomes part of how the building is experienced.

This place-based loop matters. A room with a known ghost story changes how people pay attention. Visitors may slow down, listen harder, scan corners, notice temperature changes, and interpret bodily sensations as external signs. In research on allegedly haunted environments, paranormal belief and suggestion repeatedly appear as factors that influence what people expect and report. Hampton Court Palace, for example, has been used as a real-world setting to test whether reports cluster around belief, suggestion, and measured environmental variables.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-the-alleged-haunting-of-hampton-court-palac-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.ed.ac.uk">[Edinburgh Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">an investigation into the alleged haunting of hampton court palac 2</span></span></span>

The loop is not simply “people believe, so they imagine”. It is more subtle:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. A location has a reputation, history, rumour, or atmosphere.
2. The visitor enters in a heightened state of attention.
3. Ambiguous cues become more noticeable.
4. Bodily unease is interpreted as presence.
5. The report reinforces the room’s reputation for the next visitor.

</div>

This helps explain why sensed presence is so durable in haunted places. It is easy to repeat, hard to disprove, and emotionally memorable. A photograph of an empty room proves little either way; the claim was never that something visible stood there, but that the room felt occupied.

## Why accounts of Sensed Presence endured

Sensed presence persists because it offers a powerful middle ground between ordinary unease and a full ghost sighting. It is less vulnerable to debunking than a photograph, less dependent on sound recording than a voice, and more intimate than a cold spot. The witness’s own body becomes the evidence.

It also fits the social function of hauntings. Haunted places are rarely just empty buildings. They are places where people expect the past to press into the present: old houses, prisons, theatres, castles, hospitals, hotels, tunnels, churches, battle sites, and family homes after a death. A sensed presence gives that expectation a bodily form. The past is not merely remembered; it feels as though it is standing in the room.

There is also a narrative advantage. A visible ghost may invite argument about lighting, costume, hoaxing, or misidentification. A sensed presence is harder to challenge without seeming to dismiss the witness. It belongs to the private zone of experience: “I know what I felt.” That makes it compelling in oral storytelling, guided tours, paranormal investigations, memoirs, and local folklore.

The best reading is neither automatic belief nor automatic ridicule. Sensed presence in haunted places is a real class of human experience with plausible natural mechanisms. It can be intensified by expectation, sleep disruption, grief, building conditions, low-frequency vibration, and the brain’s management of bodily selfhood. It may still remain meaningful to the person who felt it. The room may not contain a ghost, but the feeling that it is not empty can be one of the strongest reasons a haunting story survives.
