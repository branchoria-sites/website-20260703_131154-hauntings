## Introduction

Haunting stories often grow because memory and reputation feed each other. A person hears that a corridor is haunted, later notices a sound there, tells the story with a little more shape, and the next listener arrives with stronger expectations. Over time, the haunting may become sharper, not because new evidence is stronger, but because retelling selects the most memorable details, drops uncertainty, adds local history and turns a vague experience into a recognised “case”.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_memory_retelling_f9b48e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Retelling" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This does not mean every witness is lying. Memory is reconstructive: later information, discussion and repeated recall can alter what people sincerely remember. In haunted places, that normal human process is intensified by [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}), prior belief, tourism, media coverage and the social rewards of telling a good story. The result is a haunted reputation: a public identity attached to a building, road, room or landscape, sometimes long after the original events have become hard to verify.

## What people report

People rarely report a haunting in a vacuum. They report [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) in “the haunted wing”, a figure on “the staircase where someone died”, or a feeling of dread in “the room everyone avoids”. The place already has a script, and that script helps witnesses decide which details matter.

In many accounts, the first experience is ambiguous: a bang, a glimpse, a cold patch, a door moving, a voice heard half-asleep. The story becomes more convincing when it is retold alongside earlier reports. A single noise becomes “the same footsteps others heard”. A brief figure becomes “the woman in white”. A child’s fright becomes one more entry in a chain of testimony. Folklorists Diane Goldstein, Sylvia Grider and Jeannie Thomas describe ghost narratives as unusually slippery: the same story can be heard as truth, fiction, warning, entertainment or local tradition, depending on the teller and audience. That slipperiness is central to how [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) spread.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://read.upcolorado.com/read/haunting-experiences-ghosts-in-contemporary-folklore/section/44f30611-e723-4750-b20d-dc9a51ad470a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: read.upcolorado.com">[read.upcolorado.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">read.upcolorado.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upcolorado.com.</span></span></span>

The mechanism is easiest to see in famous haunted-house cases. Borley Rectory in Essex had ghost stories before Harry Price became involved, including reports of footsteps, [moving objects]({{ 'moving-objects/' | relative_url }}), phantom coach sounds and a spectral nun. But Price’s investigations and publications helped fix Borley as “the most haunted house in England”, turning local reports into a national reputation. Later Society for [Psychical Research]({{ 'research-files/' | relative_url }}) debate focused not only on whether the events were paranormal, but also on whether witnesses, residents, investigators and critics had exaggerated, misunderstood or fabricated parts of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Borley Rectory</span></span></span>

A similar reputation loop can be seen in the [Amityville]({{ 'amityville/' | relative_url }}) case. The house’s grim factual anchor was the 1974 DeFeo family murder. The haunting reputation came later, after George and Kathy Lutz claimed to have fled paranormal activity, then after Jay Anson’s 1977 book and the 1979 film made the story internationally famous. Later reporting notes that the claims have been widely disputed, that DeFeo’s lawyer said the story was invented with the Lutzes, and that later owners did not report the same phenomena. Yet the house remains culturally “haunted” because books, films and public curiosity gave the story a life beyond the original testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/the-amityville-horror/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: snopes.com">[Snopes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">snopes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the amityville horror</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the amityville horror</span></span></span>

Retelling also changes the role of the witness. In an ordinary setting, someone who heard a noise might say, “I do not know what it was.” In a haunted setting, the same person may be asked to place the experience within a known legend: Was it the nun? Was it the old owner? Did it happen at three in the morning? Those questions can sharpen a memory, but they can also steer it.

## Why retelling makes hauntings feel stronger

A haunting story usually becomes more powerful through selection. The strange parts survive; the dull parts disappear. Hesitations, failed investigations and ordinary explanations are less likely to be retold than a door slamming, a pale figure or a witness who “never believed in ghosts until that night”.

Memory research helps explain why this happens without assuming bad faith. Frederic Bartlett’s classic work on serial reproduction, often associated with “The War of the Ghosts”, showed that people tend to reshape unfamiliar stories into more familiar patterns as they retell them. Later research comparing repeated and serial reproduction found that information can decline markedly as it passes from person to person, even when the original material was the same.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211368114000485" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

For hauntings, this means a story may become more culturally fluent while becoming less precise. Local retellings often add familiar motifs: the tragic woman, the wronged servant, the murdered child, the monk, the soldier, the curse, the room nobody enters. These motifs make a haunting easier to remember and easier to pass on, but they can also make different stories sound more alike than the original reports did.

Psychologists also distinguish between remembering an event and remembering where a detail came from. False memory research describes “[source monitoring]({{ 'ghost-memories/' | relative_url }})” errors: a person may remember a detail but misattribute its source, treating something heard later as something personally witnessed. Reviews of false memory mechanisms identify source-monitoring failure as one route by which suggested or imagined details can feel like real recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10567586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That matters in haunted settings because stories circulate before, during and after experiences. A visitor may hear a guide mention a child’s footsteps, later hear a creak in an upper room, and later still remember the experience as hearing “the child”. The memory is not necessarily invented in a deliberate sense; it may be assembled from perception, expectation and later narration.

Repeated recall can also update memory. Northwestern research on retrieval-induced distortion found that recalling information can insert present details into past memory, and the university’s summary describes memory as adaptive rather than camera-like. In hauntings, each retelling can therefore become both a report and a rehearsal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2014/02/how-your-memory-rewrites-the-past" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.northwestern.edu">[Northwestern Now]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.northwestern.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Now How Your Memory Rewrites the Past</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Now How Your Memory Rewrites the Past</span></span></span>

## Natural explanations to check

A haunted reputation should not be treated as proof that witnesses are dishonest, but it should change how claims are checked. The more famous a haunting becomes, the more likely people are to arrive primed, compare notes and interpret ordinary ambiguity through the legend.

The strongest first step is to separate immediate records from later retellings. Notes, audio, photographs, maintenance logs, weather records, floor plans and independent witness statements made before discussion are usually more useful than polished versions told years later. The Enfield Poltergeist case illustrates why. Modern documentary discussion of Maurice Grosse’s tapes emphasises that they include interviews recorded soon after alleged events as well as long periods of household recording; that makes them valuable historically, even though the case remains disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jerryrothwell.com/2023/10/26/sounds-and-stories-conjuring-the-enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jerryrothwell.com">[JERRY ROTHWELL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jerryrothwell.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JERRY ROTHWELLSounds and Stories: conjuring &#x27;The Enfield Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JERRY ROTHWELLSounds and Stories: conjuring &#x27;The Enfield Poltergeist</span></span></span>

Several natural mechanisms are especially relevant when a haunting’s reputation has already formed:

* **Memory conformity:** people who discuss an event may absorb details from one another’s accounts. Eyewitness research on social contagion of memory has shown that post-event social information can distort later recall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Meade-Roediger-2002_MemCog-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychnet.wustl.edu">[Psychnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychnet.wustl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Meade Roediger 2002 MemCog 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meade Roediger 2002 MemCog 1</span></span></span>
* **Misinformation effects:** misleading later information can alter memory for an earlier event. Reviews of the misinformation effect describe both laboratory and real-world cases in which later descriptions influence recall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3213001/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCA Behavioral Account of the Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PMCA Behavioral Account of the Misinformation Effect</span></span></span>

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Expectation and attention:** once people know a place is “haunted”, they listen differently. Normal house sounds, animal movement, plumbing, wind, traffic vibration or neighbouring rooms may be noticed more intensely and remembered as part of the pattern.
* **Narrative compression:** later versions often combine separate incidents, witnesses and dates into one cleaner story. That makes the haunting easier to tell, but harder to test.
* **Authority transfer:** a guide, investigator, journalist, author or film can make a weak story seem stronger simply by repeating it confidently or placing it beside real tragedy.

</div>

The key question is not “Could this ever be paranormal?” but “What changed between the first experience and the version now being circulated?” If the named ghost, tragic backstory or dramatic timing appears only in later accounts, it should be treated as part of the haunting’s reputation, not as secure evidence about the original event.

## How reputation attaches to a place

Once a place is known as haunted, the reputation can become part of its public identity. People visit because of the story; visitors produce more stories; businesses, guides and media repeat the most marketable version; the place becomes harder to imagine without the haunting.

[Ghost tourism]({{ 'ghost-tourism/' | relative_url }}) research shows how this works. Goldsmiths researchers studying British ghost walks argue that the uncertainty of ghosts lets guides turn tragic history into playful entertainment in a way that differs from more solemn dark tourism. The ghost story creates permission to move between history, fear, humour and disbelief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gold.ac.uk/news/spectral-cities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gold.ac.uk">[Goldsmiths, University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gold.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Goldsmiths, University of London Study reveals secrets behind ghost tour boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goldsmiths, University of London Study reveals secrets behind ghost tour boom</span></span></span>

Academic work on ghost walks in British cities similarly argues that hauntings are relayed to tourists in ways that embed multiple histories in the visitor’s experience of the city. A ghost tour is not just a list of claims; it is a route, a performance and a memory-making device. A street corner becomes memorable because a story was told there, and the story feels stronger because it was heard in the place itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/76092/1/Accepted_Manuscript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk">[UEA Digital Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UEA Digital Repository Death and living memories in the dark tourism of British</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UEA Digital Repository Death and living memories in the dark tourism of British</span></span></span>

This is why haunted reputations can outlive buildings, owners and evidence. Borley Rectory was demolished in the 1940s, yet its reputation persists in books, investigations and paranormal culture. The Amityville house has been renovated, renumbered and treated by later owners as an ordinary private home, but its haunted identity remains because film and publishing made it symbolic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.umbc.edu/specialcollections/garrett/hauntings/borley-rectory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.umbc.edu">[AOK Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.umbc.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umbc.edu.</span></span></span>

Reputation can also scale from a single building to a whole town. Salem, Massachusetts, is not simply “haunted” in the narrow sense of one ghost report, but its identity shows how memory, tragedy, tourism and retelling can transform a place. The city’s Haunted Happenings festival presents Salem as a major Halloween destination, while reporting on Salem tourism notes that the meanings of “witch” there now include historical victims of the 1692 trials, modern pagan identity and Halloween entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hauntedhappenings.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hauntedhappenings.org">[Haunted Happenings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hauntedhappenings.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hauntedhappenings.org.</span></span></span>

The risk is that reputation flattens history. A murder becomes a spooky attraction. A religious conflict becomes a costume. A disputed witness report becomes a slogan. The haunting grows, but the real people and uncertainties behind it may become less visible.

## Why the story persists

Haunting stories persist because they do several jobs at once. They explain unease, give places personality, connect the present to the dead, entertain visitors and offer communities a memorable way to talk about danger, injustice or loss.

That flexibility is a strength. A ghost story can be a warning about a dangerous road, a way of remembering a former resident, a tourist hook, a family anecdote, a local joke or a serious expression of belief. Contemporary folklore scholarship stresses that personal belief, cultural tradition and commercial representations of ghosts continually feed one another rather than staying neatly separate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329235834_Haunting_experiences_Ghosts_in_contemporary_folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate Haunting Experiences: Ghosts in Contemporary Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate Haunting Experiences: Ghosts in Contemporary Folklore</span></span></span>

The persistence is also emotional. Stories linked to trauma, death or injustice can feel morally unfinished. Recent discussion of Lake Lanier in Georgia, for example, shows how a place’s haunted or cursed reputation can combine drownings, submerged hazards, local ghost stories and unresolved histories of displacement and racial violence. For some people, the “haunting” is less about proving ghosts than about naming a past that still feels present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2025/dec/23/lake-lanier-haunted-atlanta-georgia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Local lore is fueled by ghost stories, including the famous &quot;Lady of the Lake&quot; and spirits said to tug swimmers underwater. Visitors repo...</span></span></span>

A haunting reputation therefore survives even when individual details are challenged. Debunking one photograph, one witness or one historical claim may weaken a case, but it rarely erases the story if the place still feels symbolically charged. The legend has already moved from “what happened?” to “what this place means”.

This is why careful readers should hold two ideas together. First, retelling can genuinely distort memory, making haunted stories sound cleaner and more certain than the evidence allows. Secondly, the persistence of a haunting is itself evidence of cultural meaning. A ghost story may fail as proof of a ghost and still succeed as a record of fear, grief, tourism, local identity and the human habit of turning strange moments into shared memory.
