## Introduction

Many of the most convincing “haunting” experiences reported after a bereavement occur in a bedroom and, more specifically, during the unstable moments between sleeping and waking. People describe hearing a dead spouse call their name, feeling someone sit on the mattress, sensing a familiar presence beside them, or briefly seeing a figure standing near the bed. These encounters often feel more real than dreams because they occur when the person believes they are awake or partly awake. Research on bereavement experiences, sleep transitions, and sleep paralysis suggests that this combination of [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}), disrupted sleep, and altered states of consciousness creates conditions in which vivid perceptions can emerge and be interpreted as visitations or [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 104 — Includes sensory experiences (hearing...</span></span></span>

This does not settle whether such experiences are supernatural. What it does explain is why the bedroom becomes such a common stage for grief-related hauntings. ## Why the Bed Becomes a Haunting Hotspot

Bedrooms carry emotional weight long before any unusual experience occurs. A surviving partner may continue sleeping on the same side of the bed, reaching for someone who is no longer there, or maintaining routines associated with the deceased. The room becomes a place where memory, [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}), and absence repeatedly collide.

Researchers studying sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased have found that many reports involve ordinary domestic settings and familiar routines. The bereaved often experience a continuing sense that the deceased remains nearby, especially in locations strongly associated with the relationship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sensory and Quasi-Sensory Experiences of the Deceased in...by KS Kamp · 2020 · Cited by 104 — Includes sensory experiences (hearing...</span></span></span>

The bed is particularly significant because it combines emotional memory with a biological state that is already vulnerable to perceptual distortions. Unlike a daytime sighting in a busy environment, the bedroom is dark, quiet, repetitive, and closely linked to sleep-related changes in consciousness. These conditions make unusual perceptions easier to notice and harder to dismiss.

## Voices and Figures at the Edge of Sleep

The periods immediately before sleep (hypnagogia) and immediately after waking (hypnopompia) are well-known for producing vivid sensory experiences. During these transitions, dream-like imagery can blend with awareness of the real environment. A person may hear a voice, see a face, or feel a touch while simultaneously believing they are awake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.02342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1704.02342</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXivSleep Paralysis: phenomenology, neurophysiology and ...by E Solomonova · 2017 · Cited by 41 — Feeling of paralysis may be accompanie...</span></span></span>

## When Mattress Pressure Feels Like a Presence

Among the most haunting reports are sensations that someone has sat on the bed, climbed onto the mattress, or is physically present beside the sleeper.

Sleep researchers have documented tactile hallucinations and sensed-presence experiences during sleep paralysis and other sleep-wake transition states. People frequently report feeling [pressure]({{ 'pressure/' | relative_url }}), weight, touch, or bodily proximity even when no one is physically present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Jonathan-Egan-3/publication/236282103_Sleep_Paralysis/data/5a7053a2a6fdcc33daa8b2c6/Murphy-and-Egan-2010-Sleep-Paralysis.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep Paralysis and Hallucinations:September 28, 2010 — The first factor was called “Intruder” and it consisted of fear, a se...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 28, 2010</span></span></span>

In grief-related contexts, these sensations can acquire a specific identity. Rather than feeling an unknown presence, the experiencer may immediately conclude that the sensation belongs to a deceased spouse, parent, child, or pet. The interpretation often arises because the bodily feeling matches a deeply familiar memory: the weight of a partner getting into bed, a hand resting on a shoulder, or a pet jumping onto a blanket.

This is one reason mattress-pressure experiences are especially persuasive. They do not merely involve seeing something. They recreate bodily expectations that may have been repeated thousands of times during a relationship.

## How Grief Disrupts Sleep and Perception

Bereavement frequently alters sleep. Many grieving people experience insomnia, fragmented sleep, vivid dreaming, repeated awakenings, and changes in sleep timing. These disruptions increase the number of opportunities for unusual experiences to occur during transitional states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00302228221093895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Has sleep been disrupted by grief, stress, or trauma?">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Has sleep been disrupted by grief, stress, or trauma?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sagepub.com.</span></span></span>

Sleep fragmentation matters because it repeatedly places the brain in a partially awake condition. Instead of moving smoothly between sleep and wakefulness, the person may enter brief mixed states where elements of dreaming continue while awareness returns.

Grief also changes attention. The bereaved often remain highly attuned to reminders of the deceased. Small sounds, shadows, or bodily sensations may be interpreted through the lens of loss. This does not mean the experiences are imaginary in the everyday sense; the sensations themselves can be vivid and genuine. The question is how they are generated and interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cruse.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-sense-someone-whos-died/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Was the experience centred on a deceased loved one?">[Cruse+2White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Was the experience centred on a deceased loved one?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cruse.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Studies of bereavement hallucinations consistently find that such experiences are common and are not, by themselves, evidence of psychiatric illness. Many people find them comforting rather than distressing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cruse.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-sense-someone-whos-died/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Was the experience centred on a deceased loved one?">[Cruse+2British Psychological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Was the experience centred on a deceased loved one?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cruse.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## The “Bedroom Intruder” Effect

One of the most striking findings from sleep-paralysis research is the frequency of the so-called “intruder” experience. During sleep paralysis, individuals may awaken unable to move while simultaneously sensing a presence in the room. Some see shadowy figures, hear [footsteps]({{ 'footsteps/' | relative_url }}) or voices, or feel that someone is watching them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00092/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersSleep Paralysis, “The Ghostly Bedroom Intruder” and Out...by B Jalal · 2017 · Cited by 49 — We have proposed that a functional...</span></span></span>

The brain attempts to explain an intense feeling of presence and may generate the impression that another being is nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00092/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersSleep Paralysis, “The Ghostly Bedroom Intruder” and Out...by B Jalal · 2017 · Cited by 49 — We have proposed that a functional...</span></span></span>

## Practical Checks Before Assuming a Haunting

When an experience occurs in bed or during awakening, several questions can help place it in context:

1. **Did it happen while falling asleep or waking up?** Experiences clustered around these moments are consistent with hypnagogic or hypnopompic states. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.02342)

2. **Was movement difficult or impossible?** Temporary paralysis strongly suggests a sleep-paralysis episode. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11344621/)

3. **Has sleep been disrupted by grief, stress, or trauma?** Sleep fragmentation increases opportunities for transitional experiences. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00302228221093895)

4. **Was the experience centred on a deceased loved one?** Bereavement-related sensory experiences are common and often occur without signs of mental illness. [Cruse+2PMC](https://www.cruse.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-sense-someone-whos-died/)

5. **Did the experience feel comforting, frightening, or both?** Emotional tone can shape how the event is remembered and interpreted. Some people experience reassurance, while others experience intense fear. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280118642_Experiences_of_continued_presence_On_the_practical_consequences_of_%27hallucinations%27_in_bereavement)

These questions do not prove or disprove a supernatural explanation. They simply identify known mechanisms that frequently accompany bedroom hauntings reported during bereavement.

## Why These Experiences Feel So Real

The enduring mystery of bedside ghost experiences lies in their realism. They often involve multiple senses, occur in familiar surroundings, and emerge during emotionally charged periods of life. A grieving person may hear a voice, feel a touch, and see a figure all within seconds of waking. Because the event unfolds in a recognisable bedroom rather than a dream landscape, it can feel indistinguishable from ordinary reality.

Modern sleep research suggests that waking consciousness is not an instantaneous switch. During the transition out of sleep, dream imagery, bodily sensations, memory, and perception can overlap. Bereavement adds another powerful ingredient: the continuing mental presence of someone whose absence is difficult to absorb. Together, these factors help explain why grief ghosts so often appear by the bed, and why the resulting experiences can remain among the most convincing hauntings a person ever reports.
