## Introduction

Objects that seem to move by themselves are among the most difficult haunting claims to assess because they move the story from [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) into alleged physical evidence. A cold spot, a footstep or a fleeting figure can be explained as perception, acoustics or [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}); a chair crossing a room, stones falling indoors or ornaments flying from shelves appears to demand a mechanism. That is why poltergeist cases sit close to hauntings but are usually treated as a special category: repeated physical disturbances, often focused on a household, a young person, or a particular room.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_poltergeist_claims_6ad8be-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Moving Objects" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The strongest answer is not that all such cases are “proved ghosts”, nor that every witness is lying. The evidence is more awkward. Poltergeist reports are numerous, culturally persistent and sometimes involve multiple witnesses, but they are also unusually vulnerable to [hoaxing]({{ 'hoaxing/' | relative_url }}), misremembering, poor observation, environmental causes and the fact that the decisive movement is often not seen from start to finish. Serious [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) therefore starts with a narrower question: what exactly moved, who saw the beginning of the movement, what normal forces were excluded, and what changed once observation became tighter?

## What people report when objects move

In poltergeist accounts, the physical disturbance is usually not a single dramatic event. It is a run of incidents: knocking sounds, thuds, thrown objects, furniture shifting, pictures falling, bedding disturbed, stones or small items appearing indoors, and occasionally claims of fires, water, electrical effects or writing. The Society for Psychical Research places poltergeists alongside hauntings, while noting that in many cases they have been considered an effect of living minds rather than simple evidence of survival after death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/9-hauntings-and-poltergeists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

The Psi Encyclopedia, published by the Society for Psychical Research, defines poltergeist reports as anomalous disturbances connected with a place or person, including raps, knocking and the “often violent movement of furniture and other objects”. It also notes the core interpretive split: some accounts are read as ghostly or spirit-like, some as living-agent effects, and some as trickery or natural events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/poltergeists-overview/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Poltergeists (Overview) – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

Typical moving-object reports fall into a few practical groups:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Small thrown objects:** stones, toys, coins, ornaments, cutlery or household items apparently thrown when no one is seen throwing them.
* **Furniture movement:** chairs, beds, chests of drawers or tables said to slide, tip, jump or shift position.
* **Object displacement:** items vanish from one place and are found somewhere else, sometimes in locked rooms or unlikely locations.
* **Impact events:** objects hit walls, people, doors or ceilings, leaving marks or sounds but little clear evidence of launch.
* **Patterned arrangements:** objects are found stacked, lined up, arranged into words or placed in symbolic positions.

</div>

These claims feel evidential because they appear to leave a trace. Yet the trace often proves less useful than expected. A broken plate proves that a plate broke, not that it moved without human contact. A chair found on its side proves a changed state, not the cause of the change. The crucial evidential moment is usually the beginning of the movement, and that is precisely the part many reports lack.

## Why poltergeist claims are harder than ordinary haunting stories

A normal haunting report often turns on experience: “I heard footsteps”, “I saw a figure”, “I felt someone behind me”. Poltergeist claims turn on interaction with matter. That raises the evidential bar. If an object really moves without contact, the claim challenges ordinary expectations about force, friction, gravity and human agency. But if the object’s movement is only inferred after the fact, the case may be no stronger than any other ambiguous household event.

This is where poltergeist cases become so difficult. They can involve sincere witnesses and still fail to prove an extraordinary cause. A person may honestly report that “no one could have moved it” because no one was seen doing so, because the room seemed empty, or because the witness looked away just before the event. In a busy household, especially during fear, sleep disruption, family stress or media attention, those gaps matter.

A 2024 phenomenological review in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* summarised object movement as one of the typical reported features of poltergeist cases, including claims of non-ballistic trajectories, stones or other projectiles, and objects apparently behaving in ways that witnesses found hard to reconcile with ordinary throwing. The same review also highlights a key problem for investigators: detailed observation can affect whether movements are reported or captured, and many cases rely on testimony rather than continuous controlled recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofscientificexploration.org/index.php/jse/article/view/3263/2181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofscientificexploration.org">[Journal of Scientific Exploration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofscientificexploration.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on journalofscientificexploration.org.</span></span></span>

That does not make every report worthless. It means the evidence has to be sorted by quality. A case where several people saw an object already in motion is different from one where a camera records the object’s whole movement, and both are different from a case where an object is merely found in a surprising place. Much of the argument over poltergeists comes from treating these levels as if they were the same.

## The Enfield problem: famous, witnessed, and still contested

The [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) case is a useful example because it contains almost everything that makes moving-object claims compelling and problematic. In 1977, Peggy Hodgson reported disturbances at her council house in Enfield, north London, involving her daughters Janet and Margaret. The story grew to include furniture movement, knocking sounds, thrown objects, voices and alleged levitation, and it attracted journalists, police attention and investigators linked to the Society for Psychical Research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.timeout.com/london/blog/five-reasons-why-londons-most-famous-poltergeist-case-is-a-hoax-061616" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timeout.com">[Time Out Worldwide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timeout.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on timeout.com.</span></span></span>

For believers, Enfield matters because there were outside witnesses, including a police officer said to have seen a chair move, and because the case produced photographs, recordings and investigator notes. For sceptics, those same materials show the weaknesses of the case: the children were often central to the activity, key events were not clearly captured from beginning to end, and some phenomena were admitted or suspected to be faked. Psychologist Chris French has argued that the sisters admitted faking some activity and that the famous “levitation” photograph is readily explained as Janet jumping from the bed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.timeout.com/london/blog/five-reasons-why-londons-most-famous-poltergeist-case-is-a-hoax-061616" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timeout.com">[Time Out Worldwide]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timeout.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on timeout.com.</span></span></span>

Joe Nickell, writing in *Skeptical Inquirer*, approached Enfield from the perspective of a stage magician and investigator. His general criticism of poltergeist cases is that many effects resemble what a mischievous person could produce when observers are frightened, distracted or eager to see a pattern. In Enfield, the strongest sceptical reading is not that every witness deliberately lied, but that a mixture of child trickery, adult interpretation, media escalation and weak controls made ordinary causes look extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2012/07/enfield-poltergeist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer Enfield Poltergeist &#124; Skeptical Inquirer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptical Inquirer Enfield Poltergeist &#124; Skeptical Inquirer</span></span></span>

Enfield persists because neither side gets everything it wants. Sceptics can point to admitted fakery and poor controls; believers can point to the number of witnesses and the emotional force of the family’s testimony. The case shows why moving-object claims are so sticky: even when some incidents are exposed or doubted, supporters can retreat to “not all of it was fake”, while sceptics reply that once trickery is established in the central setting, the burden of proof becomes much heavier.

## Natural explanations to check first

The first rule in assessing moving-object claims is not to begin with ghosts, demons or psychokinesis. It is to begin with the object, the room and the circumstances. A good investigation asks what normal force could have acted on the object and whether that force was actually excluded.

**Human handling and trickery** are the most important checks. This includes deliberate hoaxing, attention-seeking, practical jokes, family conflict, concealed throwing, thread, magnets, pre-positioned objects, staged damage, and incidents performed when adults briefly leave or look away. Poltergeist cases have long been associated, fairly or unfairly, with adolescents in the household. The psychological literature on poltergeist cases notes that early researchers observed many disturbances occurring in the presence of a child or young person, and later parapsychologists developed “recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis” as a living-agent model for repeated disturbances around a focal person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

**Ordinary building movement** should come next. Loose shelves, uneven floors, warped wood, poorly hung pictures, slamming doors, pressure changes, rodents, pets, vibration from traffic, washing machines, boilers or nearby works can all produce movement or impacts. A plate that creeps to the edge of a shelf over days is a different claim from a plate that launches across a room under observation. Investigators should test surfaces for tilt, vibration, air movement and repeatable mechanical triggers.

**Air currents and pressure changes** can matter more than witnesses expect. Open chimneys, extractor fans, draughts, doors closing elsewhere in the building, and heating systems can move light objects, swing doors, rattle frames or make hanging items knock against walls. These causes usually cannot explain heavy furniture flying across a room, but they can explain many smaller “it moved by itself” incidents.

**Sound and bodily unease** can feed the interpretation around the movement. Recent research on infrasound — sound below the usual range of conscious hearing — found that exposure was linked to higher salivary cortisol and more negative self-reports even when participants could not reliably detect the sound. That kind of finding is more relevant to why a house feels oppressive than to why a chair would fly, but it can help explain why ambiguous bumps and vibrations are interpreted as haunting activity in the first place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2026.1729876/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Frontiers Infrasound exposure is linked to aversive responding,</span></span></span>

**Health and safety checks** should never be skipped. Carbon monoxide poisoning can cause headache, dizziness, weakness, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, and severe exposure can be fatal. It does not explain a thrown stone, but it can affect perception, judgement and memory in a household reporting strange events. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists confusion among common carbon monoxide symptoms and warns that people can lose consciousness or die when exposure is high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

## The living-agent idea: stress, focus people and psychokinesis

One reason poltergeist cases sit slightly apart from ordinary hauntings is that many researchers have not treated them as dead-person stories at all. In the living-agent model, the apparent centre of the outbreak is a person in the household, often someone under strain. The controversial parapsychological version is recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis, usually shortened to RSPK: repeated, involuntary physical effects supposedly produced by a living person rather than by a spirit.

The psychological aspects page of the Psi Encyclopedia notes that William Roll’s surveys linked many cases to a particular living person during periods of upheaval or family strain, while also acknowledging that neuropsychological findings remain suggestive rather than decisive. That distinction is important. Stress can help explain why a household becomes volatile, why attention focuses on one person, and why trickery or misinterpretation may arise. It does not, by itself, demonstrate mind-over-matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/psychological-aspects-poltergeist-cases/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Psychological Aspects of Poltergeist Cases – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

For a mainstream reader, the useful point is that “poltergeist” does not always mean “ghost”. Historically, it may mean at least four different hypotheses competing for the same story:

* a spirit or haunting acting on the physical environment;
* a living person unconsciously producing physical effects, as proposed in parapsychology;
* a living person consciously producing tricks;
* [natural causes]({{ 'natural-causes/' | relative_url }}), misperception and story-building.

The evidence needed for each explanation is different. A spirit hypothesis needs more than a strange movement; it needs a reason to connect the movement to a deceased person or haunting identity. A psychokinesis hypothesis needs reliable exclusion of normal forces and fraud. A hoax hypothesis needs opportunity, motive or exposure of method. A natural-cause hypothesis needs a plausible mechanism that fits the object, timing and room conditions.

## What would make a moving-object case stronger?

A moving-object claim becomes stronger when it survives controls that are designed before the next incident, not after the story has already become famous. Retrospective testimony can be valuable, but it is rarely enough for an extraordinary physical claim. The best cases would need clear documentation of the object before, during and after movement, with the possible human and environmental causes narrowed in advance.

Useful evidence would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* continuous video from more than one angle, showing the object, floor, ceiling, doorways and people in the room;
* a clear view of the start of the movement, not just the object already falling or already displaced;
* control over access to the room, including checks for strings, magnets, thrown objects and concealed people;
* measurements of vibration, floor tilt, air movement and nearby machinery;
* repeated events under conditions chosen by investigators rather than by the alleged phenomenon;
* preservation of damaged objects, impact marks and timestamps;
* willingness to revise the claim if ordinary causes are found.

</div>

The uncomfortable fact is that many famous cases become less clear when judged by those standards. They may contain sincere testimony, dramatic photographs, or impressive stories, but not the continuous, controlled record that would rule out ordinary movement, hidden handling or staged effects. That gap is why poltergeist cases remain culturally powerful but scientifically unresolved.

## Why the story persists

Moving-object stories persist because they occupy the most exciting middle ground in haunting lore. They are physical enough to feel stronger than a vague presence, but usually elusive enough to avoid final testing. A chair moves when no one is ready. A stone falls after the camera is pointed elsewhere. A crash is heard upstairs, but the room is reached after the event. The evidence is always almost there.

They also persist because they are socially contagious. Once a household is known as troubled or haunted, each new knock or fallen object is interpreted through the growing story. Visitors arrive primed to notice oddities. Journalists and investigators ask leading questions. Children or adults at the centre of attention may feel pressure to produce something, explain something, or keep a frightening story coherent. None of this requires everyone involved to be dishonest. It only requires ordinary people reacting to fear, attention and ambiguity.

There is also a deeper reason. Poltergeist claims turn domestic space against itself. The home is supposed to be stable: chairs stay where they are placed, doors behave, ornaments remain on shelves. When those ordinary expectations seem to fail, the haunting becomes intimate and invasive. That emotional force helps explain why even weakly evidenced cases can become famous, and why exposed fakery does not always end belief. For many readers, the lingering question is not only “Did a ghost move it?” but “What would it feel like if the place where I live stopped obeying ordinary rules?”

## How to read a poltergeist claim without being fooled

The most reliable approach is neither automatic belief nor automatic ridicule. Start with the physical claim and slow it down. What exactly moved? Was it seen to begin moving? Who was present? Could a person, pet, draught, vibration, slope or mechanical fault have acted on it? Was the object light or heavy? Was the event repeated under observation? Did the story change after media attention? Were any incidents admitted or shown to be faked?

A useful rule is to separate **reported impossibility** from **demonstrated impossibility**. “No one could have thrown it” is often a conclusion, not an observation. “The object was filmed from rest to impact, with all people visible and normal forces measured” is much stronger. Most poltergeist cases live in the space between those two statements.

That is what makes objects moving and poltergeist claims so difficult. They are not easy to dismiss as mere atmosphere, but they rarely provide the clean evidence needed to prove an extraordinary mechanism. They remain one of the most memorable branches of haunting reports because they promise physical proof, while repeatedly showing how hard physical proof is to obtain.

    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
