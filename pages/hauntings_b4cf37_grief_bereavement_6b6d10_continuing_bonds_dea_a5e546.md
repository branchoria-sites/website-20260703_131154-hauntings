## Introduction

For much of the twentieth century, [grief]({{ 'grief/' | relative_url }}) was often described as a process of “letting go” of the dead. Healthy adjustment was frequently assumed to require emotional detachment, withdrawal of attachment, and eventual closure. Modern bereavement research has challenged that assumption. A growing body of evidence suggests that many bereaved people maintain an ongoing relationship with the deceased through memories, conversations, rituals, sensed presence experiences, dreams, values, and continuing influence on daily life. Rather than signalling a failure to move on, these continuing bonds are often a normal part of adaptation to loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12891240/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCContinuing Bonds in Marriage, Death and Divorce</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIHby SS Rubin · 2024 · Cited by 6 — The shift from the detachment model of mourning to the continuing bonds paradigm in bereavemen...</span></span></span>

This shift matters for understanding [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}) and grief. Experiences that feel like contact with the dead—such as sensing a loved one's presence or feeling guided by them—may function not as signs of pathological attachment but as ways of maintaining meaning, identity, and connection after death. At the same time, not every ongoing bond is beneficial. The key question is no longer whether ties to the dead should continue, but how those ties affect the bereaved person's ability to live.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37336784/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Open Research Surrey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The impact of continuing bonds following bereavementby H Hewson · 2024 · Cited by 123 — Following bereavement, continuing bonds (CB...</span></span></span>

## What Continuing Bonds Means in Grief

The modern continuing bonds perspective emerged as a critique of older grief theories that treated detachment as the primary goal of mourning. Researchers such as Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman, and Steven Nickman argued that relationships do not simply disappear when someone dies. Instead, the relationship changes form. The deceased may remain psychologically present through memory, internal dialogue, family narratives, values, and symbolic acts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12891240/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Whats your Grief]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCContinuing Bonds in Marriage, Death and Divorce</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIHby SS Rubin · 2024 · Cited by 6 — The shift from the detachment model of mourning to the continuing bonds paradigm in bereavemen...</span></span></span>

Under this view, grief is not about erasing attachment. It is about reorganising it.

* Talking to the deceased privately.
* Seeking guidance by imagining what they would say.
* Visiting graves or meaningful places.
* Keeping treasured possessions.
* Maintaining family rituals connected to the person.
* Experiencing dreams or sensed-presence encounters.
* Living according to values learned from the deceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37336784/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Hospiscare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The impact of continuing bonds following bereavementby H Hewson · 2024 · Cited by 123 — Following bereavement, continuing bonds (CB...</span></span></span>

## Why the “Cutting Ties” Model Lost Influence

The decline of strict detachment models was driven partly by observation. Bereaved people frequently reported ongoing connections to the dead even years after loss. Parents who had lost children, widowed spouses, and bereaved children often described continuing relationships that appeared comforting and meaningful rather than pathological. Researchers increasingly found that many psychologically healthy mourners did not sever their bonds at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12891240/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2Whats your Grief]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCContinuing Bonds in Marriage, Death and Divorce</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIHby SS Rubin · 2024 · Cited by 6 — The shift from the detachment model of mourning to the continuing bonds paradigm in bereavemen...</span></span></span>

The older assumption also struggled to explain why people commonly preserve photographs, tell stories about the deceased, celebrate anniversaries, or consult an internal image of a dead loved one when making important decisions. These practices are widespread across cultures and historical periods. Complete emotional detachment appears less common than earlier theories suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hospiscare.co.uk/how-we-help/advice-support/information-about-grief/understanding-grief-continuing-bonds-grief-model/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hospiscare.co.uk">[Hospiscare+2Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hospiscare.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Understanding grief: Continuing bonds grief modelAs well as helping loved ones to grieve, the continuing bonds model proposes t...</span></span></span>

## How Sensed Presence Can Support Meaning

Within the context of hauntings and bereavement experiences, one of the most significant forms of continuing bonds is the sensed presence experience. A bereaved person may feel that the deceased is nearby, perceive a familiar touch, hear a voice, encounter a vivid dream, or experience a powerful awareness that the loved one remains present in some way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/view/pdfCoverPage?download=true&amp;filePid=13140657760002346&amp;instCode=44SUR_INST" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openresearch.surrey.ac.uk">[Open Research Surrey+2British Psychological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openresearch.surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although this experience tends to be reported as...Read more...</span></span></span>

Research suggests these experiences are often interpreted positively. Many people report reassurance, comfort, reduced loneliness, or a renewed sense of connection. Rather than disrupting grief, the experience may help the bereaved maintain continuity between life before and after the death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/view/pdfCoverPage?download=true&amp;filePid=13140657760002346&amp;instCode=44SUR_INST" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openresearch.surrey.ac.uk">[Open Research Surrey+2British Psychological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openresearch.surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although this experience tends to be reported as...Read more...</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms may explain why such experiences can be helpful:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Identity continuity:** The deceased remains part of the survivor's self-understanding.
* **Meaning reconstruction:** The relationship continues to provide guidance and significance.
* **Emotional regulation:** Feelings of abandonment or isolation may be softened.
* **Narrative coherence:** The death becomes integrated into an ongoing story rather than experienced as a complete rupture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6819407_Continuing_Bonds_and_Reconstructing_Meaning_Mitigating_Complications_in_Bereavement" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2SUDC Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Continuing Bonds and Reconstructing MeaningThe authors assessed the relation between continuing bonds coping and meanin...</span></span></span>

</div>

## The Risk of Romanticising Continuing Bonds

The popularity of continuing bonds theory has occasionally produced a misunderstanding: that maintaining a connection with the deceased is always beneficial. The evidence is more complicated.

Researchers have repeatedly noted that continuing bonds can be adaptive, maladaptive, or mixed depending on their form and context. Some ongoing connections help mourners engage with life. Others become associated with persistent distress, yearning, or difficulty accepting the reality of death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20034720/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2adela&#x27;s community]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">There is lack of clarity in the scientific literature concerning the adaptive functions of continuing versus relinquishing bonds to decea...</span></span></span>

A crucial distinction concerns flexibility. Adaptive bonds tend to evolve over time. The bereaved person recognises the death while still maintaining an emotional relationship. Maladaptive bonds may become rigid, preventing adjustment to changed circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735809001627" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Continuing bonds in adaptation to bereavementby M Stroebe · 2010 · Cited by 402 — In this article we have proposed a new int...</span></span></span>

Researchers continue to debate whether certain bonds cause distress or simply reflect it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16972374/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Continuing bonds and grief: a prospective analysisby PA Boelen · 2006 · Cited by 191 — That is, continuing bonds through recovering...</span></span></span>

## When Ongoing Bonds Need Extra Help

Most continuing bonds do not require clinical intervention. However, professionals may become concerned when the relationship with the deceased contributes to severe impairment, persistent suffering, or an inability to function in daily life.

Potential warning signs include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Extreme social withdrawal centred on maintaining contact with the deceased.
* Inability to acknowledge that the death occurred.
* Persistent and overwhelming yearning that does not ease over time.
* Significant disruption of work, relationships, or self-care.
* Experiences involving the deceased that generate fear, confusion, or dangerous behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37336784/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The impact of continuing bonds following bereavementby H Hewson · 2024 · Cited by 123 — Following bereavement, continuing bonds (CB...</span></span></span>

## A Different Way to Understand Haunting

Viewed through the continuing bonds perspective, many bereavement-related hauntings look less like intrusions from the dead and more like ongoing relationships adapting to absence. A voice heard in a moment of crisis, a vivid dream, a sense of companionship in an empty house, or the feeling that a deceased parent still influences important decisions can all be understood as expressions of an enduring bond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/view/pdfCoverPage?download=true&amp;filePid=13140657760002346&amp;instCode=44SUR_INST" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openresearch.surrey.ac.uk">[Open Research Surrey+2British Psychological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openresearch.surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although this experience tends to be reported as...Read more...</span></span></span>

This does not settle questions about whether the dead are objectively present. Continuing bonds theory remains largely agnostic about supernatural explanations. What it does suggest is that many experiences commonly described as hauntings occur within a broader human process of maintaining connection after loss. From this perspective, the opposite of healthy grieving is not staying connected to the dead. It is becoming trapped in a relationship that can no longer adapt.
