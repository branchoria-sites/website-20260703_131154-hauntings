## Introduction

Psychical research societies and haunting files matter because they show how ghost stories become records: witness statements, letters, cuttings, photographs, case notes, published reports, catalogues and, sometimes, counter-investigations. In the field of [hauntings]({{ 'hauntings/' | relative_url }}), these institutions do not merely ask whether a house is “really haunted”. They decide what counts as a report, how to protect witnesses, how to classify recurring experiences, when to visit a site, what natural explanations to test, and which unresolved stories are preserved for future readers.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/hauntings_b4cf37_psychical_research_860bf0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Research Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The best-known example is the Society for Psychical Research, founded in London in 1882, whose archives now include material on [apparitions]({{ 'apparitions/' | relative_url }}), hauntings, poltergeists and famous disputed cases such as Borley Rectory and the [Enfield]({{ 'enfield/' | relative_url }}) poltergeist. Its own public guidance is cautious: the society says it holds no corporate opinion, may advise on possible explanations, and cannot “rid” a location of phenomena. That is the central governance point. Haunting files are not verdict machines. They are systems for collecting fragile testimony, preserving controversy, and making later re-examination possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paranormal Experience? &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Experience? &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

## Why haunting files exist at all

A haunting usually begins as a private problem: a family hears knocks, hotel staff see a figure, visitors feel watched in a particular corridor, or objects appear to move without an obvious cause. Without an institution to receive such accounts, the story may remain a local rumour, a newspaper item, or an anecdote shaped by [retelling]({{ 'retelling/' | relative_url }}). Psychical research societies offered a different route: send in the report, name the witnesses where possible, preserve the details, and compare the case with earlier reports.

The Society for Psychical Research’s “Hauntings and Poltergeists” index shows this archival habit in miniature. It treats hauntings as repeated sightings of the same apparition in one location, places poltergeists nearby, and groups discussions of [Borley]({{ 'borley/' | relative_url }}) Rectory under the same research heading. The entries are not all endorsements. One early Shropshire case is indexed with “poltergeist/cheating”, noting that admissions of trickery did not account for every reported incident. That mixed language is important: the file keeps both the suspicion and the remaining claim in view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/9-hauntings-and-poltergeists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

This makes psychical research archives different from ghost-tour storytelling. A tourist version of a haunted place often smooths the tale into a neat legend: the grey lady, the murdered monk, the cursed room. A research file is messier. It may include names, dates, floor plans, rival interpretations, witness disagreements, letters from sceptics, and later attempts to explain what earlier investigators missed. The file does not necessarily make the haunting stronger; it makes the chain of evidence more inspectable.

## Who keeps the files on haunted places?

The most important keepers are not always ghost-hunting clubs. They are libraries, archives, universities and long-running psychical research bodies that have inherited the paperwork of investigators.

The SPR’s archive is the central British example. Its own archive description says it has collected articles, correspondence, photographs, audio-visual material, artefacts, equipment and journals since 1882. Much of the documentary archive is held at Cambridge University Library, while audio-visual material is kept separately and can be consulted by arrangement. The Psi Encyclopedia’s archive guide states that the manuscripts alone extend to about 160 linear metres and that the collection covers topics including poltergeists, survival research, séances, reincarnation and apparitions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/archive-society-psychical-research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The Cambridge catalogue and related archive descriptions show why this matters for hauntings specifically. The SPR archive includes hundreds of unpublished haunting and poltergeist cases with geographical information, a major Borley Rectory correspondence collection, and the complete Grosse and Playfair material on the Enfield poltergeist, including correspondence, cuttings and articles. These are not just “ghost stories”; they are administrative and evidential bundles that allow later researchers to ask how the case was built.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/archive-society-psychical-research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

Other collections broaden the map. Senate House Library’s [Harry Price]({{ 'harry-price/' | relative_url }}) Library of Magical Literature, based on the collection of psychical researcher Harry Price, contains nearly 13,000 books, pamphlets and periodical titles on magic, psychical phenomena, ghosts, spiritualism, conjuring and related subjects. Its strength in conjuring is not incidental: a serious haunting file often needs knowledge of fraud, misdirection and stage effects as well as sympathy for witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.london.ac.uk/about/services/senate-house-library/collections/printed-special-collections/harry-price-library-magical-literature" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: london.ac.uk">[University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">london.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on london.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

In the United States, the American Society for Psychical Research describes itself as the oldest psychical research organisation in the country and says its library and archives are a significant repository for American and scientific history. The Parapsychology Foundation’s Eileen J. Garrett Research Library, now preserved at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County, holds more than 10,000 volumes on topics including ghosts, poltergeists, spiritualism, healing research and psychic espionage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aspr.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aspr.com">[aspr.com+2aspr.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aspr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aspr.com.</span></span></span>

There are also broader international routes into the material. The Institut für Grenzgebiete der Psychologie und Psychohygiene lists parapsychology archives including the SPR, the ASPR, Duke University’s Institute for Parapsychology archive, the Institut Métapsychique International in Paris, the College of Psychic Studies in London and Archives for the Unexplained in Sweden. The practical result is that haunting files are dispersed: a single tradition of ghost [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) is spread across societies, university libraries, private deposits and specialist archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://igpp.de/en/archive/archives-collections-on-parapsychology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: igpp.de">[igpp.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">igpp.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives / Collections on Parapsychology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives / Collections on Parapsychology</span></span></span>

## What people report

Haunting files usually preserve the raw experiences that later summaries compress. The recurring reports include apparitions, footsteps, raps, thrown objects, cold sensations, sudden fear, electrical oddities, voices, object disappearances and the feeling of a presence. In formal psychical research, the first task is often to separate the type of report before asking what caused it.

The SPR’s current reporting page asks for witness statements where possible and says witnesses may be contacted, with anonymity assured. For ongoing poltergeists or hauntings, the society may discuss options and, in some UK cases, visit. It also treats categories such as sleep paralysis separately, describing it as temporary immobility around sleep and waking that can involve shadowy figures, sensed presence, pressure on the chest or out-of-body sensations. This matters because a report of a figure by a bed at night belongs to a different evidential category from a daylight apparition seen by several people in a corridor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/report-an-experience" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paranormal Experience? &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Experience? &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

The older files also show how reports were made comparable. The SPR’s late nineteenth-century Census of Hallucinations asked thousands of people whether, while believing themselves fully awake, they had ever had a vivid impression of seeing, hearing or being touched by something not due to an external physical cause. Cambridge’s catalogue describes the British survey as involving 17,000 people. That was not a haunting survey in the modern “haunted house” sense, but it shaped later file-making by treating apparitions as reportable experiences that could be counted, compared and checked rather than simply celebrated or mocked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/630399" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk">[ArchiveSearch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">archival objects</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">archival objects</span></span></span>

A good haunting file therefore tries to preserve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The event:** what was seen, heard, felt or moved.
* **The setting:** room, building, light, weather, time, access and history.
* **The witnesses:** number, relationship, prior belief, stress, sleep, expectation and opportunity for collusion.
* **The pattern:** whether the event repeated, changed, clustered around one person, or followed publicity.
* **The record trail:** letters, interviews, photographs, recordings, plans, newspaper cuttings and later critiques.

</div>

The value is not that every file proves a ghost. The value is that a later reader can distinguish a rumour from a documented chain of claims.

## Natural explanations to check

Psychical research societies sit in an awkward position. They receive reports from people who may feel frightened, bereaved, mocked or overwhelmed, but they also inherit a duty not to turn every anomaly into a spirit. The best files therefore keep possible normal explanations close to the witness material rather than treating them as an afterthought.

Modern haunting research has shown why. A British Journal of Psychology study by Richard Wiseman, Caroline Watt, Paul Stevens, Emma Greening and Ciarán O’Keeffe investigated Hampton Court Palace and the [South Bridge Vaults]({{ 'vaults/' | relative_url }}) in Edinburgh, both known for haunting reputations. Participants walked through the sites and reported unusual experiences. Reports clustered in areas already reputed to be haunted, and those locations also correlated with environmental factors such as lighting levels and local magnetic-field variation. The authors concluded that alleged hauntings may partly reflect people responding to normal features of their surroundings rather than ghostly activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/an-investigation-into-alleged-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.ed.ac.uk">[Research at Edinburgh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An investigation into alleged &#x27;hauntings&#x27; - University of Edinburgh Research Explorer...</span></span></span>

That does not mean every witness is foolish or dishonest. It means a responsible file needs to ask ordinary questions before extraordinary ones. Old buildings have draughts, settling timbers, pipes, animals, poor lighting, odd acoustics, vibration, mould, reflective surfaces and unreliable wiring. People also bring fatigue, grief, expectation, suggestibility, fear, sleep disruption and group reinforcement into the experience. A record that omits these factors may preserve a story, but it does not preserve an investigation.

Poltergeist files raise an additional problem: trickery and social dynamics. The SPR’s own index includes early cases in which cheating or suspected cheating was part of the record. The Psi Encyclopedia overview of poltergeists notes that some observers attribute all such activity to fraud, especially because children and young people are often described as the focus, while other investigators argue that discovered trickery does not necessarily account for every reported incident. This is precisely why the file matters: it can hold both admissions of deception and claims that remain disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spr.ac.uk/9-hauntings-and-poltergeists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spr.ac.uk">[spr.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9. Hauntings and Poltergeists &#124; spr.ac.uk</span></span></span>

The natural checks most relevant to haunting files include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Misidentification:** shadows, reflections, animals, passers-by, sleep states or ordinary noises interpreted under stress.
* **Building effects:** draughts, loose fittings, subsidence, plumbing, heating systems, electrical faults and acoustic quirks.
* **Environmental cues:** lighting, magnetic variation, vibration, air quality, temperature and spatial layout.
* **Social cues:** reputation, local legend, media coverage, group expectation and leading questions.
* **Human agency:** pranks, fraud, exaggeration, memory drift, attention-seeking or attempts to protect a cherished story.

</div>

A well-kept file does not have to solve every case. It has to show what was checked, what was not checked, and where the uncertainty remains.

## When files become disputes

The most famous haunting files are often famous because they are contested. Borley Rectory is the clearest example. Harry Price’s investigations helped turn the Essex rectory into “the most haunted house in England”, and his library and archive remain major resources for studying psychical research, conjuring and exposure of illusion. But Borley also demonstrates the danger of a charismatic investigator becoming part of the phenomenon being investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.london.ac.uk/about/services/senate-house-library/collections/printed-special-collections/harry-price-library-magical-literature" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: london.ac.uk">[University of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">london.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on london.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

After Price’s death, SPR-linked investigators Eric Dingwall, Kathleen Goldney and Trevor Hall re-examined the Borley material and published a strongly critical report. Later summaries of the case note allegations that some phenomena were faked or better explained by rats, acoustics and other ordinary causes. The point for this page is not to retry Borley, but to show how haunting governance works when a file is reopened. Preservation allowed later critics to compare claims, testimony, site conditions and investigator behaviour rather than relying only on Price’s public narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borley_Rectory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Borley Rectory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Borley Rectory</span></span></span>

The Enfield poltergeist shows a different kind of dispute. The SPR archive guide says it holds the complete Grosse and Playfair collection of correspondence, cuttings and articles. Public accounts of the case describe divisions among investigators: Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair treated the case as significant, while sceptical critics and some psychical researchers were unconvinced and pointed to possible faking by the children. A file that keeps recordings, correspondence and press material together helps future readers see how family distress, media attention, investigator belief and sceptical critique interacted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/archive-society-psychical-research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

These disputed cases reveal three governance lessons. First, investigators can become advocates, and advocacy can shape the file. Secondly, media attention can change a haunting by attracting visitors, journalists, imitators and pressure on witnesses. Thirdly, later re-examination depends on dull archival work: dates, letters, access logs, photographs, floor plans and unglamorous notes.

## Why the story persists

Haunting files persist because they serve more than one public. Believers look for continuity, patterns and possible evidence of survival after death. Sceptics look for fraud, misperception and environmental causes. Historians look at grief, religion, class, media, domestic life, science and popular culture. Archivists look at provenance, preservation and access. Witnesses may simply want their experience taken seriously without being forced into a dramatic conclusion.

This multi-use quality explains why institutions keep even doubtful material. A debunked or partly explained haunting can still be valuable evidence of how people reported anomalous experiences, how investigators framed questions, how newspapers shaped reputation, and how old buildings became story-bearing places. The SPR archive guide explicitly notes that reports of spontaneous phenomena, including hauntings and poltergeists, are likely to remain an important part of new content even as parapsychology has shifted more towards experimental work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/archive-society-psychical-research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk">[Psi Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psi Encyclopedia Society for Psychical Research Archives – Psi Encyclopedia</span></span></span>

The files also persist because they protect ambiguity better than folklore alone. A local legend often asks the reader to choose: believe the ghost or dismiss the tale. A research archive allows a more useful response: compare the accounts, check the setting, examine the investigator’s assumptions, separate first-hand testimony from later embellishment, and identify what evidence would actually change the assessment.

That is the quiet importance of psychical research societies in the history of hauntings. They have not settled the ghost question. Their stronger achievement is procedural: they created places where unusual experiences could be reported, classified, preserved, disputed and revisited. In a subject crowded with certainty, performance and fear, the file is valuable because it slows the story down.
