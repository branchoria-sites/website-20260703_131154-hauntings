## Introduction

One of the most revealing features of many electronic voice phenomenon (EVP) recordings is that the “message” often becomes clear only after someone tells listeners what they are supposed to hear. A faint burst of noise may sound meaningless at first, yet once a caption, transcript or suggested phrase is supplied, many people report hearing the exact words. This effect is not unique to ghost hunting. It reflects how human speech perception works when sound is weak, noisy or incomplete. Research on auditory [pareidolia]({{ 'pareidolia/' | relative_url }})—the tendency to perceive meaningful speech in ambiguous sounds—shows that expectations can strongly influence what listeners hear, particularly when recordings contain little clear linguistic information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2michaelanees.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — These shows commonly featu...</span></span></span>

Within haunting investigations, this [mechanism]({{ 'mechanism/' | relative_url }}) matters because EVP clips are usually brief, low-quality and heavily dependent on interpretation. The question is often not whether a sound exists, but why a particular sequence of words emerges from it.

## How Suggested Phrases Shape What Listeners Hear

Human hearing is not a passive recording system. The brain constantly combines incoming sound with expectations, memories and context to decide what a signal means. When speech is degraded by noise, listeners rely more heavily on these top-down expectations to fill in missing information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.914239/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Interaction between predictability, acoustic signal, and noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">teraction between predictability, acoustic signal, and noiseDecember 16, 2022 — by M Van Os · 2022 · Cited by 16 — Models of s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2022</span></span></span>

This creates a powerful effect in EVP listening sessions. Consider a recording containing a vague hiss or crackle. If listeners receive no guidance, they may disagree about whether it contains speech at all. If they are told beforehand that the sound says “Get out”, many will begin hearing that phrase because the suggested wording gives the brain a template against which to match the ambiguous audio.

Experimental research has demonstrated this directly. Psychologist Michael Nees and colleagues compared reactions to EVP clips, degraded speech and other ambiguous sounds. Participants who were primed to think about paranormal voices were significantly more likely to report hearing voices than participants given a neutral explanation. Even when listeners detected a voice, agreement about the actual words was generally low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2michaelanees.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — These shows commonly featu...</span></span></span>

This finding highlights an important distinction:

* **Detecting speech-like sound** is one task.
* **Identifying specific words** is another.

Many EVP debates jump directly to the second step, even when the first remains uncertain.

## Why Captions and Transcripts Feel So Convincing

A common feature of paranormal television programmes, online videos and [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) reports is the appearance of subtitles beneath a noisy recording. Once viewers read the caption, the ambiguous sound often seems dramatically clearer.

The effect occurs because written text supplies a ready-made interpretation. Rather than analysing countless possible speech patterns, the brain tests the suggested phrase against the noise and often finds enough matching fragments to create the impression of a complete sentence. Speech perception research shows that contextual information becomes increasingly influential as the acoustic signal becomes less reliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.914239/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Interaction between predictability, acoustic signal, and noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">teraction between predictability, acoustic signal, and noiseDecember 16, 2022 — by M Van Os · 2022 · Cited by 16 — Models of s...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2022</span></span></span>

This is why two groups can hear radically different messages in the same EVP clip:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* One group receives a suggested transcript.
* Another group listens blind.
* The first group reports a coherent sentence.
* The second group hears unrelated words, different phrases or no speech at all.

</div>

In many famous EVP examples, the caption becomes inseparable from the recording itself. After repeated exposure, listeners may struggle to hear anything except the suggested wording, even if alternative interpretations are equally plausible.

## Why Replay Loops Strengthen Certainty

Another factor is repetition. EVP clips are frequently replayed many times, often in short loops lasting only a second or two.

Repeated listening changes perception in several ways:

**Pattern reinforcement.** Once listeners think they recognise a phrase, each replay reinforces that interpretation.

**Selective attention.** People begin focusing on portions of the noise that support the expected words while ignoring contradictory sounds.

**Perceptual learning.** The brain becomes better at extracting a chosen pattern from a degraded signal after multiple exposures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6877468/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Auditory hallucinations, top-down processing and language...by JN de Boer · 2019 · Cited by 60 — Top-down processes play an important...</span></span></span>

As a result, confidence often increases with repetition even when the underlying recording never becomes objectively clearer. The listener's certainty may grow faster than the evidence itself.

This creates a familiar experience in ghost investigations: a newcomer hears random noise, while experienced members insist that the phrase is obvious. The difference may reflect exposure rather than audio quality.

## Why People Rarely Agree on the Exact Words

One of the strongest challenges to many EVP claims is the low level of independent agreement about message content.

Studies examining EVP perception have repeatedly found that listeners often report different words from the same recording. Even when participants agree that something speech-like is present, consensus about the exact wording can be surprisingly weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryAuditory Pareidolia: Effects of Contextual Priming on...by MA Nees · 2015 · Cited by 49 — These shows commonly featu...</span></span></span>

This pattern is what researchers would expect if the recording contains ambiguous acoustic information rather than a clearly articulated sentence.

In practical terms:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Clear speech recordings usually produce high agreement.
* Ambiguous EVP clips often produce low agreement.
* Suggested transcripts increase agreement by giving everyone the same interpretive framework.

</div>

The phrase therefore may emerge from social guidance as much as from the recording itself.

## Blind Listening Checks That Reduce Priming

Because suggested phrases can influence perception so strongly, some investigators use blind listening methods to test whether an EVP message stands on its own.

Useful approaches include:

1. **Remove captions and transcripts.** Present the audio without any suggested wording.
2. **Use independent listeners.** Choose people unfamiliar with the location, story and claimed message.
3. **Collect written responses first.** Ask listeners to write what they hear before discussing interpretations.
4. **Compare responses statistically.** Look for genuine agreement rather than isolated matches.
5. **Randomise clips.** Mix alleged [EVP recordings]({{ 'evp-recordings/' | relative_url }}) with ordinary ambiguous sounds to reduce [expectation]({{ 'expectation/' | relative_url }}) effects.

If a phrase is genuinely clear, independent listeners should converge on similar wording without prompting. If responses vary widely, the result suggests that expectation may be contributing substantially to the perceived message.

## What Suggested Words Reveal About EVP

The phenomenon of suggested phrases does not require dishonesty, deliberate manipulation or faulty equipment. It arises from ordinary cognitive processes that help people understand speech in difficult listening conditions.
